The atoms in solids are not rigid; instead, they constantly move and interact with one another. These oscillations propagate as lattice waves called phonons. In most cases, these atoms oscillate in linear waves. However, in altermagnets—a class of materials discovered as recently as 2019—chiral phonons occur. In this case, the atoms rotate around their equilibrium positions.\

"The overarching aim of our research is to develop new concepts for electronic components that transmit information not via electric currents but, for example, via spin waves—thereby saving energy," says physicist professor Ingrid Mertig from MLU.\

Coupling spin waves and atomic rotations\
In magnetic materials, spin waves arise from the wavelike propagation of coupled oscillations of the spins, i.e., the magnetic moments of the electrons. Chiral phonons can interact with these spins through their mechanical angular momentum, thereby influencing or even exciting the spin waves. The study investigated chiral phonons using a theoretical model that combines magnetic properties and lattice oscillations.\

Mertig's team identified a particular subtype of these chiral phonons: so-called d-wave phonons. "With this type of phonon, the direction of rotation changes. The atoms can rotate either clockwise or counterclockwise depending on the direction of propagation. We were the first to describe this phenomenon," says Dr. Robin Neumann from MLU, who designed and analyzed the theoretical model together with co-author Hannah Bendin.\

The complex behavior of chiral phonons arises from the selective coupling of the lattice oscillations with the material's spin waves: right-handed spin waves couple only with right-handed chiral phonons and vice versa. However, until now it was not known what brought about this effect and under what conditions it could be utilized.\

Sorting phonons by their rotation\
A key finding of the research is that these oscillations form a d-wave pattern; the atoms change direction four times in the crystal, forming a cloverleaf-like symmetry.\
\
This discovery enables researchers to predict a phonon angular momentum splitter effect. Even a simple change in temperature is enough to sort these phonons according to their direction of rotation. And since the rotation is driven by spin waves, the oscillations can be precisely controlled using external magnetic fields.\
