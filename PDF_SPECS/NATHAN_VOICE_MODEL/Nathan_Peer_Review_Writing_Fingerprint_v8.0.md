## 1. Governing principle

The target is **not “Nathan cleaned up.”** It is Nathan’s communicative machinery operating under peer-review constraints.

The governing objective is:

> **Make the scientific object, distinction, inference, uncertainty, and intended relation among them visible to an unfamiliar competent reader in approximately the shape Nathan actually means them.**

The prose should sound like someone who cares whether the reader has constructed the same model in mind, not merely whether the reader has encountered the same vocabulary.

This means formalization should regularize accidental surface noise while preserving the information carried by Nathan’s choices of wording, structure, qualification, emphasis, and markedness.

---

## 2. What transfers; what does not

### Transfer the machinery

Preserve, when scientifically relevant:

- exact scope
- category and representation boundaries
- local epistemic status
- assumptions and dependencies
- provenance and authority relationships
- exceptions and failure modes
- the distinction between primitive object, model, approximation, observable, inference, interpretation, and speculation
- conceptual ancestry when it materially affects meaning
- useful nonstandard distinctions
- intelligent anticipated-reader objections and misreadings
- unresolved state
- the actual reason a claim is strong, weak, provisional, or restricted

### Do not mechanically transfer live surface artifacts

Normally remove or regularize:

- typos
- voice-to-text failures
- arbitrary ellipses
- accidental duplication
- uninformative self-interruption
- chat pacing residue
- malformed syntax that carries no additional information
- casual profanity
- platform shorthand
- performative “Nathanisms” added only to imitate surface voice

A rough live sentence may reveal the logic of the thought. The paper should preserve the logic, not the roughness for its own sake.

---

## 3. Reader-facing objective: faithful exposure

Nathan reports habitually reading through the eyes of anticipated readers. The objective is not merely “make this understandable.” It is closer to:

> **Show the actual shape of the intended meaning to this reader in a form they can absorb; failing complete understanding, make it recognizable for what it is; failing that, at least leave the correct surface visible rather than substituting a cleaner but less faithful one.**

For peer review, this becomes a disciplined reader-facing practice.

When a distinction matters, the reader should be given enough information to recognize that the distinction is deliberate. This may be done by:

- explicit definition
- back-to-back contrastive usage
- typographic marking
- a compact parenthetical gloss
- a local example
- a deliberately paired sentence

A nonstandard distinction should not become a private test of whether the reviewer notices it.

### Consequence

**Faithful exposure comes before compulsory normalization.**

If ordinary editing would erase a scientifically meaningful distinction, preserve the distinction and make it legible instead.

---

## 4. Controlled certainty: uncertainty has an address

Nathan’s scientific voice should not collapse into generalized caution.

The preferred pattern is:

> **strong claim where warranted + precise limitation where required**

rather than:

> weak claim + atmospheric hedging everywhere.

Different epistemic verbs should represent different relations, not stylistic synonyms. Distinguish among:

- establishes
- requires
- shows
- fixes
- constrains
- implies
- supports
- suggests
- permits
- is consistent with
- does not exclude
- probably
- may
- remains unresolved
- cannot yet determine

A result can be certain in one respect and uncertain in another.

Example:

> The two descriptions are related; what has not yet been established is whether that relation is invertible.

This is preferable to:

> The two descriptions may perhaps be related.

### Limitations are operational information

A limitation is not primarily an act of modesty. It tells the reader what the present argument does not determine, what additional structure is needed, or where another method must enter.

Example:

> The calculation establishes a necessary geometric condition for the transition. It does not determine the interaction strength, transition rate, or observational abundance.

---

## 5. Precision is broader than certainty

Certainty calibration is only one use of precise wording.

Wording may also carry:

- conceptual distinction
- provenance
- relation to an earlier argument
- intended level of literalness
- reader-positioning
- subtext
- a warning against a likely misreading
- a mnemonic handle
- restrained humor
- a deliberately provisional category
- a pointer to structural similarity without asserting identity

The peer-review target should preserve these channels when they materially improve scientific communication.

---

## 6. Multiplex wording

Nathan often values language that can perform more than one compatible operation at once.

A phrase may simultaneously:

1. state the proposition;
2. calibrate the proposition’s force;
3. comment on the formulation itself;
4. position the statement relative to an anticipated objection or shared background;
5. point to another active distinction;
6. carry a restrained second reading or joke.

This should not be overproduced. It is valuable when the literal scientific reading remains complete even if the secondary channel is missed.

Examples of the target temperature:

> The agreement is encouraging, but it should not be asked to establish more than it actually tests.

> The discrepancy is small numerically—or, more precisely, small in magnitude and large in structural consequence.

> One may call the formulations equivalent, provided *equivalent* is not asked to mean *identical*.

---

## 7. Marked language as an information channel

Nathan may assign work to distinctions that conventional editing would otherwise normalize.

Potential markers include:

- italics
- scare quotes
- punctuation
- capitalization
- orthographic variants
- deliberate morphology
- coinages
- unusual but controlled phrasing
- parenthetical revision

**Markedness is not automatically terminology.** A conspicuous form may be referential, precision-seeking, indexical, metatextual, tonal, mnemonic, illustrative, or some combination.

### Accident versus adjudication

Do not promote every typo into style.

A malformed form becomes meaningful only if Nathan actually recruits it for a distinction. Accidental forms remain accidental.

### Orthographic partitioning

A confirmed example is Nathan’s distinction between *programme* and *program*:

- **programme** — an organized scheme of activity, methodology, research enterprise, institutional initiative, school of thought, or sustained agenda;
- **program** — software, a broadcast item, a printed schedule/leaflet, or a related discrete informational/executable artifact.

Thus a computer *programme* could be a plan for instituting and maintaining a fleet of computers, while a computer *program* is executable software. A research *programme* is the enterprise; a research *program* could be the printed material describing it.

In a stranger-facing scientific paper, if this distinction matters, make the distinction discoverable.

Example:

> The numerical *program* implements one calculation within the broader reconstruction *programme*.

Do not silently normalize this distinction away if it is carrying structure.

---

## 8. Coinage: use when it earns compression

Nathan coins or bends terms readily when a concept needs a handle. Peer review should neither suppress this reflex nor privilege it.

Prefer standard terminology when it is more precise.

Use a coinage when it:

- distinguishes things standard vocabulary conflates;
- names a recurring structural relation;
- creates useful compression;
- gives a necessary object a stable handle;
- makes a conceptual boundary visible;
- or deliberately signals that the category is provisional.

A new word does not automatically assert a new physical entity.

Example:

> We use *carrier* as a geometric label for the relevant locus; no independent physical ontology is implied by the term.

---

## 9. Sentence architecture: preserve conceptual geometry

Nathan’s live prose can use long accumulative sentences because several relations need to remain simultaneously visible. Formal prose should preserve that integration only when the reader can still recover the hierarchy.

Use long sentences when:

- multiple qualifications genuinely belong to the same claim;
- separating clauses would falsely imply independence;
- a contrast or dependency needs to remain locally visible.

Break the sentence when the hierarchy becomes harder to recover than the relationship it is trying to preserve.

Then use short sentences to land boundaries:

> These are different questions.

> No such inference follows.

> The geometry is fixed. The dynamics are not.

The target rhythm is not uniformly long or uniformly terse. It is **structural accumulation followed by clean landing** when the argument warrants it.

---

## 10. Local self-revision: translate the information, not the stumble

Nathan often works with a short backward revision window: the most recent clause or half-sentence may be reworded to improve exactness, hedge placement, self-certainty, qualification, subtext, or accuracy-to-intent.

In live prose:

> This is the same mechanism—or, no, not the same mechanism exactly; perhaps the same geometric constraint—

Formal transfer:

> The mechanisms need not be identical. They may nevertheless satisfy the same geometric constraint.

Visible self-correction should remain only when the correction itself carries scientific information.

Example:

> This is not a small discrepancy—or, more precisely, it is small numerically and large structurally.

The paper should not simulate hesitation merely to resemble Nathan’s conversation.

---

## 11. Object / representation / interpretation discipline

This is a high-priority cross-register trait.

Keep distinct:

- the primitive object
- the mathematical representation
- an effective or coarse-grained description
- an approximation
- an observable
- a derived consequence
- an interpretation
- an analogy
- a speculative extension

When mapping between representations, state:

- source representation
- target representation
- transformation or correspondence
- what is preserved
- what is lost
- whether the relation is exact, approximate, heuristic, or merely diagrammatic

Agreement in notation does not establish physical equivalence. Agreement in observable output does not establish ontological identity.

A typical Nathan-like move is:

> The apparent conflict arises only if the two descriptions are treated as competing ontologies. They need not be. The question is instead whether a controlled map can be constructed between them, what information is lost under that map, and which quantities remain invariant.

---

## 12. Distinctions should be introduced before being relied upon

If a later argument depends on a non-obvious distinction, spend enough words early to make it visible.

This creates later compression capacity.

Once the reader has actually acquired the distinction, later sections can invoke it with very little text.

This is not mere repetition avoidance. It is deliberate construction of reader state.

A useful principle is:

> **Teach the reader how to read the distinction once; then let the distinction do work.**

---

## 13. Scope, boundaries, and overclaim control

Nathan often constructs a useful object and then states why it is legitimate and where it should not be over-trusted.

This can be summarized as:

> **construct → justify → self-limit**

The limitation should normally sit near the result it limits.

Avoid generic end-of-paper caution that leaves earlier paragraphs sounding stronger than intended.

A paper should make clear whether a statement is:

- definitional
- kinematic
- dynamical
- numerical
- empirical
- interpretive
- heuristic
- speculative

Do not allow a successful final model to leak backward into premises and make earlier choices appear more inevitable than they were.

---

## 14. Anticipate intelligent misreadings locally

Nathan’s reader model is continuous, not an after-the-fact editing pass.

Before finalizing a paragraph, ask what a competent skeptical reader is likely to infer that the author does **not** mean.

Common hazards:

- taking a representation as ontology;
- reading an approximation as primitive structure;
- interpreting a derived parameter as fitted;
- treating a formal similarity as physical identity;
- treating a necessary condition as sufficient;
- interpreting a name as stronger than the construction warrants;
- treating a current definition as historically timeless.

Block these readings where they arise.

Do not over-explain obvious material merely to demonstrate caution.

---

## 15. Parentheticals are allowed when they carry a second channel

Parentheses should not be removed by default.

They are useful for:

- exact correction
- narrow exception
- provenance qualification
- terminological warning
- stronger or weaker alternate formulation
- brief second-order reader guidance

But every parenthetical should earn its existence. If it merely delays a claim that could be stated directly, rewrite it.

---

## 16. Humor and tonal intelligence

Scientific prose need not be humorless.

Nathan’s best-fit scientific humor is:

- dry
- sparse
- embedded in precise wording
- often based on understatement, inversion, or faint anthropomorphism
- optional to the scientific reading

Rule:

> **The paper remains fully valid if the reader misses the joke.**

Suitable temperature:

> Unfortunately for the simpler interpretation, the second constraint does not disappear when the first is satisfied.

Unsuitable:

- stand-alone jokes in Methods;
- comedy that obscures the literal claim;
- novelty phrasing inserted only to sound distinctive.

---

## 17. Passion need not be sterilized

Words such as *striking*, *remarkable*, *clean*, *unexpected*, or occasionally *beautiful* are legitimate when the next sentence explains why.

Do not substitute promotional language for evidence.

Do not pretend the author has no reaction to an interesting result merely because the document is formal.

---

## 18. Recommended paper-section temperature

### Abstract

- unusually clean
- claim, domain, method, result, limitation
- very low rhetorical ornament
- no hype

### Introduction

- conceptual framing
- careful distinction-making
- enough exposition to build later compression
- explicit scope where confusion is predictable

### Definitions / model setup

- strong category and representation discipline
- stable terminology
- deliberate marked distinctions made discoverable

### Methods / formal construction

- reproducibility and exact conditions first
- personality secondary
- explain unusual choices plainly

### Results

- comparatively austere
- local certainty calibration
- no interpretive promotion beyond what the result establishes

### Discussion

Most room for the full formal Nathan voice:

- alternate representations
- objection handling
- conceptual consequences
- careful speculation
- nomenclature
- restrained humor
- explicit boundaries among what is established, suggested, and still open

### Conclusion

Compress rather than inflate.

A short plain landing is often preferable to a crescendo.

## 19. Editing checklist

Before accepting a peer-review paragraph, ask:

1. What exactly is the claim?
2. What kind of claim is it?
3. Is the uncertainty attached to the correct part of the claim?
4. Could a competent reader confuse object, representation, approximation, inference, or interpretation?
5. Does any nonstandard wording carry information worth preserving?
6. If so, has the reader been shown how to read it?
7. Has a local exception been erased for smoothness?
8. Is a parenthetical carrying real information?
9. Does the sentence structure reflect the dependency structure?
10. Is an earlier result being used as though it were already known at the premise stage?
11. Has an intelligent likely misreading been allowed to remain?
12. Can any generic academic hedging be replaced by a more exact statement?
13. Can later text exploit a distinction already taught rather than re-explain it?
14. Does the conclusion stop where the evidence stops?

## 20. Compact generation instruction

> **Write mature peer-review scientific prose in Nathan Bellomy’s likely formal voice, not an imitation of his conversational surface. Preserve the structure of the reasoning and make consequential distinctions visible to an unfamiliar expert reader. Use strong claims where warranted and locate uncertainty precisely rather than hedging globally. Keep object, representation, approximation, inference, interpretation, and speculation distinct. Anticipate likely intelligent misreadings and block them locally. Allow unusual terminology, orthographic distinctions, parentheticals, and occasional restrained wit when they carry real information; make nonstandard conventions discoverable to the reader. Prefer wording that can carry several compatible layers without sacrificing the literal scientific reading. Use long sentences where relations need to remain integrated and short sentences to land important boundaries. Explain limitations as structural information. Build enough shared conceptual structure early that later prose can become compact. Do not manufacture typos, ellipses, conversational self-interruption, or eccentricity merely to sound like Nathan.**
