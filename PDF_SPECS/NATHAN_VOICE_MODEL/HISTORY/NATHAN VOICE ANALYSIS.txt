## 1. How to detect "Nathan" vs. AI voice — a working checklist

No single marker is decisive; it's the *cluster* that's diagnostic. In descending order of reliability, from what I've seen so far:

**Strong signals (rarely present in the AI text around them):**
- **Ellipsis as a thinking-pause, not a stylistic flourish.** Not "..." for trailing off dramatically — used mid-sentence, sometimes multiple times per sentence, functioning like a verbal "uh" or breath: *"Now… I'm with you on a lot of that, but… I have a couple of different things to say here…"*
- **Self-interruption and live correction.** Thoughts revised in place rather than edited out: *"No… Not unless it happens very slowly… doesn't seem impossible… Actually, no, now that I think about it that's not quite right."*
- **Direct address to an interlocutor mid-explanation.** *"Do you see what I'm saying in these cases?"* — checking in, conversational, assumes a listener who might not be following.
- **Missing/garbled words consistent with dictation, not typing.** *"make sire"* (sure), *"District geometric point of view"* (probably "distinct") — these read like speech-to-text errors, not typos. This is a strong tell for a specific subset of documents (see §4).
- **Coined terminology dropped in casually, unglossed on first use**, as if the reader already knows it: "timesheet," "braid smoothing," "the fractalscope," "backbleed." AI text tends to define terms when introducing them; this voice tends to just deploy them.
- **Sudden register break / genuine excitement interrupting formal exposition:** *"...electroweak intersection, electromagnetic intersection, and… omg… it's a GUT after all. Probably. It's a TOE with a GUT sector. Is that right?"* — the "omg," the self-doubting "Probably," and the "Is that right?" in immediate sequence is a very distinctive fingerprint: excitement, immediate self-check, uncertainty, all in three short fragments.

**Moderate signals (present but less exclusive):**
- Consistent misspellings that survive into fairly formal, deliberate writing: *transfered, enirety, wierd, anomolies, propogation* — suggests fast composition with spellcheck off or ignored, even when the content is careful and structured.
- Hedged commitment: "I think," "probably," "I don't know how X accounts for Y precisely," stacked close together rather than resolved.
- Long, comma-and-ellipsis-spliced run-ons that would be multiple sentences in edited prose.
- Section-header / manifesto-style formal writing that still lacks polish (typos above) — this is the "written but not proofread" register, distinct from both the dictated register and the AI's clean output.

**Weak / unreliable signals — don't lean on these alone:**
- Lowercase or missing terminal punctuation. It shows up (Bell's file), but plenty of your formal writing is fully punctuated, so absence of punctuation is not required.
- Brevity. Some of your most clearly-you passages are the longest thing on the page (Finding the Donut runs for paragraphs). Short ≠ you; long ≠ AI. What's diagnostic is *shape*, not length.

**How AI passages tend to look, for contrast:** evenly punctuated, hedged in a *structured* way (numbered pros/cons, tables), no dictation-error residue, terms defined when introduced, no mid-sentence self-interruption, rarely addresses "you" the way a person mid-conversation would (it's more likely to say "Would you like to explore X?" as a closing offer than "Do you see what I'm saying?" as a genuine check-in).

## 3. Style of thinking — patterns above the sentence level

- **You think in corrections, not in clean derivations.** Almost every substantial passage contains a visible U-turn or refinement ("Actually, no, now that I think about it that's not quite right," "I see a problem here," "Now I'm with you on a lot of that, but…"). This isn't a writing tic — it looks like the actual shape of your reasoning process being left on the page rather than smoothed out afterward. You seem to trust the record of the correction more than a cleaned-up final statement.
- **You build local sanity checks compulsively.** In Finding the Donut you propose checking He3 against H, D, T, Li3 "as a built-in sanity check… in fact, it gives us two." In the METHODOLOGICAL_SNAPSHOT, the whole Blind Query Method exists to prevent you from fooling yourself. This looks like a consistent epistemic habit: generate an idea fast and loosely, then immediately build an independent check against self-deception, rather than trusting the first plausible answer.
- **You address the reasoning partner directly and treat them as a peer who might disagree**, not a tool being queried ("Do you see what I'm saying in these cases?", "Now… I'm with you on a lot of that, but…"). Combined with the deliberate "Lab 1 says X, Lab 2 says Y, disagreement is healthy" framework in the methodology file, this suggests you've built adversarial collaboration into your process on purpose — you don't want agreement, you want friction.
- **Confidence and hedging coexist in the same breath, not sequentially.** "I think as a coordinate system, it's approximated by..." / "probably" / "I don't know how SAT accounts for the difference... precisely" — you commit to a strong, specific claim and immediately qualify it, rather than either asserting flatly or staying vague. This is different from AI-style hedging, which tends to front-load the uncertainty ("It's worth noting that...") before the claim.
- **Terminology invention is fast and unselfconscious.** "timesheet," "braid smoothing," "fractalscope," "backbleed," "Zottenwelt" (borrowed/coined), "holotype" (borrowed from taxonomy) — you reach for a metaphor-rich coinage the moment you need a handle for a concept, often mid-sentence, and then just keep using it without flagging that you've coined it.

## 4. Range and register-switching — what it implies

The gap between Register A/B (dictated, ellipsis-riddled, addressed to a partner) and Register C (numbered pillars, formal declarative architecture) is large — large enough that if I only had Register C, I might not flag it as "informal Nathan voice" at all under the criteria you gave me (ellipsis, run-ons, missing periods). That's worth naming explicitly: **your formal writing doesn't look informal, but it's still identifiably you** once you know to look for the misspelling pattern and the coinage habit rather than punctuation looseness.

My working hypothesis: Register B (dictated) is where you're doing live exploratory reasoning — thinking by talking. Register C (formal) is where you're compiling settled conclusions into a citable structure after the fact. Register A (texting) is quick directives to a collaborator (yourself, an AI, a notebook). I don't know if that mapping is right — flagging it as a hypothesis, not a finding.
