Paired but out of step: Why electron pairing alone is not enough for room-temperature superconductivity
Story by Science X staff • 2w • 3 min read

Levitating trains, ultra-efficient energy storage, and quantum computers: superconductivity could unlock a wealth of possibilities for the future. New research led by scientists from Utrecht, Leiden, and Munich has now shed light on one of the key requirements for achieving superconductivity at room temperature. Their findings, published in Science, bring scientists a step closer to understanding how this might be achieved.

When you turn on a toaster, electricity flows through a metal wire. The wire heats up until it glows red-hot, toasting your bread. This happens because the wire resists the flow of electric current, converting electrical energy into heat.

That is exactly what you want in a toaster. But for most other applications, resistance is undesirable. For example, when you want to charge your phone, resistance causes valuable energy to be lost.

Superconductivity offers a way around this problem. It is a physical phenomenon in which certain materials conduct electricity without any resistance. The catch? Most superconductors only work at extremely low temperatures. For many materials, this means cooling them to around -260°C using liquid helium. This is an energy-intensive and expensive process.

Early discoveries
Scientists first discovered superconductivity in 1911. In the mid-1980s, they made another breakthrough: the discovery of materials that become superconducting at considerably higher temperatures, although these temperatures were still around -173°C.

There was a problem, however. The theory that explains conventional, low-temperature superconductivity could not account for this new type of superconductivity. And scientists still do not understand how it works.

"We don't understand superconductivity at these higher temperatures at all," says Ingmar Swart, one of the research leaders. "And if you don't understand it, you can't systematically search for materials that become superconducting at room temperature, which is ultimately our goal."

New potential route
The new research has helped Swart and his colleagues unravel part of this mystery. Their findings provide new insight into how high-temperature superconductivity works and offer a potential route toward superconductivity at room temperature.

"This is really good news," says Swart. "We show that one of the two key conditions for superconductivity can be met at room temperature. If that weren't possible, room-temperature superconductivity would be out of reach altogether."

Dancing pairs
The two key ingredients for superconductivity are the formation of electron pairs, known as Cooper pairs, and the ability of these pairs to move synchronously. Imagine 10 couples on a dance floor: it is not enough for each couple to dance together; they must also move in step with one another.

In conventional, low-temperature superconductors, electron pairing and this collective coordination occur together. The new research shows that this is not the case in high-temperature superconductors. In these materials, electrons can already form pairs at temperatures well above the point at which superconductivity sets in.

"The 'glue' that holds the pairs together is strong enough to keep them together even at room temperature," says Swart. "This suggests that the second key ingredient, phase coherence, is the bottleneck."

Swart is referring to the synchronous movement of the pairs, also known as phase coherence. "If we understand phase coherence, it may point to a path toward superconductivity at higher temperatures." Swart plans to focus on this in follow-up research.

More information: Jiasen Niu et al., Equivalence of pseudogap and pairing energy in a cuprate high-temperature superconductor, Science (2026). DOI: 10.1126/science.adt3444

Exclusive: FAA probe zeroes in on culprit of nationwide ground stop, but agency still faces tough questions
Story by René Marsh, CNN • 3h • 4 min read

Investigators looking into what led to a nationwide ground stop earlier this month have been able to recreate the mysterious technical failure in a lab and believe they found the culprit, according to a source familiar with the investigation.

Investigators have focused on determining why a new router at a remote Arkansas radio site overwhelmed the nation’s air traffic control system with a massive amount of data, triggering gaps in radar and radio communications.

They found an overlooked router setting affected how it interfaced with other decades-old routers that the FAA is decommissioning, the source said. Investigators believe software updates are needed and that those patches will prevent a repeat of the system failure.

The investigators are preparing a report on their findings. There are still some unanswered questions, but the source familiar with the probe said investigators are “90% sure” the disruption wasn’t caused by nefarious actors. The investigation also involved the FBI and other national security representatives.

The findings come as FAA administrator Bryan Bedford and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy are set to appear at hearings next week on Capitol Hill, where they are expected to face tough questions about the investigation and what happens next as they race to modernize the nation’s air traffic control system.

CNN has reached out to the FAA and L3Harris, a contractor that owns and operates the telecommunications system, for comment.

This month’s ground stop not only focused attention on upgrades being made to the aging system, which have been paused as the agency investigated the incident, but also raised questions about FAA oversight of private companies charged with maintaining the network.

Quantum blueprint maps a practical path to testing unusual entanglement
Story by Science X staff • 5h • 4 min read

Adrian Del Maestro knows how to engineer a quantum fingerprint. Rick Mukherjee has a gift for tuning atoms. Their combined expertise has taken a theory sketched out on paper and created a pathway for real experiments. The results, published in the journal Physical Review Letters, move a law-defying "mathematical oddity" ever so slightly up the technology readiness scale

A mathematical oddity
Quantum physics is not the familiar physics where you drop a ball from a balcony and it hits the ground below. There's a different play on the quantum stage, where objects abandon the rules of classical physics and the plot is unpredictable. Del Maestro and Mukherjee want to edit the script and help shape where the story goes. Ultimately, they'd like to take advantage of quantum phenomena and lay the groundwork for future technologies.

Del Maestro is a professor and the head of UT Knoxville's Department of Physics and Astronomy. One of his interests is quantum entanglement, where subatomic particles are so intertwined that it's impossible to think of them as separate entities, even when they're far apart. Mukherjee is an associate professor of physics at UT Chattanooga and director of the UTC Quantum Center. His research is in atomic, molecular and optical (AMO) physics, exploring the quantum behavior of atoms and molecules to understand how light and matter interact.

"He says that I'm his condensed matter friend, so I guess I would call him my AMO friend," Del Maestro joked.

Motzkin spin chains are the most recent link between these physics friends. Named for mathematician Theodore Motzkin, they're a theoretical concept that includes two important elements of quantum computing: quantum entanglement and superposition (multiple possible states at one time). They also ignore the law.

As Stephen Hawking discovered, even black holes have to respect certain restrictions in nature. Area laws dictate that the entropy of a black hole grows proportionally to its surface area, not its volume. Motzkin spin chains don't work that way.

"Their entanglement grows much faster than the area law that most physical systems obey," Mukherjee explained, "but each site carries more than two quantum states, which makes them very hard to simulate on classical computers and not obvious to realize in the lab."

Del Maestro's group has long been fascinated with this model but kept hitting roadblocks in pinning it down.

"We were really obsessed with the structure of these states," he said. "We'd studied (them) before and were always trying to get some traction, but it was just a mathematical oddity. I was telling Rick about this, and he said, 'I think we can make this in Rydberg atoms.'"

Atomic knobs and quantum fingerprints
Del Maestro described Rydberg atoms (named for physicist Johannes Rydberg) as "basically just big puffy atoms, excited into these very high-energy states."

He said that in this scenario, if you have two atoms close to each other, "they can strongly repel. Then as soon as they're maybe one step further away, it's like they don't even know about each other at all. Local constraints are important if you want to engineer custom physics, so you need lots of knobs."

Rydberg systems have plenty of those, and they can be moved around with lasers and light patterns and optical tweezers.

"You can literally grab them with a laser beam and drag them into certain places," Del Maestro explained. "So Rick said, 'Maybe we can figure out how to tune all these knobs.'"

Del Maestro's group tracked how that tuning got them closer or farther from a structure that showed entanglement indicating a Motzkin state.

"We learned how to engineer that fingerprint," he said. "Our knowledge of the fingerprint combined with Rick's knowledge of the knobs is what allowed us to come up with this protocol."

Mukherjee said "the natural next step is to see these states realized experimentally, and we are keen to work with Rydberg-atom experimental groups to make that happen."

He added that the collaboration across condensed matter and AMO physics brought the research to this stage.

"Quantum science doesn't respect the traditional boundaries of physics," he explained. "The questions condensed matter physicists have asked for decades, about entanglement, exotic phases and how many particles behave collectively, are now questions we can test directly by building quantum systems atom by atom.

"Adrian and (research assistant professor) Hatem Barghathi brought expertise in entanglement and in how to diagnose these unusual states," he continued. "My group, led by our postdoc Kaustav Mukherjee, worked out how to encode effective Motzkin spins in Rydberg atoms using parameters experiments can actually reach. The result is a practical route to creating and studying these states in the lab, rather than just on paper. Neither group could have done that alone."

Del Maestro said that in this case, "the ability to move up the technological readiness scale, going from zero to maybe one, is something that required curiosity-driven knowledge with experimental expertise. Those are connections that hopefully will lead somewhere interesting."

Does meme accurately compare ages of actors when they were cast as James Bond?
Story by Nur Ibrahim • 1d • 4 min read


File photo showing the wax statues of actors Roger Moore (left to right), Timothy Dalton, Daniel Craig, Sean Connery, George Lazenby and Pierce Brosnan in their roles as James Bond in Madame Tussaud's in Berlin, Germany. Britta Pedersen/picture alliance, accessed via Getty Images

Claim:

A meme accurately states the age of each of the actors who played James Bond when their casting was announced.

Rating:

Mostly True (About this rating?)

In November, Robert Pattinson was announced as the seventh actor hired to play the titular British assassin and spy, James Bond. Pattinson, who is 40 years old, is older than some of the other actors who have played the role before.

A popular meme on Instagram captured all the previous Bond actors and the purported ages they were hired to play Bond:

Sean Connery: 31, cast in 1961 (Dr. No)

George Lazenby: 29, cast in 1968 (On Her Majesty's Secret Service)

Roger Moore: 44, cast in 1972 (Live and Let Die)

Timothy Dalton: 42, cast in 1986 (The Living Daylights)

Pierce Brosnan: 41, cast in 1994 (GoldenEye)

Daniel Craig: 37, cast in 2005 (Casino Royale)

Robert Pattinson: 40, cast in 2026 (Bond 26)


Does meme accurately compare ages of actors when they were cast as James Bond?
Does meme accurately compare ages of actors when they were cast as James Bond?
(Instagram user "thefilmthusiasts")

The meme is largely accurate, notwithstanding conflicting accounts about Dalton's exact age when he got the part. Below, we break down the ages of each actor cast to play Bond.

Sean Connery, according to "James Bond, Dr. No" a book about the making of the first Bond film, was cast in 1961 and the movie came out in 1962. Connery, who was born in 1930, was 31 years old at the time of his casting.

George Lazenby, an Australian actor, played Bond only once, in the 1969 film "On Her Majesty's Secret Service." Lazenby was born in 1939. In 1968, newspapers announced he was cast as the new Bond. He would have been 29 years old at the time, and 30 years old when the movie was released.

Roger Moore was born on Oct. 14, 1927. His first Bond film was the 1973 film "Live and Let Die." Moore was announced as the new Bond at the Dorchester Hotel in London on Aug. 2, 1972. He was 44 years old, just a few months shy of turning 45. 

Expand article logo  Continue reading

Timothy Dalton first played the role in 1987's "The Living Daylights" and was announced as the next Bond on Aug. 7, 1986, according to Reuters. If we go by the date of birth given on his IMDb page, March 21, 1946, Dalton was 40 years old when cast. If we go by the age stated in the Reuters announcement, he was 38 years old when cast and 39 when the film was released. In an interview on the official website for the Bond films, 007.com, Dalton said he was first approached for the role (which he turned down at that time) in 1968, when he was only "about 25 years old," which would make his year of birth 1943, while IMDb lists it as 1946. Dalton, who was much older when he gave the interview to 007.com, may have misremembered at what age he was first approached.
