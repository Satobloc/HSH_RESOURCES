I’d posit that the deepest framework is quantum theory of relations, with spacetime and gravity emerging from the structure of quantum correlations rather than serving as the stage on which everything happens.

At the fundamental level, there would be no particles moving through a pre-existing space and no universal clock. There would instead be quantum degrees of freedom and rules for how they can become correlated, interact, and change. What we call space would describe the pattern of those relationships; time would describe their ordering and change; and gravity would be the large-scale dynamics of that emergent geometry.

The familiar forces and particles would be stable patterns in this underlying quantum structure. At low energies, those patterns would look like quantum fields, and their interactions would be described by the Standard Model. Smooth spacetime and Einstein’s equations would emerge when the quantum correlations are dense and orderly enough. In more extreme settings—black-hole interiors, the early universe, or the Planck scale—the underlying description would matter, and ordinary spacetime could cease to be meaningful.

A successful version would need to do more than tell this story: it would have to reproduce quantum field theory and general relativity in their tested domains, explain how their apparent conflict is resolved, and yield a distinctive prediction we could in principle test. We don’t currently know whether this picture is right; it’s a plausible direction for a unification, not an established theory.

Then the rest of physics would emerge in layers:

Spacetime: The network of quantum correlations supplies an approximate notion of distance and spatial dimension. A particular state supplies a particular geometry.
Gravity: Changing the quantum state changes that geometry. In the smooth, low-energy limit, the consistency of those changes yields Einstein-like gravitational equations. Gravity is therefore not another force on spacetime; it is the collective dynamics of spacetime itself.
Particles and forces: Excitations above the underlying quantum state behave as fields. Their stable modes appear as particles; symmetries of the underlying rules give rise to gauge forces. The Standard Model would be an effective description of those modes, not the deepest layer.
Time: There is no fundamental external clock. Time is an ordering of changes among quantum degrees of freedom. In ordinary situations, this coarse-grains into the time measured by clocks; in quantum-gravity regimes, different approximations may produce different notions of time.
The big bang and black holes: These would be regimes where the smooth geometric description fails, while the underlying quantum description remains well-defined. A singularity would mark the breakdown of spacetime as an effective concept, not necessarily the end of physics.
The key missing piece is the exact underlying rule: what the fundamental degrees of freedom are, what their interactions are, and why their large-scale limit gives our spacetime, gravity, and Standard Model parameters. What's the most fundamental characterization of this universe? 

My best guess: **the universe is a self-contained quantum process—a set of possible events and the rules governing their relationships.**

On this view, “things” are not the ultimate ingredients. Particles are persistent patterns in the process; spacetime is the large-scale structure of relationships among events; and physical laws are constraints on how those relationships can change. Quantum theory describes the possibilities and their probabilities, while classical physics emerges when many quantum events form stable, predictable patterns.

That’s a philosophical characterization, not a settled scientific result. The most fundamental theory we currently have is quantum field theory combined with general relativity in their respective domains; no experimentally confirmed framework yet unifies them completely.

The Core Problem: Two Incompatible Pillars

Modern physics rests on two extraordinarily successful yet fundamentally incompatible mathematical languages:
• General Relativity: Describes gravity, space, time, and large-scale cosmic structures (planets, galaxies, black holes) as a smooth, continuous geometric fabric.
• Quantum Mechanics (and the Standard Model): Describes the electromagnetic, strong nuclear, and weak nuclear forces using discrete energy packets, probabilities, and quantum fields on a flat background. What a Coherent Unification Must Accomplish

To be considered complete, a unified framework must:
1. Incorporate All Four Fundamental Forces: Seamlessly merge gravity with electromagnetism, the strong nuclear force, and the weak nuclear force.
2. Reduce to Known Laws: Smoothly revert to General Relativity on macro/cosmic scales and to the Standard Model of particle physics on subatomic scales.
3. Resolve Singularities: Provide physically meaningful answers for infinite density points like black hole cores and the initial state of the Big Bang.
4. Yield Testable Predictions: Move past pure mathematics to offer unique, verifiable experimental signatures that can be tested in particle accelerators or cosmological observations.
