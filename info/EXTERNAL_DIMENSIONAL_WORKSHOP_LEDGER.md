### Especially promising immediate leads

1. **Baez & Otter, “Operads and Phylogenetic Trees,” Theory and Applications of Categories 32 (2017), 1397–1453.** This is a direct mathematics-to-evolutionary-biology bridge. The first geometric task is to visualize a phylogenetic tree as a compositional object and show how operadic composition corresponds to joining/grafting tree structures. Do not reduce this to the bare fact that a tree is a graph; investigate the extra compositional structure.

2. **Kanari et al., “Quantifying Topological Invariants of Neuronal Morphologies,” arXiv:1603.08432 (2016).** Direct geometry/topology-to-neuroscience bridge. Visualize neuronal branching geometry, scale, and the topological invariants being measured. Determine which invariants preserve biologically relevant shape information and which discard metric detail.

3. **Category theory for genetics (Tuyeras).** The nLab collection identifies DNA segment categories and pedigrads for genome data, sequence alignment, mutations, genotype, phenotype and haplotype. This is an especially promising bridge among geometry, evolution, computation and biological organization.

4. **Hyperstructures in biology (Baas).** The archived nLab text contrasts hyperstructures with n-categories, noting that biological processes may be organized as aggregates with bonds rather than simply as oriented globular arrows. This deserves a careful geometric comparison with higher-dimensional categories, not a quick dismissal as another abstract formalism.

5. **Higher-dimensional category models of process calculi.** Stay and Meredith's work on higher-dimensional category models of the π-calculus may bridge computation and biological process modeling. The core visualization question is what a higher-dimensional morphism represents between process histories, and what geometric dimension that object has in the model.

### Particularly important geometric observation: hyperstructures versus higher-dimensional categories

The nLab biology page presents a useful contrast. It says hyperstructures permit biological organization that is not necessarily oriented as globular arrows; instead, biological systems may organize through aggregates and bonds.

Its illustrative sequence is:

`cells → selected group of liver cells → liver → organ systems → body`.

Each aggregate is a subset of lower-level agents together with a label/description that identifies the aggregate. The operation repeats at higher organizational levels, with compatibility conditions among the labels.

This is a particularly useful geometrical comparison for our project:
- higher-dimensional categories organize objects and transformations in dimensionally stratified, often globular forms;
- hyperstructures model iterated aggregation and bonding, potentially capturing organization that is not naturally represented as a simple directed sequence of globular cells.

We should visualize both and compare their incidence structures, not assume one is a synonym or replacement for the other. This may also connect to the user's earlier point about lower-dimensional structures being components of a higher-dimensional whole, though the exact mathematical relation must be specified.

## 29. Hinton: handedness, four-dimensional rotation, and modern controls — 2026-11-24

- **Primary text inspected:** C. H. Hinton, The Fourth Dimension, first edition 1904; repository text is the May 1906 second edition, Chapters III and VII (HISTORICAL/HINTON — 4TH DIMENSION.txt, blob ece2e416). **Primary item:** J. H. Woodger, *Biological Principles: A Critical Study* (first published 1929; scan of the 1948 second impression with 1967 reissue notice), Internet Archive identifier `biologicalprinci0000jhwo`, 538 scanned pages. Item record: https://archive.org/details/biologicalprinci0000jhwo. Full OCR text: https://archive.org/stream/biologicalprinci0000jhwo/biologicalprinci0000jhwo_djvu.txt. The Archive item metadata identifies the 1929 first publication and the scanned edition details. The source read here is the primary book's OCR transcription; OCR can contain errors, so any quotation used in a formal paper should be cross-checked against the corresponding page image.

### Direct primary passages inspected

1. **Book p. 219 — organism as event and temporal/spatial parts.** Woodger analyzes the problem of “the same organism” through a pin-prick example: the organism is changed by the first stimulus, so a repeated stimulus is not applied to exactly the same organism. He then treats the organism as an event extended in space and time, with temporal parts as well as spatial parts. Yesterday's and today's dog are different temporal parts of one continuing dog, just as head and tail are different spatial parts. Identity is grounded in the particular continuity between temporal parts, not their numerical sameness. This is the primary locus quoted by Dupré and Nicholson (2018: Woodger 1929a, p. 219).

2. **Book pp. 299–300 — taking time seriously in biology.** Woodger compares the minimal spatial extension needed for an object to exist with the time required for an organism to manifest its organization. A frog abstracted from its history is no more concrete than a life-history abstracted from the frog. His memorable methodological point is that anatomy studies the organism in “timeless space” and thereby omits the time-dimension. A preserved frog is treated as a cross-section of the living organism's history, not as the complete living organism.

3. **Book pp. 301–302 — the four-dimensional event.** Woodger makes an unusually explicit distinction: assimilating space and time yields the notion of a four-dimensional event, but this does **not** mean time is simply “a fourth dimension of space.” Rather, space and time are both abstractions from a four-dimensional continuum; an event is a limited slab of space-time, and a living organism is such an event. He then analyzes different time-slices of the organism: their character varies with both the interval between slices and their position within the organism's total duration. In metazoa he distinguishes overlapping developmental and behaviour periods. Successive temporal slices differ in serial order; continued serial change is part of what constitutes the organism's persistence, and its cessation marks the cessation of life and the breakdown of organization. This is a direct primary source for temporal organismal extension, not for an additional spatial axis.

4. **Book pp. 303–306 — repetition, division, and biological relations.** Woodger treats development as temporal and spatial differentiation, with irreversible/serial temporal change and increasing elaboration of spatial organization. A pattern can be repeated spatially through division. He distinguishes two outcomes: repeated structures may remain in an organic relation as parts of one whole organism (part-producing division), or become two whole organisms (whole-producing division, such as identical twins). He emphasizes that the first yields a later temporal stage of the same organism now with two spatial parts; the second yields two organisms. He further says that offspring may remain in physiological relation with a parent after separation without remaining in an organic part-whole relation. This is a sharp conceptual distinction between *organic membership* and *physiological relation*.

5. **Book pp. 306–307 — symmetry across space and time.** Woodger treats symmetry as spatial repetition and rhythm as repetition in time, both relevant to biological organization. He distinguishes simultaneous repetition in bilateral/radial symmetry, successive emergence in spiral symmetry, and successive paired repetition in metameric segmentation. A footnote explicitly recommends that symmetry be extended from a four-dimensional point of view, and names D'Arcy Thompson's *Growth and Form*, Jaeger's *Lectures on the Principle of Symmetry*, and Whitehead's work on four-dimensional geometry as relevant reading routes. This is a direct new bridge to biological morphology and mathematical symmetry.

### Why this matters to the private histological proposal

Woodger gives us a historically grounded set of distinctions that are highly useful for clarifying, but do not prove, the user's construction:

- **Temporal persistence:** a persisting organism is a temporally extended event whose different temporal parts are not identical but are related by a continuity rule.
- **Part-whole membership:** not every physiological or causal relation makes something a part of an organism. Woodger distinguishes products that remain organically related parts of one whole from products that are now separate whole organisms but retain physiological relations.
- **Branching and division:** ordinary biological division can increase the number of spatial parts within one temporally extended organism, or produce distinct organisms. These are not interchangeable cases.
- **4D symmetry:** body-plan repetition may be analyzed across time as well as space; Woodger explicitly suggests extending symmetry from a four-dimensional point of view.
- **Histological organization:** Woodger discusses how tissue territories and cellular relations can elaborate beyond properties of individual cells. This may be a useful conventional-biology comparator for the user's insistence on histological relations rather than reducing the model to individual filaments or genetic inheritance.

**Key diagnostic question for our model:** when the user's proposed 4D tendrils connect apparently separate people across time, is that relation intended as (a) ordinary genealogy/physiological relation between distinct organisms, (b) part-producing spatial branching within one larger temporally extended organism, or (c) a third kind of intertemporal organic relation not captured by Woodger's conventional developmental/reproductive categories? Arthur immediately qualifies that these are strictly two distinct timescales, not literally two independent time dimensions; he nevertheless finds the “five dimensions” language useful for exposing analyses that ignore one of the temporal scales.

4. **Scott F. An accessible author-uploaded copy/record is indexed at https://www.academia.edu/102758256/Fate_Maps_Gene_Expression_Maps_and_the_Evidentiary_Structure_of_Evolutionary_Developmental_Biology. Arthur's article is an accessible, critically engaged route to this passage.

### Conceptual distinctions to preserve

This research now reveals at least four meanings of dimensionality that can coexist in biological writing without making the same claim:

- **Ontological spatial dimensionality:** an organism or tissue is proposed to extend into an additional spatial direction. This remains the stronger higher-space reading associated with Hinton and the user's own distinct histological hypothesis.
- **Spatiotemporal event dimensionality:** an ordinary organism occupies a four-dimensional space-time continuum (three spatial dimensions plus time); Woodger explicitly frames the organism as a limited event/slab and rejects the careless phrase that time is merely a fourth spatial dimension.
- **Morphological representation dimensionality:** a biological form, projection, grid, map or diagram has a certain number of representational dimensions. A 2D grid transformation of adult body outlines does not contain all dimensions necessary to represent real ontogeny or phylogeny as physical causal processes.
- **Multi-timescale evo-devo dimensionality:** a model must represent both within-lifetime development and across-generation evolutionary change. The “five-dimensional” vocabulary can denote three spatial dimensions plus two temporal *scales*. Arthur explicitly cautions against treating the two temporal scales as two independent physical time dimensions.

### Relevance to the user's histological model

The Woodger–Thompson–Arthur sequence is useful for clarifying what a biologically meaningful 4D representation must contain. Woodger supplies the temporally extended organism and 4D symmetry problem; Thompson supplies mathematical morphology, geometric transformations, and organismal shape comparison; Arthur exposes the danger of treating a static shape transformation as a complete causal account when development and evolution occur on different timescales.

For the user's proposal, this suggests a methodological check: separate the geometry of an individual 4D organismal tendril from (a) its ordinary spatial morphology at a given temporal slice, (b) the developmental history of that tendril, and (c) evolutionary changes in the organization of such tendrils across generations. A model that projects all three into one undifferentiated “time” coordinate risks conflating distinct processes. Conversely, adding an extra spatial dimension must be a separately justified geometric claim, not an inference from the fact that biology needs temporal and morphological coordinates.
