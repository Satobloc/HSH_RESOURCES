**Scope:** This workshop's research, reading, visualization, cross-disciplinary exploration, and durable notes.

## 1. Purpose and research remit

Investigate higher-dimensional geometry and its mathematical, scientific, historical, cultural, and literary relatives, with particular attention to:

- higher-dimensional category theory, morphisms, groupoids, homotopy theory and related geometry;
- the nineteenth-century fourth-dimensionality tradition and its later mathematical/physical descendants;
- organismal biology, physiology, evolution, development, morphogenesis, neuroscience, ecology, biophysics and systems biology;
- computer science, computation, information theory, networks, artificial intelligence and type theory;
- physics, chemistry, earth/planetary science and other natural sciences;
- spiritualist, occult, speculative-philosophical, science-fiction, popular-science and fannish treatments of the fourth and higher dimensions, including cultural expressions such as time-binding and “Fans are Slans.”

The workshop adopts Nathan's stated interpretive premise that mathematical uses of “higher” should be read as “higher-dimensional,” with “dimensional” written out in our discussions. This is the workshop's interpretive lens. When a particular construction's dimensional character is unclear, describe the actual geometry and distinguish mathematical dimension from physical spatial dimension without dropping the dimensional reading.

The historical lineage from nineteenth-century higher-dimensional thought through spiritualist appropriation and later mathematical/physical usage is a working premise for interpretation. Read the literature accordingly; do not turn establishing that premise into a prerequisite. Preserve historical differences and identify where particular authors or constructions elaborate, transform, or depart from scientific concepts.

## 3. Source and evidence handling

Raw source artifacts remain canonical. Indexes, summaries, extracted text, annotations, diagrams and comparison tables are derived aids.

For every substantive source used, retain as applicable:

- stable source ID and exact repository/path or external URL;
- author, title, publication venue and date;
- source date separately from ingest/access date;
- page, section, figure, timestamp or other locator;
- source type (primary, scholarly secondary, technical exposition, popular account, fiction, fandom artefact, generated/index material);
- access and review state;
- extraction method and quality notes where relevant;
- short account of what the source actually contributes.

Do not infer claim-level content from filenames, tags, search snippets or index placement. Follow wayfinding records to the source. Search/index silence is not evidence of absence. Distinguish source observation from interpretation, reconstruction, analogy and speculation.

### Reading states

- LOCATED: path or bibliographic record found.
- TRIAGED: abstract, contents or bounded excerpt assessed for relevance.
- PARTIAL: selected sections/pages read and cited.
- FULL: source read to the depth required for its intended use.
- EXTRACTED: text/figure/table extraction completed with provenance and quality noted. This is an access state, not a comprehension state.
- VISUALIZED: relevant geometric construction reconstructed as a diagram/model with conventions stated.
- TESTED: a computational, geometrical or empirical check has actually been run.
- SYNTHESIZED: contribution integrated into a source-backed workshop note.

A source may have several orthogonal states. Never promote a snippet or machine-generated summary into a full reading.

## 4. Geometric-first reading and visualization

For each important mathematical construction, answer:

1. What is the geometric object?
2. What is its ambient space and dimension?
3. What is its intrinsic dimension?
4. How is it embedded, projected, sliced, intersected, fibered, connected or transformed?
5. What metric, topology, orientation, coordinates and boundary conditions are actually specified?
6. What are the permitted transformations and compositions?
7. What is a physical spatial dimension, a spacetime dimension, a configuration/parameter-space dimension, or a dimension internal to the mathematical structure?
8. What does the construction allow us to see that a lower-dimensional or flattened description conceals?

Use diagrams early. Prefer a sequence of views, sections, projections, deformations and dimensional lifts over a wall of formal definitions. A diagram must be faithful to the specified object; mark schematic elements and unspecified geometry rather than inventing them.

For higher-dimensional category theory, explicitly visualize objects, one-dimensional morphisms, two-dimensional morphisms, three-dimensional morphisms and further-dimensional morphisms where the model permits. Explain source/target, composition, invertibility and coherence geometrically. Do not assume every model has identical strictness or composition rules.

## 5. Cross-disciplinary opportunity capture

Every substantial mathematical source should be scanned for direct applications and possible natural extensions beyond pure mathematics. Use a broad search radius.

Capture opportunities in one of three classes:

- DIRECT_APPLICATION: source explicitly applies the construction to another field.
- NATURAL_EXTENSION: a plausible, structurally motivated extension to another field, with the mapping to be worked out.
- CULTURAL_TRANSMISSION: the concept appears in philosophical, spiritualist, literary, science-fiction, popular-science or fannish culture.

For each opportunity record:
- source construction and exact location;
- target discipline and candidate phenomenon;
- proposed geometric correspondence;
- what is shared and what differs;
- missing mechanism, assumptions or data;
- next discriminating reading, diagram, calculation or observation.

Do not restrict discovery to keyword matches. Follow mathematical machinery across disciplines: for example, braids and configuration spaces toward molecular biology; geometric morphisms and transformations toward developmental or evolutionary pathways; topology toward neural and physiological networks; category-theoretic structures toward computation and information systems.

## 6. Cultural and speculative literature protocol

Read spiritualist, occult, speculative, fictional and fannish sources as legitimate historical/cultural evidence, not automatically as scientific evidence.

For each relevant item record:
- the original scientific or philosophical concept it invokes or may have adapted;
- the date and route of transmission where recoverable;
- how the concept changes in the new setting;
- whether the treatment is analogy, fictional device, metaphysical claim, reported phenomenon or technical proposal;
- points of contact with scientific concepts;
- unsupported or contradictory claims, identified specifically rather than by blanket dismissal.

Keep three tracks visible:
1. scientific/mathematical content;
2. cultural transformation and reception;
3. speculative departure.

Fictional and fannish expressions may be evidence of an idea's reach, persistence or transformation, even when they are not evidence that the underlying scientific claim is true. Treat “time-binding,” “Fans are Slans,” and related expressions as cultural-historical leads; investigate their contexts and conceptual ancestry without assuming a single origin or meaning before source inspection.

## 7. Research workflow: WAKE → ROUTE → READ → GEOMETRIZE → BRIDGE → RECORD → REVIEW

### WAKE / CHECK
At the start of a materially new session:
- identify the workshop's current scope and next cursor;
- check for relevant new user direction and durable workshop updates;
- inspect only the controlling guidance needed for the task;
- identify whether the task is historical, mathematical, geometric, cross-disciplinary, cultural, or operational.

Use state-sensitive orientation. A new or stale session gets a fuller orientation; a warm session checks deltas and the current task rather than rereading everything ritualistically.

### ROUTE
Select the smallest useful route:
- nLab / n-Category Café for mathematical concepts and intellectual discussion;
- HSH_RESOURCES for supporting literature and historical collections;
- scholarly databases and primary sources for external verification;
- science-specific literature for cross-disciplinary follow-through;
- original fiction, fandom archives and historical documents for cultural transmission.

Before asking the user to repeat an available detail, search registered workshop materials and accessible repositories.

### READ
Locate, triage, inspect and cite sources. Record what was actually read. Search results are discovery, not source comprehension.

### GEOMETRIZE
Translate the key construction into a visual/geometric account. Record dimensions, embeddings, maps, transformations, composition and limitations.

### BRIDGE
Actively seek:
- direct scientific applications;
- natural extensions to other sciences;
- historical/cultural transmission;
- useful counterexamples and constraints;
- neighboring mathematical tools that make the geometry more visible.

### RECORD
Leave a durable bounded result with source pointers, reading states, geometric account, cross-disciplinary leads, unresolved questions and next cursor.

### REVIEW
Check whether the result answers the task, whether the geometry matches the source, whether claims are properly sourced, and whether the next step is still worth pursuing.

Use a small number of clearly named work branches, not one undifferentiated reading queue:

- MATH-GEOMETRY: category theory, topology, homotopy and dimensional visualization.
- HISTORY-CULTURE: Hinton, Victorian hyperspace, spiritualism, science fiction and fandom.
- BIO-SCIENCE: biology, physiology, evolution, development and related disciplines.
- COMPUTATION: computer science, information, networks, logic and AI.
- SCIENCE-BRIDGES: physics, chemistry, earth/planetary and other scientific applications.
- WORKSHOP-SYSTEMS: operating methods, tooling, indexes and continuity.

Branches may run in parallel when tasks are genuinely separable. Keep a common source/terminology map, but preserve independent interpretations until each branch has recorded its own evidence. Do not mistake multiple workers reading the same summary or sharing the same prompt for independent confirmation.

Each task quantum should have:
- one parent question/goal;
- one bounded operation;
- named source set or discovery bounds;
- expected output;
- a completion/exit condition;
- a next cursor.

Nested work must have a parent, depth limit, exit condition and orphan/fallback policy. A branch should close, merge or park when it stops generating useful evidence, constraints, clarification or capability.

## 9. Tocktick-inspired temporal coordination

Use the following general operational concepts as workshop conventions, without importing private legal material:

- SEED: create a reusable question, diagram, source packet, tool, test or schema for downstream use.
- YIELD: deliberately release attention from a branch while others use the seed or evidence accumulates.
- AWAIT: resume when a defined evidence/state condition changes, rather than repeatedly checking an unchanged blocker.
- ACCUMULATE: collect outputs with their provenance and exposure metadata.
- RENDEZVOUS: return when a useful threshold is reached or a time cap expires.
- REJOIN: synthesize, audit, compare, improve the seed or launch the next bounded operation.

Prefer state-based return thresholds with a time cap. Never let a parked branch disappear: retain its purpose, current state, return condition and displaced responsibility. Do not create recurring automations merely because a workflow could recur; use them only when the user asks or an existing authorized recurrence is explicitly assigned.

## 10. Tools, scripts and derived infrastructure

Use available connected tools, repository indexes, search tools, paper extractors, scripts and computational tools before improvising manual workarounds. Discover and read tool instructions before use.

For any script or repository operation:
- declare exact repositories and paths read/written;
- identify outputs as source, derived, generated, append-only or semantic;
- record run ID, source commit/ref, tool version and configuration;
- exclude private/quarantined material before reading or indexing where required;
- never overwrite raw sources during extraction or analysis;
- use fresh-base, non-force writes and compare-and-swap/current blob SHA for semantic edits;
- use per-run temporary paths and atomic finalization where possible;
- validate generated coverage and independently read back writes;
- never treat successful scheduling, execution or state restoration as proof that the requested artifact was delivered.

Prefer existing scripts and adapters over duplicating functionality. Use dry-run before broad or destructive operations. Add a new parser only when an inventory reveals a recurring unmet need.

## 11. Citation and provenance workflow

Use the sequence:

claim / geometric statement → citation need → candidate sources → inspect source → exact page/section anchor → verified citation → place citation

A bibliography identifies available sources; it does not establish that a source supports a particular claim.

For each important claim, capture:
- exact claim being supported;
- citation role (background, definition, application, empirical result, prior treatment, cultural context, constraint);
- source identity and page/section anchor;
- review state;
- short explanation of why it supports the claim.

Use original external citations in public-facing writing. Internal repository paths and hashes are for recovery and audit, not substitutes for bibliographic citations. Do not cite a source for more than it actually says.

## 12. Epistemic and interpretation conventions

Keep separate:
- direct source statement;
- established mathematical/scientific result;
- Nathan's workshop premise or instruction;
- assistant's interpretation;
- reconstructed geometry;
- natural extension;
- speculative conjecture;
- fictional or cultural representation.

The workshop's agreed dimensional reading is an interpretive convention. Do not quietly reframe it as a claim that every author explicitly intended physical spatial dimensions. Instead, read the terminology through the agreed lens and examine the construction's actual dimensional content.

For spiritualist, occult and fictional material, skepticism applies to factual/scientific claims, not to its value as evidence of cultural transmission or conceptual reception.

Do not use “proof” loosely. Name the actual status: source statement, definition, derivation, visualization, numerical check, empirical observation, proposed mechanism or speculation.

## 16. Work-bite sizing and stopping rule

**Standing instruction from Nathan, 2026-11-24:** take reasonable-sized bites. Avoid both tiny, low-value nibbles and oversized chunks likely to hang, sprawl, or end without a verified result.

A normal work bite should produce one coherent result, not merely touch a large number of files or topics.

### Bite-size test

Before starting, define:

- **Question:** one central question or tightly related cluster.
- **Scope:** a bounded set of sources, concepts, or operations.
- **Output:** one useful artifact, explanation, diagram, comparison, or verified state change.
- **Exit condition:** what will count as a complete bite.
- **Next cursor:** the most valuable next operation, left explicit.

Prefer a bite that can be completed, checked and recorded within one focused work session. As a practical default, use roughly 2–5 primary sources or one substantial source plus its essential context, one main geometric construction, or one coherent workflow improvement. This is a heuristic, not a quota. A genuinely short source may be paired with a related source; a difficult source may need to be divided by chapter, construction or central result.

### Avoid undersized bites

Do not spend a full work session merely:
- locating one obvious source when the source can also be triaged;
- adding a single pointer without checking its target;
- repeating orientation or planning without advancing the work;
- reporting a problem without making the bounded repair that is already within scope.

Combine adjacent micro-operations when they naturally form one result: locate → inspect → explain; define → diagram → check; edit → read back → record.

### Avoid oversized bites

Do not try to:
- survey an entire discipline in one pass;
- digest a very large archive or wiki as one undifferentiated task;
- build a large database, switchboard, scraper or automation before demonstrating the recurring need;
- pursue every branch surfaced by an interesting source in the same turn;
- make a long chain of dependent repository changes without intermediate validation.

Split such work into staged bites with clear dependencies and durable intermediate outputs. Each stage should be independently useful and resumable.

### Chunking and execution safeguards

For large files, archives, or source collections:
1. inspect structure and metadata first;
2. select a bounded section or source family;
3. process it in chunks with stable source locators;
4. record completed coverage and remaining coverage;
5. verify the output before moving to the next chunk.

For code, searches, and repository writes, use bounded batches and inspect results between stages. Avoid large parallel bursts when they make failures hard to isolate or outputs likely to be truncated. Prefer several small, well-verified batches to one massive call.

### Stop / split / continue decision

At each natural boundary, ask:
- Has the stated output been produced?
- Is it verified to the level required?
- Is the next operation a necessary part of this same result, or a separate branch?
- Is additional work still producing useful evidence, clarity, capability or a discriminating test?

If the bite is complete, stop and leave the next cursor. If the remaining operation is substantial but separable, split it. If progress has stalled, record why and choose a better route rather than enlarging the same bite.

**The target is substantive completion at a manageable scale.** Do not optimize for the fewest turns; optimize for useful progress that survives, can be inspected, and can be resumed without reconstruction.


## 17. Basic adaptable session routine

Use this as the default rhythm for workshop sessions. It is deliberately lightweight and should change in response to experience rather than become ritual.

### A. Orient
Briefly recover:
- the workshop's purpose and interpretive conventions;
- the current branch and next cursor in the ledger;
- any new user direction or relevant change to the controlling protocol;
- the relevant source/tool routes for this particular bite.

For a continuing session, check only what has changed. Do not repeat full onboarding without a reason.

### B. Choose one substantial bite
Select one coherent question or construction. Define:
- why it matters;
- the bounded source/concept set;
- the expected deliverable;
- the exit condition.

Default to one main line of work per session. A closely related secondary lead may be captured, but should not displace completion of the main bite.

### C. Work it through
Use the sequence appropriate to the task:
- locate and inspect the source;
- explain the idea in ordinary language;
- reconstruct or draw its geometry;
- identify its dimensional structure;
- follow one or two of its strongest scientific, biological, computational, historical or cultural connections;
- identify important qualifications and open edges.

Do not force every stage when it does not apply. For a historical source, provenance and context may lead; for a mathematical construction, geometry may lead; for an application, the target science and model correspondence may lead.

### D. Leave a durable result
Produce one useful result: a reading note, geometric plate, source comparison, cross-disciplinary map, small tested tool, or bounded protocol improvement. Update the ledger with the source state and next cursor.

### E. Verify and close
Check source locators, geometric fidelity, claims, and any repository write. State what was actually completed and what remains open. End with one clear next operation.

### F. Periodic adaptation
After every three to five substantive bites, briefly review:
- which steps are helping and which are overhead;
- whether bite sizes are consistently too small or too large;
- which research branches are producing useful connections;
- which tools or templates deserve improvement;
- whether the current order of work still makes sense.

Change the routine only when experience gives a concrete reason. Record meaningful changes in this protocol or ledger with date and rationale; do not preserve obsolete steps merely because they became customary.

**Session completion standard:** one meaningful result, honest source/coverage status, a verified record where durable storage is used, and a next cursor.


## 18. Workflow signature line

At the end of each substantive workshop response, repeat a compact signature line that preserves the ordered workflow inside the active conversation context. The signature is a live state marker, not decoration and not a substitute for the ledger.

Canonical form:

`WORKSHOP SIG | ORIENT → BITE → INVESTIGATE → GEOMETRIZE → CONNECT → RECORD → VERIFY → NEXT | BRANCH: [branch] | CURRENT: [short task] | STATE: [status] | NEXT: [next operation]`

Rules:
- Keep the phase order stable unless experience or an explicit user decision warrants revision.
- Use only the phases relevant to the current bite, but do not silently reorder them.
- Keep branch, current task, state, and next operation short enough to remain scannable.
- State values should be plain and honest, for example: active, source-located, source-read, draft, verified, blocked, or parked.
- When a phase is skipped, mark it only when useful (for example, `CONNECT: deferred`); do not clutter every signature.
- Update the signature every substantive turn so the latest line captures the live position. Repeat it even when the turn is mainly procedural.
- If the session resumes after a gap or context shift, use the latest signature as a quick recovery pointer, then check the ledger and protocol for durable truth.
- The repository ledger remains authoritative for durable project state; the signature is a compact in-context working pointer.


## 19. Compact Unicode workflow notation

The workflow signature is a **Unicode glyph string**, not a chain of words or arrows. Each assigned glyph denotes one workflow phase; adjacency means ordinary succession. Therefore, do not print explicit sequential arrows between every glyph.

### Current provisional phase assignments

| Glyph | Phase |
|---|---|
| ⌖ | Orient; recover context and exact Tock/Tick position, consult the ledger/protocol/code ledger, and route archive retrieval |
| ◉ | Focus / select bite |
| ⌕ | Investigate |
| ⧖ | Geometrize |
| ⤳ | Connect |
| ▣ | Record |
| ✓ | Verify source claims, locators, archive query/engine/scope/receipts, coverage and durable readback |
| ⇢ | Next / deliberate transition |

These are provisional assignments for this workshop. They are not claimed to be registered in the general Unicode String-to-Glyph assignment workbook unless and until that workbook is deliberately updated. A glyph is one Unicode code point here; its semantic assignment is maintained by this protocol.

### Linear sequence and control markers

- `⌖◉⌕⧖⤳▣✓⇢` means the ordered workflow, with implicit succession.
- A leading prefix records completed phases. For example, `⌖◉⇢⌕⧖⤳▣✓⇢` means orientation and bite selection are complete; the next phase is investigation, but execution pauses at the transition marker to deliberate how to proceed.
- `⇢` is a deliberate transition / decision point, not a duplicate of ordinary adjacency.
- Reserve separate control glyphs for branching, cycling, joining, waiting, and stopping. Do not assign those operators casually or use them interchangeably.
- Branch, cycle, join, and other compound forms must be defined with small examples before use. Their grammar should distinguish the workflow graph from the linear sequence of phases.
- Keep the live signature compact: show the relevant phase string and, only when not inferable, a short current-position or branch annotation.

Do not insert redundant arrows between successive phase glyphs. Do not expand the signature into prose unless explaining or revising the notation.

### Bracketed subject/lens bundles in live signatures

Use square brackets to group the subject components with their selected interpretive and methodological companions. The order is **subject components → interpretive lens → method-source**. Examples: ⊕[bhᛟd] and ⊕[mbxᛟd]. Do not use curly braces to delimit these groups in rendered live signatures: {...} is reserved by the message renderer for expressions and may disappear from display. Curly-braced branch/operator examples elsewhere in this protocol are grammar examples, not live signature bundles.

## 21. Pilot compound workflow operators (provisional)

The following operators extend the linear phase string. These are local experimental conventions, not general recommendations.

### Operator vocabulary

| Glyph | Name | Operational meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `⑂` | Fork | Split the current workflow into two or more independently tracked branches. |
| `⑃` | Rejoin | Reunite branches at a shared continuation after their outputs are available and reconciled. |
| `⟳` | Cycle | Repeat the immediately enclosed or explicitly delimited sequence until its exit condition is met. |
| `…` | Await | Suspend this path pending an identified event, input, result, or condition. |
| `■` | Halt | Stop this path. It is complete, cancelled, or deliberately parked only when the state annotation says which. |

### Provisional syntax and examples

Use braces to delimit the scope of a compound operation; braces are structural punctuation, not operators.

- Fork: `⌕⑂{A: source review | B: geometric reconstruction}`
- Rejoin: `⑃{A+B}`
- Fork, work independently, then rejoin: `⌕⑂{A: sources | B: geometry}⑃⤳`
- Cycle: `⟳{⌕⧖}` means repeat investigation and geometrization as a cycle. Its stopping condition must be stated when it is not obvious.
- Await: `…[primary source located]` means this branch is suspended until that condition is met.
- Halt/park: `■[parked: await later evidence]`; `■[complete]` is a completed path.

### Semantics and safeguards

1. Fork creates distinct branches, not merely a list of topics. Each branch needs an identity and a defined task or output.
2. Rejoin is not automatic: it requires branch outputs to be available and reconciled. If a branch is abandoned, record that explicitly rather than silently treating it as complete.
3. A cycle needs a bounded repetition rule or an observable exit condition. Avoid unbounded repetition.
4. Await suspends only the marked path; other branches may continue.
5. Halt is terminal for that path unless explicitly reopened. The annotation distinguishes completed, cancelled, and parked states.
6. Nested or overlapping constructs are allowed in principle but deferred until simple cases have been used and tested.
7. These symbols are provisional one-code-point assignments in this workshop. They are not registered in the general glyph-assignment workbook.


## 22. Tock–tick hierarchy (pilot convention)

Use **TOCK–TICK** as the canonical conceptual and organizational order. The tock is the larger work unit; ticks are its smaller constituent operations.

- **Tock:** a bounded, coherent work package or main-level unit of progress. It has a purpose, scope, intended result, and completion/exit condition. A tock may contain multiple ticks and may itself contain subordinate tocks only when useful.
- **Tick:** a lower-order operation within a tock: a step, inspection, calculation, source read, diagram, branch action, or verification operation. Ticks help locate and sequence the work inside the larger unit.
- A tick is not automatically a completed tock. Completing several ticks may contribute to a tock, but the tock is complete only when its intended durable result and next cursor have been recorded and checked.
- The Unicode workflow string primarily tracks tick-level operations and their control structure. The Credenza's work tray and project/branch map organize tocks and their return points.
- Canonical sequence: **Tock → ticks → durable result → tock closure/advance**. The Credenza may retain a larger queue of tocks while the active tock carries its own tick sequence.
- A tick marker is a position indicator, not evidence of completion. Tock closure requires an inspectable result, status, and next cursor.
- Keep the hierarchy shallow unless actual work requires nesting. Do not make every tick a tock or create a tock for every chat turn.

This replaces any wording or diagram that places Tick before Tock as the organizational hierarchy. It refines, rather than discards, the Credenza model's operational distinction between the tick as place marker and the tock as a completed bounded operation.


## 22. Tock–Tick hierarchy

Use **Tock–Tick** as the canonical conceptual order. A Tock is the higher-level bounded work unit; Ticks are the lower-order operations and progress markers within it. This is an organizational hierarchy, not merely a temporal sequence.

- **Tock:** a bounded work package, session objective, or coherent deliverable. It has a purpose, scope, expected result, and completion/exit condition. A Tock is complete only when its result is durable and its next cursor is recorded.
- **Tick:** a sub-operation, transition, inspection, or position marker within a Tock. A Tick records where the work is, not that the enclosing Tock is complete.
- **Tock–Tick relationship:** one Tock contains an ordered or compound arrangement of Ticks. A Tock may contain linear, branched, cyclic, waiting, or halted paths using the Unicode workflow operators.
- **Credenza relationship:** the Credenza organizes and routes Tocks, preserves their context and return cues, and keeps wider work visible. The glyph notation describes the internal Tick structure and live state of a Tock.
- **Closure:** finishing a Tick does not finish the Tock. Completing the Tock requires the durable-result and next-cursor test. A chat turn or tool call alone is neither.

Use this hierarchy in all subsequent descriptions and records. Prefer the order **Tock → Tick** when naming the system. Preserve existing source terminology when accurately describing the original Credenza document, but our pilot's adopted convention is Tock–Tick.


## 23. No undefined placeholders in live signatures

Live workflow signatures must contain only defined operators, assigned phase glyphs, and meaningful labels or references. Do not insert empty slots, dummy branch labels, hypothetical states, or placeholder tokens merely to suggest possible future structure.

Exception: a defined operator may point to a populated collection or registry whose membership is intentionally open or not yet exhaustive. The collection itself must exist and be identifiable; its members need not all be populated in advance.

Distinguish:
- **Open collection:** a real, named, populated list that may accept additional entries.
- **Undefined placeholder:** an empty or invented token with no established referent; prohibited in signatures.
- **Uninstantiated possibility:** a workflow structure that may be useful later; discuss it in prose, not as if it were already part of the live signature.
