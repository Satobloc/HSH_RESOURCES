Yes. The cleanest way to map 2024 physics is to use two different maps, because “mainstream,” “professional,” “public-facing,” and “orthodox” are not the same axis.

The first map is the whole social/intellectual ecosystem, from institutional physics to the genuine fringe. The second zooms in on people who are actually inside or adjacent to professional physics and asks how far from consensus they are willing to work. A string theorist at IAS can be much more speculative than an optical engineer at a national lab while being vastly more institutionally mainstream; a science communicator can sound conservative in public while doing much more adventurous research privately.

One important town rule: “minority program” is not “fringe.” Perimeter, for example, explicitly treats loop quantum gravity, spin foams, group field theory, causal dynamical triangulations, asymptotic safety, causal sets, and background-independent string approaches as legitimate quantum-gravity research programs, while acknowledging that no approach has yet clearly won.

That gives a useful counterintuitive result:

Witten is institutionally more mainstream than many people doing much less exotic-sounding physics. String theory’s claims about extra dimensions, strings, duality and emergent spacetime sound wildly speculative to a layperson, but the program sits deep inside elite theoretical physics. IAS still describes quantum field theory, string theory, quantum gravity and their geometric relationships as ordinary components of its theoretical-physics program.

Conversely, MOND sounds modest—change one force law at low acceleration—but sociologically it sits farther from the consensus cosmological model than holography does from mainstream high-energy theory. McGaugh nevertheless chairs an astronomy department and works directly on galaxy dynamics and the mass-discrepancy problem.

And ’t Hooft is the great spectrum-breaker. If an unknown independent researcher said “quantum mechanics may emerge from an underlying deterministic system,” many physicists would immediately suspect crank territory. When Gerard ’t Hooft says it, it is a minority foundational research program pursued by somebody whose earlier work helped establish the quantum consistency of the Standard Model. The proposition does not become true because of his CV; the CV tells you that “heterodox claim” and “fringe scientist” are not synonyms.

Where the famous public faces actually live

This is where the map gets amusing.

Sean Carroll is not principally “a science communicator who knows physics.” He is an academic foundational theorist working on quantum mechanics, spacetime, cosmology, emergence and entropy who also happens to have a large public platform. On the insider map he is around mainstream-frontier/foundations territory; his public persona is much closer to consensus exposition than some of the questions he researches.

Sabine Hossenfelder is almost the reverse public archetype: a trained foundations physicist with a substantial publication record whose public niche is aggressive criticism of hype, fashionable methodology, and weak evidentiary claims. Her own site describes more than 80 research papers in cosmology, quantum foundations and particle physics and now identifies her prominently as physicist/author/content creator. On the public spectrum she can sound much farther from the establishment than her actual technical provenance warrants.

Brian Greene is a bona fide string theorist whose public work occupies clean science communication and popular science. His research identity is closer to Witten/Maldacena territory than most viewers of The Elegant Universe probably realize.

Brian Cox came out of experimental particle physics and is largely a mainstream-consensus public interpreter. His public profile sits closer to “tour guide to the downtown” than “founder of a heterodox district.”

Neil deGrasse Tyson is a legitimate astrophysicist and institutional science communicator, but he is not a useful marker for the cutting edge of fundamental-theory factional politics. His public role is much more “explain the scientific consensus and scientific worldview.”

Katie Mack and Becky Smethurst are useful examples of current-research-trained communicators: their public material largely gives outsiders a pretty honest view of mainstream cosmology/astrophysics without pretending the field is more settled than it is.

Michio Kaku is the interesting boundary case. His professional ancestry is real theoretical physics/string-field work, while his media persona ranges much further into futurism and highly extrapolative material. If you only see the public persona, you would place him much farther toward “buzz-space” than his academic provenance alone would suggest.

Roger Penrose barely fits the usual communicator category at all. He is one of the bedrock figures of mathematical relativity who also occupies some of the field’s most eccentric neighborhoods. This makes him an excellent demonstration that the spectrum is about claims, not people.

Some towny knowledge that matters

There are several unwritten rules an outsider can easily miss.

First, physicists tolerate astonishing speculative freedom downstream of competence. You can talk about emergent spacetime, extra dimensions, multiverses, deterministic subquantum dynamics, cyclic cosmology, causal sets or modified gravity and remain completely respectable if you know the existing theory cold, formulate the departure precisely, engage the literature, and accept constraints.

Second, the real taboo is usually not “weird idea.” It is not knowing what has already been ruled out. A strange proposal with a careful EFT limit and a constraint table gets a hearing. A modest-sounding proposal that violates Lorentz tests, conservation law, known scattering data, or dimensional consistency and does not notice will rapidly get sorted into a different social category.

Third, credentials buy attention, not truth. Penrose can get a room for a wild idea because everyone knows he can do the conventional calculation. The wild idea still gets attacked. A Nobel Prize allows ’t Hooft to make determinism a serious seminar topic; it does not make determinism consensus.

Fourth, the theoretical community is substantially less monolithic than “mainstream physics” sounds. Even inside quantum gravity, an institution like Perimeter deliberately houses LQG, spin foams, group field theory, matrix models, CDT, asymptotic safety, causal sets and string-related approaches simultaneously. The insider argument is often not orthodoxy versus heresy. It is which kind of heresy has earned enough mathematical and empirical discipline to become a research program.

Fifth, there is a big gulf between “not consensus” and “nobody is working on this.” MOND is not the cosmological consensus. It is nevertheless a real research literature. LQG is not the dominant quantum-gravity program. It is nevertheless a professional research community. Deterministic quantum mechanics is not the standard interpretation. A Nobel laureate nevertheless publishes on it.

Sixth, the farthest acceptable insider position is not fixed. A technique can migrate. Twistors were once seen by many theorists as an elegant curiosity; Witten’s use of them in scattering theory helped move them much closer to the mainstream. Yesterday’s weird mathematics can become tomorrow’s standard machinery without the underlying grand theory that motivated it ever winning.

And that, I think, is the most useful way to picture the town:

The center is not “people with conventional ideas.” The center is people embedded in powerful correction mechanisms.

The further outward you go, the less the decisive issue becomes how strange is the proposition? and the more it becomes how much independent constraint, technical competence, reproducibility, literature contact, adversarial feedback, and institutional cross-checking surrounds it?

That distinction is going to matter a great deal when we eventually ask where SAT sits on this map, because on the content axis several SAT ideas have analogues in perfectly respectable minority neighborhoods, while on the social-validation axis SAT is presently an independent research program with essentially none of the institutional error-correction that protects even very heterodox insider physics. Those are two completely different measurements.
