Unlike electric charge, as far as we know all mass is positive, and positive masses attract one another. Could negative mass exist, and if so, what would be the ramifications?

Antigravity between a positive mass and a hypothetical negative mass has received a fair bit of attention in both physics and science fiction over the years. For example, in 1901's "First Men in the Moon" author H. G. Wells imagines a substance he calls "cavorite" which creates a negative force of gravity and thus acts as a gravity shield. In Newton's theory of gravity, negative mass would effectively appear as his same equation but with the gravitational constant G replaced by -G. But Einstein's version of gravity, general relativity, is not so kind, and does not seem to consistently allow anti-gravity.

In a new paper in Physics of the Dark Universe, Shin'ichi Nojiri from Japan and S.D. Odintsovc from Spain dig deeper into the possibility of negative mass objects (NMOs) and conclude that the idea may not be as exotic as is thought. Using theoretical tools, they show that negative mass "does not always lead to any inconsistency."

Where negative mass could arise
Mass comes from a particle's interaction with the Higgs field, and most of a particle's mass is actually binding energy (remember Einstein: m=E/c2) between its constituents. Protons, 1,836 times more massive than electrons, are composed of three quarks and gluons bound together. The quark masses are only about 9% of the proton's mass, according to lattice gauge theories of quantum chromodynamics. The rest comes from the field energy of gluons that mediate the dynamics inside the proton.

The authors begin by noting that a bubble in water effectively acts as a negative mass: "The bubble behaves as if it has a negative mass because the water around the bubble falls [away] due to gravity." They then consider a world with a positive point mass (such as a compact star) in a "fluid" with negative pressure, such as dark energy, with a negative cosmological constant. (In reality, the cosmological constant—proportional to the vacuum energy of the universe—is positive but very tiny.) The fluid alters this from a simple positive-negative mass system.

They argue that due to the negative pressure, the positive point mass pushes away the fluid. Therefore, stable regions of negative energy density would exist. A simple version of Einstein's equations of gravity, just one step beyond Newton's Universal Law of Gravity, finds that the NMO will impart a repulsive force to the positive mass object. Einstein's equivalence principle shows that the NMO also has negative inertial mass, the mass that appears in F=ma.

Negative mass in simple cosmological models
From this they solve a few simple cosmological models. In one they use a simplified version of Einstein's equations of general relativity to find instances of NMOs where the cosmological constant is negative. In another, they assume the fluid pressure is proportional to the energy density, and for values of the proportionality constant that are less than -2/3 an NMO could appear.

Space is not asymptotically flat in this simple manufactured scenario. Our own universe is also not asymptotically flat—there is matter and radiation everywhere and not a vacuum at the largest distances—and it is expanding due to dark energy.

Modified gravity offers another route
The authors also examine so-called "modified gravity" with conditions inspired by string theory. Although Einstein's general relativity has passed all experimental and observational tests to date, it doesn't explain, for example, dark matter or dark energy, or offer simple explanations for cosmic acceleration and galactic dynamics.

Thus the authors consider Einstein's theory but with two additional simple ["scalar"] fields added. Such fields might be one that propels early cosmological inflation, or fields that supply dark matter or dark energy, or a novel neutron star or black hole structure. In special circumstances such models contain NMOs created by evolution of the simple fields.

The resulting spacetime acts as gravitational convex lenses: a beam of light travels hyperbolically around the NMO due to a repulsive force, unlike general relativity's bending of star light towards the massive object. The inertial mass is negative, so there is an attractive force between a positive mass object and an NMO. Two pure NMOs repel one another.

Extreme densities and possible clues
But could negative mass objects ever be observed? Not easily. In one of the author's calculations, the density of a negative mass region or object must be greater than about 1018 kg/m3, about 200 trillion times Earth's average density! For the models mentioned earlier, the mass of the NMO depends on the parameters that define the theory.

But what might be observable is an NMO that is moving towards a positive mass object and thus screens its gravity, or a bound system of a positive mass object and NMOs. "Such a system might also be observed due to the rather strange lensing effect," the authors write. The jet of a black hole could be modified by NMOs, as would neutron star-black hole collisions, with the NMO altering the general relativistic results that have been confirmed to date.
