Why does matter exist in the first place?
©blackdovfx/Getty Images
Scientists have been trying to understand why the universe exists at all. When the universe was very young, they believe it created two types of particles. Texas city planned to build data center. Then residents sued
Story by Laerke Christensen • 1mo • 9 min read


Attendees wearing t-shirt's reading 
Attendees wearing t-shirt's reading

The claim that a Texas city paid a family farm $10 in exchange for land meant to be used for a park and then sold the land to a data center developer for $10 million is still under investigation. 
Deeds from Williamson County show that four members of the Cromwell family sold a plot of land to the Texas Parks and Recreation Foundation for $10 "to be held in the trust for future use as parkland" in July 1999. The land changed hands four more times until NCP Travis TPP Project LCC, a data center developer, bought it from the Taylor Economic Development Corp. for $10 million in 2025.
Blueprint Data Centers, whose parent company is reportedly NCP Travis TPP Project LCC, got approval in 2024 from the city of Taylor to build a 135,000-square-foot data center on the land that the Cromwell family originally sold for parkland.
The requirement that the land should be parkland featured only in the Cromwells' deed, not subsequent ones. At the time of this writing, an appeals court in Texas was considering a group of Taylor residents' lawsuit to stop the construction of the data center based on the parkland clause.
Another appeals court was considering a request from campaigners to force the city of Taylor to let the public vote to change its zoning laws and prohibit data centers in existing zones.
Snopes will update this report if additional information surfaces. We have contacted Blueprint Data Centers to ask about the status of the project in Taylor and are waiting for responses.
In August 2026, a claim circulated online that a Texas city paid a family farm $10 in exchange for land meant to be used for a park and later sold the land to a data center developer for $10 million.

James Webb just confirmed a crack in our understanding of the universe
The James Webb Space Telescope was expected to help settle one of the biggest disputes in modern cosmology, but its measurements instead reinforced the disagreement. Early-universe calculations imply an expansion rate of about 67 km/s per megaparsec, while measurements using nearby stars and supernovae point to roughly 73 km/s per megaparsec - a gap of around 8-9%. Webb’s sharper observations confirmed the Cepheid measurements that some researchers suspected were being distorted by observational errors. The standard cosmological model remains highly successful, but scientists are now debating whether the tension points to measurement differences or physics that has not yet been identified.

Have we discovered only half of physics? The hidden supersymmetry
In this video, I explain supersymmetry (SUSY), its significance, and why it remains undiscovered. Supersymmetry posits a new layer of symmetry connecting matter particles and force-carrying particles through their spins. This theory proposes potential solutions to critical issues in physics, including: 1. Addressing the hierarchy problem of the Higgs boson’s low mass. 2. Unifying three fundamental forces: electromagnetism, strong force, and weak force. 3. Providing a candidate for dark matter. The concept of symmetry in physics suggests that changes should not alter interactions, encompassing charge, parity, and time symmetries (CPT). Supersymmetry introduces counterparts for each fermion and boson, contributing to the universe's balance. Despite its theoretical foundation, supersymmetric particles have not been detected, likely due to their significant mass or weak interaction properties, making them elusive in current experimental setups. #supersymmetry

The Bible’s most mysterious king and he may not have been human
Nimrod appears in the Bible for just four verses, yet his legend exploded into one of the most dramatic stories in ancient tradition. Described as the first mighty warrior and founder of powerful cities like Babylon and Nineveh, he quickly became a symbol of rebellion and empire. Later Jewish and Christian writers transformed him into a giant descended from the Nephilim, the mastermind behind the Tower of Babel, and even a tyrant who tried to burn Abraham alive. Some scholars believe he may echo the legendary king Sargon of Akkad, while others argue he was originally a Mesopotamian god reimagined as a human ruler. His name itself may mean “we will rebel,” fueling centuries of interpretation that painted him as an enemy of God. Over time, Nimrod was even linked to fire worship, sorcery, and the figure of Zoroaster. Whether hero, villain, or mythic symbol of empire, Nimrod remains one of the Bible’s most mysterious and controversial kings.

Astronomers caught a supernova at the moment its explosion began
Story by Dylan Moore • 10h
Astronomers Caught a Supernova at the Moment Its Explosion Began
©Photo by NASA Hubble Space Telescope on Unsplash
Astronomers achieved a remarkable observation in 2025 when the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope captured a massive star's explosion at an extremely early stage. The event, designated SN 2024ggi, was detected on April 10, 2024, and follow-up observations began only about 26 hours later. Researchers were able to examine the shape of the explosion while the blast was breaking through the star's surface.

This matters because supernovae evolve extremely quickly during their earliest moments. Usually, astronomers discover these explosions after the most fleeting phase has already passed. In this case, rapid detection allowed researchers to study the geometry of the blast almost immediately, revealing a shape that differed from a simple spherical explosion.

The Goldilocks universe: Is the cosmos fine-tuned for our existence?
Opinion by Christopher L. Reese • 5mo • 6 min read


Greg Rakozy/Unsplash 
Greg Rakozy/Unsplash
One of the remarkable scientific discoveries of the past several decades is that the universe and Earth appear fine-tuned for life.

Philosopher of science Stephen C. Meyer explains that fine-tuning “refers to the discovery that many properties of the universe fall within extremely narrow and improbable ranges that turn out to be absolutely necessary for complex forms of life ... to exist.” It’s important to note that the term fine-tuning/tuned is a neutral description that doesn’t imply the existence of God. It’s a designation routinely used by scientists and scholars of all stripes.

Although scientific findings are always provisional, it seems difficult to avoid the conclusion that an incredibly powerful and intelligent being designed our universe to support life. In what follows, we’ll look at the scientific credibility of fine-tuning, specific examples, possible explanations for it, and consider some objections. Fine-tuning is not surprising if Christianity is true, since God intended to create human and animal life (Genesis 1), but it is surprising on naturalism, where it appears to be an astounding coincidence.

An established belief

One will occasionally meet skeptics who believe fine-tuning is an idea invented by Christians but not taken seriously by scientists. The following quotations show that this is a misconception.

Agnostic physicist Sir Fred Hoyle: “A common sense interpretation of the facts suggests that a superintellect has monkeyed with physics, as well as with chemistry and biology, and that there are no blind forces worth speaking about in nature.”
Atheist physicist Stephen Hawking: “The remarkable fact is that the values of these numbers seem to have been very finely adjusted to make possible the development of life.”
Agnostic physicist P. C. W. Davies: “The entire universe is balanced on a knife-edge, and would be total chaos if any of the natural ‘constants’ were off even slightly.”
“On the face of it, the universe does look as if it has been designed by an intelligent creator expressly for the purpose of spawning sentient beings.”

Atheist physicist Steven Weinberg: “Life as we know it would be impossible if any one of several physical quantities had slightly different values.”
It’s notable that cosmic fine-tuning was one of the reasons the distinguished atheist thinker Antony Flew changed his mind about God’s existence, as recounted in his 2007 book There Is a God: How the World’s Most Notorious Atheist Changed His Mind.

Examples of fine-tuning

Philosopher Robin Collins points out, “If the initial explosion of the big bang had differed in strength by as little as one part in 1060 [i.e., 10 followed by 60 zeros], the universe would have either quickly collapsed back on itself or expanded too rapidly for stars to form. In either case, life would be impossible.”

This is a mind-boggling number. Collins likens this improbability to “firing a bullet at a one-inch target on the other side of the observable universe, twenty billion light years away, and hitting the target.”

He also observes that “if gravity had been stronger or weaker by one part in 1040, then life-sustaining stars like the sun could not exist.” If gravity were slightly stronger, stars would burn out in millions, rather than billions, of years (our sun is about 4.6 billion years old). If gravity were slightly weaker, most stars would never form at all — or would be too small and cold.

Oxford mathematician and philosopher John Lennox helps us understand this vast improbability as follows:

“Cover America with coins in a column reaching to the moon (380,000 km or 236,000 miles away), then do the same for a billion other continents of the same size. Paint one coin red and put it somewhere in one of the billion piles. Blindfold a friend and ask her to pick it out. The odds are about 1 in 1040 that she will.”

A little closer to home, Earth’s position in the solar system is in what scientists call the Goldilocks Zone, where it’s not too hot and not too cold, allowing for liquid water to exist on its surface. The size of Earth also ensures that it has the right gravity to retain an atmosphere suitable for life without being too strong to inhibit the mobility of organisms.

Many other examples could be cited, but these illustrate the almost inconceivable odds against a life-permitting universe and Earth.

Explanations and objections

These numbers are so surprising that they call out for an explanation, and there seem to be only three options: physical necessity, chance, or design.

Regarding physical necessity — that the universe had to have the properties that it does — there are no good reasons to believe this. As far as scientists can tell, the universe could have had a vast range of different laws, constants, and qualities. To cite Davies again, “There is not a shred of evidence that the [parameters of our] universe [are] logically necessary. Indeed, as a theoretical physicist I find it rather easy to imagine alternative universes that are logically consistent, and therefore equal contenders for reality.”

Regarding chance, we saw earlier how incredibly unlikely it is that any possible universe would support life. When you combine the improbabilities of all the fine-tuned parameters together, the odds against life become overwhelming. The one remaining option is design. All our experience tells us that only rational agents design things, and thus a cosmic designer is the best explanation for the universe’s fine-tuning.

Objections

Space prohibits an extended discussion of objections to fine-tuning. I’ll briefly address two that are frequently mentioned.

The first is known as the Weak Anthropic Principle, and is raised, for example, by physicist Martin Rees: “Some would argue that this fine-tuning of the universe, which seems so providential, is nothing to be surprised about, since we could not exist otherwise.” Thus, we should not be surprised that the universe is fine-tuned for life, since we are here observing that it is.

But as philosopher Douglas Groothuis points out, this confuses two related but distinct ideas: 1. the truism that we couldn’t observe anything unless the universe was life-permitting, and 2. an explanation of why the universe is so finely tuned. Acknowledging the first observation doesn’t negate the need to explain why, against all odds, our universe is life-permitting.

Second, some thinkers appeal to the idea of a multiverse to explain fine-tuning. If billions, or even an infinite number, of other universes exist, one of those universes will inevitably permit life. We happen to be in the lucky universe that does. There is no experimental evidence, however, that a multiverse exists, and some see it as an ad hoc proposal to avoid the theistic implications of fine-tuning. As physicist John Polkinghorne writes, “Let us recognize these speculations for what they are. They are not physics, but in the strictest sense, metaphysics. There is no purely scientific reason to believe in an ensemble of universes.”

While the multiverse hypothesis is complex, ad hoc, and lacks evidence, the design hypothesis is simple (one creator) and, as noted earlier, draws on our universal experience that only minds design things. Thus, fine-tuning provides compelling evidence that God exists and intended to create living beings.

And this sounds very much like the kind of God we find described in Genesis who creates a heavens and earth that are hospitable for life.

False (About this rating?)

Story by Emery Winter • 1w • 5 min read


A screenshot of pair of AI-generated images stacked vertically together, the top one showing a black bear carrying a raccoon to the back of a building and the bottom one of a black bear carrying a fox to the back of a very similar, but not exactly the same building. Snopes' red 
A screenshot of pair of AI-generated images stacked vertically together, the top one showing a black bear carrying a raccoon to the back of a building and the bottom one of a black bear carrying a fox to the back of a very similar, but not exactly the same building. Snopes' red

Claim:

As shared in September 2026 social media posts, a black bear took a raccoon and later a fox to a wildlife center where it had previously been treated.

Rating:

False (About this rating?)

In September 2026, a rumor circulated online about a black bear that apparently took a wounded raccoon, and later a wounded fox, to a wildlife center where the bear itself had been treated.

For example, one Facebook post (archived) said:

The first time, nobody believed it. Security cameras at a wildlife center captured a black bear approaching a rear entrance carrying a wounded raccoon in its mouth. It placed the animal gently beside the door. Then disappeared into the woods.

[…]

Several weeks later, cameras recorded a bear approaching a side entrance with an injured fox. Same careful approach. Same gentle placement near the door. Same quiet exit into the trees.

Both animals were found alive and received treatment.

Workers compared the two recordings. Both bears had the same unusual white mark on their chest. Same bear. Two deliveries. Two injured animals brought to the one place that could help them.

Staff searched old photographs and records of bears previously treated at the facility. That's when everything clicked.

Years earlier, the same bear had been rescued, treated at that very wildlife center, and released back into the wild. He had been a patient there. And now he was bringing patients back.

Officials believe the bear remembered where it had once been helped and returned because it somehow understood that injured animals would be safe there.



Other Facebook (archived) posts (archived) shared the same text. The black bear story also appeared in a YouTube short (archived).

Expand article logo  Continue reading

Snopes readers sent emails and searched the website wondering whether the story was true.

There's no evidence supporting the rumor that a black bear took a wounded raccoon and a fox to a wildlife center it had previously received treatment at. It was posted online by a page with a history of posting heartwarming — though fictitious — animal stories generated with artificial intelligence tools.

25 Ways The World Around You Isn’t What You Think It Is
Reality is a lot stranger than it looks. Every single day, you walk through a world that your brain has quietly reconstructed, simplified, and filtered — presenting you with a version of life that feels solid, logical, and complete. It isn’t. Beneath the surface of ordinary experience lies a cascade of mind-bending truths about science, nature, history, and human perception that challenge nearly everything you assume to be straightforward.

From the color of the sky to the food on your plate, from the way your brain processes time to the biological complexity of a single breath, the world around you isn’t what you think it is — and that’s not a bad thing. It’s actually one of the most exciting realities you could absorb. The 25 revelations below span physics, biology, psychology, history, and everyday life, and each one promises to permanently shift how you see something familiar.

Your Senses Are Not Showing You Reality
Color Doesn’t Exist Outside Your Brain
When you look at a ripe tomato and see red, you’re experiencing one of the most elegant illusions in nature. The tomato itself has no color. It simply reflects light at a wavelength of approximately 700 nanometers. Your brain intercepts that signal and assigns it the experience of “red.” Different species interpret the same wavelength completely differently — a mantis shrimp can detect 16 types of color receptors compared to your 3, meaning its version of that tomato looks nothing like yours.

Color is entirely a construct of your nervous system. It doesn’t exist in the external world. The universe, at its most fundamental level, is colorless.

You’re Mostly Empty Space
Here’s something that should stop you in your tracks: you are not solid. Atoms — the building blocks of everything you’re made of — are approximately 99.9999999% empty space. The nucleus of an atom is so tiny relative to the atom’s size that if you scaled an atom up to the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be roughly the size of a marble sitting at the center.

The reason you can’t walk through walls isn’t that matter is solid. It’s because the electromagnetic forces between atoms repel each other. You are, in the most literal sense, fields of energy floating in an almost entirely empty structure.

Your Brain Fabricates Your Visual Field
You have a blind spot in each eye — a point on the retina where the optic nerve connects, leaving zero photoreceptors. Yet you never experience a gap in your vision. Your brain fills it in, seamlessly inventing what should be there based on surrounding information.

This isn’t a glitch. It’s a feature. Your brain is constantly constructing your experience of reality rather than passively receiving it. What you see is always a best guess — an educated prediction — not a direct feed.

The Science Beneath Everyday Things
Fire Is Not a Thing — It’s a Process
You’ve probably never questioned what fire actually is. Fire is not a substance or an object. It’s a chemical reaction — specifically, rapid oxidation releasing heat and light. The flame you see is actually superheated gas. You’re looking at a process, not a thing, and that process consumes itself to continue existing.

This distinction matters: fire has no specific shape, no fixed temperature, and no independent existence. It only persists as long as fuel, heat, and oxygen keep the reaction going.

Glass Is Neither a Solid Nor a Liquid
You may have heard the myth that old windows are thicker at the bottom because glass flows slowly downward over centuries. That’s false — but the truth is equally strange. Glass is technically an amorphous solid, meaning it lacks the crystal structure of true solids but doesn’t flow like a liquid either. It occupies a unique state of matter that scientists sometimes call a “supercooled liquid” trapped in a rigid state.

The old windows are thicker at the bottom simply because historical glassmakers placed the thicker side down for stability. But the fact that glass doesn’t neatly fit into the solid-liquid-gas model says a lot about how incomplete our everyday categories really are.

Time Moves Differently Depending on Where You Are
Einstein’s theory of relativity isn’t just an abstract concept — it has measurable, practical consequences on Earth right now. Time passes slightly faster at higher altitudes, where gravitational pull is weaker. GPS satellites orbiting the Earth have to account for this effect constantly; without corrections for relativistic time dilation, GPS systems would accumulate errors of roughly 10 kilometers per day.

Your own body ages infinitesimally faster at your head than at your feet because of the difference in gravitational field. The world isn’t running on one universal clock. Every point in space experiences time at a slightly different rate.

Nature Is Far Stranger Than You Imagined
Trees Are Communicating Right Now
Beneath every forest floor lies an intricate network of fungal threads called mycorrhizal networks — what scientists have nicknamed the “Wood Wide Web.” Trees use this fungal highway to exchange nutrients, water, and chemical signals. Older “mother trees” actively send carbon and nutrients to younger seedlings through this network, particularly to their own offspring.

Research published in the journal Nature has demonstrated that trees can even send distress signals through this network when they’re under attack from insects, allowing neighboring trees to ramp up their own chemical defenses in advance. A forest isn’t just a collection of individual trees. It’s a cooperating superorganism.

Cleopatra Lived Closer in Time to the Moon Landing Than to the Pyramids
The Great Pyramid of Giza was completed around 2560 BCE. Cleopatra was born in 69 BCE — more than 2,490 years after the pyramid’s construction. The Apollo 11 Moon landing happened in 1969 CE, just 2,038 years after Cleopatra’s birth.

This means that if you’re drawing a timeline, Cleopatra sits closer to humans walking on the Moon than she does to the people who built her civilization’s most iconic monuments. The ancient world stretched back far longer than most people intuitively grasp.

Sharks Are Older Than Trees
Modern sharks have been swimming Earth’s oceans for approximately 450 million years. Trees, as a life form, only appeared on Earth roughly 350 million years ago. This means sharks predate the existence of forests by 100 million years. They survived every mass extinction event — including the one that killed the dinosaurs — and emerged largely unchanged.

To put that in perspective, sharks are older than Saturn’s rings, which scientists now estimate formed between 10 and 100 million years ago.

The Human Body Defies Your Assumptions
Your Gut Has Its Own Nervous System
The enteric nervous system — the complex web of neurons lining your gastrointestinal tract — contains approximately 500 million nerve cells. That’s roughly five times as many neurons as are in your spinal cord. It operates largely independently of your brain, earning it the nickname “the second brain.”

This system doesn’t just manage digestion. It produces about 90% of your body’s serotonin, the neurotransmitter most associated with mood and emotional wellbeing. The connection between gut health and mental health isn’t metaphorical. It’s neurological.

You Replace Most of Your Body Constantly
The idea that you are the same physical person you were 10 years ago is a biological fiction. Different cells in your body are continuously replaced at different rates — red blood cells last about 120 days, skin cells are replaced every 2 to 3 weeks, and the cells lining your gut renew every 5 days. Even bone, which seems permanent, completely regenerates roughly every 10 years.

The atoms that make up your body today are almost entirely different from the atoms that made up your body a decade ago. You are more like a pattern than a fixed object.

Your Memory Is Reconstructed Every Time You Access It
Memory doesn’t work like a video recording. Every time you retrieve a memory, your brain reconstructs it from stored fragments — and in doing so, you subtly alter it. The process of remembering is also the process of rewriting. Emotional state, context, and new information all bleed into the reconstruction, which is why eyewitness testimony is notoriously unreliable and why your memory of childhood events may bear only a loose resemblance to what actually happened.

Neuroscientist Elizabeth Loftus has spent decades demonstrating how easy it is to implant entirely false memories in people’s minds — memories they experience with complete confidence.

History and Society Are Not What You Learned
Napoleon Was Not Particularly Short
One of history’s most persistent myths is that Napoleon Bonaparte was unusually short. He wasn’t. Napoleon stood approximately 5 feet 7 inches tall (about 170 cm), which was average to slightly above average for a French man of his era. The confusion arose partly from a difference between French and British units of measurement — his height of “5 feet 2 inches” in French units converted to taller in English inches — and partly from British wartime propaganda that deliberately caricatured him as small and ridiculous.

The “short Napoleon” is a myth that has endured for over 200 years largely because it’s too satisfying to abandon.

Vikings Did Not Wear Horned Helmets
The horned helmet is perhaps the most durable historical costume error in popular culture. Actual Norse warriors wore practical iron or leather helmets, often with a simple nose guard. Only one well-preserved Viking Age helmet has ever been found — the Gjermundbu helmet — and it has no horns.

Horned helmets did exist in ancient Scandinavia, but they were ceremonial objects dating from the Bronze Age, over 1,000 years before the Viking Age. The popular image of the horn-helmeted Viking was largely created by 19th-century Romantic artists and theatrical costume designers.

The Great Wall of China Is Not Visible From Space
Astronauts have definitively confirmed that the Great Wall of China cannot be seen with the naked eye from low Earth orbit, let alone from the Moon. Chinese astronaut Yang Liwei specifically looked for it during his 2003 mission and could not find it. The wall is simply too narrow — averaging only 15 to 30 feet wide — to be resolved by the unaided human eye from that distance.

NASA has confirmed that roads, airports, and reservoirs are actually far more visible from space than the Great Wall.

Food and Everyday Objects Hide Surprising Truths
Honey Is Essentially Bee Vomit
Honey bees collect nectar from flowers, storing it in a special second stomach called a honey stomach or crop. Back at the hive, they regurgitate that nectar and pass it between bees multiple times, with enzymes breaking down the sugars at each transfer. The resulting liquid is then deposited into honeycomb cells and evaporated to create honey.

Technically, the process is enzymatic regurgitation rather than classic vomiting, but the basic mechanism is that bees are storing and transferring partially digested material. Honey is still delicious. It’s also extraordinarily shelf-stable — edible honey has been found in 3,000-year-old Egyptian tombs.

Strawberries Are Not Berries — But Bananas Are
Botanical definitions of “berry” are nothing like what you’d expect. A true botanical berry is a fleshy fruit that develops from a single flower with one ovary. By that definition, bananas qualify as berries. Avocados qualify. Cucumbers qualify. Pumpkins qualify.

Strawberries, however, are technically “accessory fruits” — the red flesh develops from the receptacle of the flower, not the ovary. Raspberries and blackberries are “aggregate fruits” made up of many small drupelets. The common names humans gave these fruits had nothing to do with botany.

Chocolate Was Once Currency
The cacao bean was so highly valued by the Aztec civilization that it functioned as a currency. Historical records indicate that one turkey could be purchased for 100 cacao beans. Slaves were sold for 100 beans. A rabbit cost about 10 beans. Chocolate drinks were a luxury reserved for elites, warriors, and religious ceremonies — not a casual snack.

When Spanish conquistadors arrived in Mesoamerica in the 16th century, they encountered a sophisticated economy running partly on what would eventually become the world’s most popular flavor of ice cream.

Physics and the Cosmos Will Break Your Brain
The Sun’s Light Is Ancient Before It Reaches You
The light hitting your skin on a sunny afternoon left the surface of the Sun approximately 8 minutes and 20 seconds ago. But before that photon ever escaped the Sun’s surface, it spent an almost incomprehensible amount of time bouncing around inside the solar core. Estimates vary, but a photon generated by nuclear fusion in the Sun’s core may take anywhere from 10,000 to 170,000 years to randomly walk its way outward to the surface.

The warmth of today’s sunshine is the result of energy created tens of thousands of years ago, long before modern humans had developed written language.

Most of the Universe Is Invisible
Everything you can see — every star, planet, galaxy, and nebula — accounts for only about 5% of the total energy content of the universe. The remaining 95% consists of dark matter (roughly 27%) and dark energy (roughly 68%), neither of which has ever been directly detected or fully explained.

Dark energy, which appears to be driving the accelerating expansion of the universe, is arguably the biggest unsolved mystery in all of physics. Scientists know it must exist to explain observations, but no one knows what it actually is.

Quantum Objects Exist in Multiple States at Once
At the quantum level — the scale of electrons, photons, and subatomic particles — objects genuinely exist in multiple states simultaneously until they are observed or measured. This isn’t a theoretical abstraction. It’s been confirmed through repeated experimentation, most famously through the double-slit experiment, which shows that particles behave differently depending on whether they are being observed.

This phenomenon, called quantum superposition, means that the act of observation literally changes the behavior of matter. The universe at its smallest scale is participatory, not passive.

Psychology and Perception Bend Reality
The Dunning-Kruger Effect Is Working on You Right Now
Psychological research by David Dunning and Justin Kruger published in 1999 demonstrated a consistent pattern: people with limited knowledge in a domain tend to overestimate their own competence, while genuine experts tend to underestimate theirs. The less you know, the more confident you often feel.

This isn’t a flaw in other people. Studies suggest it operates in virtually everyone across virtually every domain. The areas where you feel most confident are often the areas most worth examining with fresh skepticism.

Your Decisions Are Made Before You’re Conscious of Them
Neuroscientist Benjamin Libet’s landmark experiments in the 1980s revealed something deeply unsettling about free will. Using EEG measurements, Libet found that brain activity associated with a voluntary movement begins approximately 550 milliseconds before subjects reported consciously deciding to move — and about 350 milliseconds before they were even aware of any intention.

Your conscious mind appears to be narrating decisions that your brain has already initiated. Whether this eliminates free will or simply reframes it remains fiercely debated, but the data is solid: conscious awareness is not the starting point of your choices.

You Can’t Actually Multitask
The human brain does not multitask. What it does is task-switch — rapidly shifting attention from one thing to another. Stanford researcher Clifford Nass found that people who considered themselves chronic multitaskers were actually worse at filtering irrelevant information, worse at organizing thoughts, and slower at switching between tasks than those who rarely multitasked.

Every time you switch tasks, there’s a cognitive switching cost — a brief period of reduced performance. What feels like parallel processing is actually rapid serial processing, and the switching costs add up significantly over time.

The Living World Upends Your Categories
Viruses Are Not Alive — But They Evolve
The question of whether viruses are “alive” is not settled. They have no cells, cannot reproduce on their own, don’t metabolize, and don’t maintain homeostasis — failing every traditional definition of life. Yet they carry genetic information, evolve through natural selection, and adapt to their environments in ways we typically associate with living things.

Viruses exist in a strange gray zone that forces biologists to question what “life” even means. Some scientists now argue that life should be understood as a spectrum rather than a binary category.

Fungi Are More Closely Related to Animals Than to Plants
When you think of fungi — mushrooms, mold, yeast — you might lump them in with plants. Taxonomically, that’s wrong. Genetic analysis places fungi closer to animals on the tree of life than to plants. Both fungi and animals belong to a group called Opisthokonta; plants do not.

One key similarity: fungi, like animals, are heterotrophs — they absorb nutrients from external sources rather than producing their own through photosynthesis. They also have cell walls made of chitin, the same material found in insect exoskeletons.

A Single Teaspoon of Soil Contains More Microorganisms Than There Are People on Earth
One teaspoon of healthy agricultural soil contains between 100 million and 1 billion bacteria, in addition to thousands of species of fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and other microorganisms. The total number of microbial species in a single handful of good forest soil can exceed the number of known plant and animal species on the entire planet.

The ground beneath your feet is one of the most densely populated and biologically complex environments in existence. Every footstep you take lands on an entire civilization.

The Unexpected Truths That Change Everything
Your Smartphone Has More Computing Power Than NASA Used to Land on the Moon
The Apollo Guidance Computer that navigated astronauts to the Moon in 1969 ran at 0.043 MHz and had 4 kilobytes of RAM. A modern mid-range smartphone runs at over 3,000 MHz and carries multiple gigabytes of RAM — millions of times more processing power. The phone in your pocket has capabilities that would have been incomprehensible to the engineers who accomplished one of humanity’s greatest achievements with slide rules and mechanical systems.

90% of the Cells in Your Body Aren’t Human
The human body contains trillions of microorganisms — bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses — collectively known as the microbiome. While earlier estimates suggested microbial cells outnumbered human cells 10-to-1, more recent research from the Weizmann Institute of Science revised the ratio closer to roughly 1:1. Either way, a significant portion of what makes up “you” is not human in origin.

These microorganisms are not passengers. They perform essential functions including digestion, immune regulation, vitamin synthesis, and even influencing mood and behavior.

Frequently Asked Questions
Why does our brain create illusions instead of showing us reality directly?

Evolution optimized your brain for survival, not accuracy. Constructing a simplified, useful model of the world is far more efficient than processing raw sensory data in real time. Your brain takes shortcuts — and most of the time, those shortcuts work beautifully. But they also mean your experience of reality is always a filtered interpretation.

Is quantum superposition something that happens at the human scale?

No — quantum effects like superposition occur at the subatomic scale. At the scale of everyday objects, quantum effects average out and classical physics takes over. However, some researchers are investigating whether quantum processes play a role in biological systems like photosynthesis and bird navigation.

Did Libet’s free will experiments actually prove we have no free will?

Not definitively. Libet himself noted that while unconscious brain activity precedes conscious intention, subjects retained the ability to veto the movement after becoming aware of it. Many philosophers argue this “veto power” is itself a meaningful form of free will. The debate remains active in neuroscience and philosophy.

How can fungi be more related to animals than plants if they look so different?

Evolutionary relationships are determined by genetic ancestry, not physical appearance. Convergent evolution often makes unrelated organisms look similar, and divergent evolution can make closely related organisms look very different. Genetic sequencing revealed the fungi-animal connection in the late 20th century, overturning centuries of classification based on appearance alone.

Is the Wood Wide Web a proven scientific phenomenon?

Yes, the mycorrhizal network itself is well-established science. The extent to which trees intentionally “communicate” is still debated — some scientists prefer more cautious language — but the transfer of carbon, nutrients, and chemical signals through fungal networks between trees has been confirmed through multiple peer-reviewed studies.

Why do these facts feel so counterintuitive?

Because your brain is built to predict and categorize, not to question. Your cognitive systems are wired to confirm what you already believe and to make sense of your immediate experience. Facts that contradict deep-seated assumptions create cognitive dissonance — an uncomfortable mental friction that your brain naturally tries to resolve by dismissing the new information. Recognizing this tendency is the first step to thinking more clearly.

—

The Takeaway
The 25 ways the world around you isn’t what you think it is aren’t reasons for anxiety — they’re invitations to curiosity. Reality is far richer, stranger, and more interesting than the simplified version your brain presents by default. From the quantum behavior of subatomic particles to the fact that a forest floor is a living network of communication, the universe is packed with truths that reward those willing to look past the obvious.

Websites like List25 have built entire audiences around exactly this kind of revelation — because humans are fundamentally wired to find wonder in the unexpected. The next time something feels certain and settled, that’s exactly when it’s worth asking: what’s actually going on here? You might be surprised how often the answer is stranger than you imagined.

You can expand your consciousness to see hidden layers of reality, Oxford physicist claims
Story by Vlatko Vedral • 5mo • 6 min read


A leading physicist argues the brain may operate like a quantum system—and that enhancing it could create humans who perceive hidden layers of reality.

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

As a quantum physicist, my work has led me to believe we could drastically enhance human consciousness, the inexplicable machinations that make you feel like you. It explains everything from your most profound revelations to how you decide which snack you’re hungry for, and yet scientists still struggle to understand it.

In my view, there are certain biological limitations to human creativity, and if my theory is correct, we could one day create “enhanced humans” that could experience parts of reality that are otherwise hidden—expanding on our very consciousness.

My idea hinges on an equally mysterious topic: quantum physics, or the study of how the universe works at the smallest levels. The late American physicist David Bohm, PhD, argued that the basic features of quantum physics—like superposition, interference, and entanglement—are an extension of the workings of our brain. Bohm was here echoing one of the founders of quantum physics, the Dane Niels Bohr, PhD.

Bohr believed that particles (like electrons) and waves (like light) could each exhibit particle-like or wave-like properties, depending on the circumstances. Bohr thought this special wave-particle duality of quantum physics could mirror the duality between our own intuitive and logical thinking—or the wandering, uncertain mind and definitive decision making.

Thanks to advances in science, we’re now able to move beyond Bohm and Bohr’s theorizing; we can test their hypotheses through experiments. The famous double-slit experiment, for instance, is a quintessential test that proved a quantum object is capable of being in two places at the same time, a property known as quantum superposition.

In the most basic version of the experiment, a laser shoots photons (or light particles) at a plate pierced with two slits. Light passes through these openings and creates a pattern on a screen behind the plate. All quantum objects we have tested so far—such as electrons, neutrons, and larger molecules—are capable of passing through the two slits at the same time. When the particles pass through both slits at the same time, they produce a distinct interference pattern, indicating the particles are also acting as waves would. However, if we observe which of the slits the quantum object has gone through, then the interference effect disappears and the object suddenly behaves much like a tennis ball travelling through the air in a predictable arc.

Bohr saw this as an instance of the principle of complementarity, where the two features—waves and particles—can each manifest in quantum physics, but never at the same time in the same experiment. This is, in fact, the foundation of Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle, according to which, if we know one property of the particle (say, we measure its position accurately), then another one must become highly uncertain (its speed).

Indeed, Bohr and Bohm must have been onto something since our thinking process appears to obey a kind of uncertainty principle akin to Heisenberg’s. When we are making up our mind about what to do next—say, what to eat for dinner—we follow several paths in a seemingly dreamlike manner, unaware of the details of each road we explore. Finally, a definitive decision is made resembling the outcome of a quantum interference process in which several paths are enfolded simultaneously, only to yield a single, definitive outcome. This may look like deciding, “I will have chicken for dinner.”

Once you are aware of a definite thought (or which slit the particle goes through in the double-slit parlance), you can no longer think laterally—or about many outcomes at once—so there is no interference. Likewise, when you are thinking laterally, with a multitude of thoughts at the same time, you cannot be aware of definite thoughts.

I will here take a big leap and assume, despite lack of direct experimental evidence, that, at some fundamental level, our consciousness is the product of a huge number of quantum double-slit experiments executed within the circuitry of our brain. This model of consciousness is basically an interconnected array of micro quantum double-slit experiments, with the outcomes of some fed as inputs to others. In other words, it’s one big snowball effect, with interactions compounding each other. These interactions may also become entangled—or behave in perfect synchronicity—during their interfering actions.

So, when contemplating, we start with a definitive idea, then enter a superposition of thoughts, or a state of uncertainty. This might look like deciding to renovate a room in your house, and then having to decide which color to paint the walls. Then, presumably through introspection, we collapse this superposition into another definitive outcome; this then expands into yet another superposition, which lasts until the next definitive thought. In terms of remodeling, you may decide that the room will be blue, but then you must contemplate which shade, starting the cycle over again.

This seems to be a good model for the interplay between our conscious (definite states) and subconscious (interference of thoughts) states of mind. Our comprehension is basically a huge computational process in which we switch between the logical and intuitive pathways, or between particles and waves.

I imagine that most if not all of the creative power of human intellect resides in the parallel processing of the subconscious due to it being in many states at the same time. Notably, artificial intelligence is devoid of this feature; AIs follow a well-defined logical sequence of steps, a computational program, albeit a stupendously complex one. But there is no quantum uncertainty in any of it, not at the fundamental level. In contrast, most people report that a new idea, a flash of genius and originality, frequently comes on suddenly and unexpectedly, as if out of nowhere, when the mind is not even focusing on the problem to be solved. None of that happens with AI.

But what if our biological makeup limits how creative we can be? Maybe the timing of the clock that governs our introspections forces our intuitive periods—or the times of uncertainty—to be too brief. Could we use our quantum technologies to extend the wavelike processing inside our brains? I am here inspired by Aldous Huxley, who suggested in his famous book, The Doors of Perception, that drugs could alter our consciousness, revealing true reality. But rather than using drugs, I envision quantum chips designed to suppress the “noise” that induces introspection, allowing a longer interference period for our intuitive thoughts to develop. This has the potential to be far more potent than what Huxley could ever have imagined.

For my idea to work, we would first have to understand where and how these superpositions are stored and manipulated in the brain. The British physicist Roger Penrose, PhD, has speculated that this occurs within microtubules, which are dynamic, hollow, rod-like components of the eukaryotic cytoskeleton that are responsible for things such as intercellular transport. Despite some circumstantial evidence, we do not have a strong reason to believe that microtubules are capable of quantum interference, but they are certainly worth further investigation. Once we understand how our brain uses quantum effects, we could then design a quantum chip that interfaces with the relevant biological components. Theoretically, the device would be able to upload superposition states to store them for longer periods and shield them from collapse, helping us to enhance our creative wavelike thinking.

One wonders what kind of power would be unleashed by doing this. I imagine the change would not be purely quantitative, so that we merely become faster calculators or quicker problem solvers, although even that would be amazing. Instead, I think the change could be qualitative, expanding our perception into a completely different realm, effectively creating a new species. We might theoretically become more powerful than modern humans, just as we currently are with respect to other apes. Quantum-enhanced humans would see further domains of reality that would otherwise remain hidden forever from us ordinary humans.

Whether this is just wishful thinking of yet another optimistic physicist—only time will tell.

Research into ancient Austrian salt mines revealed an extensive Iron Age tunnel complex and evidence of mining from as long as 7,300 years ago.

Nearly 1,000 feet of ancient salt mining tunnels and chambers in the mountains near Hallstatt, Austria, have revealed a remarkably extensive mining history from the Iron Age. But the mountain’s history of salt mining goes back even further—7,300 years to be exact. Archaeologists have the ancient tools to prove it.

As the Natural History Museum in Vienna continues to open a window into the salt mining history of the site, newly discovered mining chambers tell the story of how ancient inhabitants tracked salt deposits through the mountains, digging deeper with each hopeful find. “These impressively demonstrate how salt mining has driven innovations in technology, organization, and transport since prehistoric times, playing a pivotal role in Hallstatt’s development into one of Europe’s oldest industrial centers,” the museum wrote in a statement. “The exceptionally well-preserved finds from the salt mine provide valuable insights into the economic practices and lifestyles of past societies.”

In a major breakthrough, physicists directly image the quantum fluctuations of empty space
Story by Michelle Starr • 3w • 4 min read


Even 'empty' space is seething with quantum fluctuations. (Quardia/iStock/Getty Images Plus)
Even 'empty' space is seething with quantum fluctuations. (Quardia/iStock/Getty Images Plus)
Empty space is never actually truly empty.

Even a quantum field in its lowest-energy state, the vacuum, has unavoidable fluctuations that arise from the Heisenberg uncertainty principle: Certain pairs of properties cannot both have precisely defined values at the same time.

In real-world terms, this means that a quantum field should resemble a staticky TV, and plenty of indirect evidence suggests this is the case.

Now, however, physicists led by Yansheng Zhang at the University of Cambridge in the UK have made a major breakthrough: They have directly imaged fluctuations in a lab-made quantum field.

"Observing such fluctuations in the sine-Gordon limit opens many possibilities for laboratory simulations of relativistic fields in regimes that are presently not theoretically tractable," they write in a preprint posted to arXiv.


YouTube Thumbnail
YouTube Thumbnail
Vacuum fluctuations aren't just theoretical jiggery-pokery – they actually have real effects on the physical Universe.

Excited atoms interacting with fluctuations in a vacuum can spontaneously decay to a lower state, emitting a photon in the process. Electrons in a hydrogen atom can have slightly different energy levels due to fluctuation interactions.

Then there's the Casimir effect. If you put two conducting plates very close together in a vacuum, they experience a measurable force that pulls them towards each other due to the difference in quantum fluctuations between the plates compared to outside the plates.

Expand article logo  Continue reading

Heck, they even play a role in the theoretical Hawking radiation emitted at the boundary of a black hole.

And in cosmology, tiny primordial quantum fluctuations are thought to have been stretched to enormous scales during cosmic inflation. Those tiny variations eventually provided the seeds from which matter collected, ultimately producing the cosmic web of galaxies and clusters we see today.

Quantum field theory describes the Universe as permeated by fields, with particles emerging as excitations in those fields. Each field can be thought of as a collection of modes of different wavelengths, a little like the different ways a guitar string can vibrate.
