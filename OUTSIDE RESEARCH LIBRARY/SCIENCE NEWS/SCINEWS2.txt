What are the Navier-Stokes equations, and why do they matter?
The solution in question looked at the Navier–Stokes equations. These equations describe the motion of fluids and are used in a bajillion technical applications.

They arise from Newton’s second law of motion, the famous F=ma, in which force is equal to the mass of an object times its acceleration, in combination with the assumption that stress in a fluid is a combination of its viscosity and pressure.

They are the generalization of what are called the Euler equations, which consider a fluid that is not viscous.

The equations are used in everything from the design of cars and airplanes and understanding how blood flows through our body, to how pollution spreads through the environment. But in pure mathematical thought, they represent a problem.

This is called the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem.

The question is: are there always solutions for these equations in three dimensions, or do the equations break down, or ‘blow up’? This would be the presence of a nonsensical solution in which the velocity becomes infinite, something that does not happen in a physical system.

Because of this, the Navier-Stokes equations are part of something called the Millennium Prize.

What are the Millennium Prize Problems?
The Millennium Prize Problems are a set of seven complex mathematical problems selected by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000. If anyone solves any of these problems, then they will win a prize of $1 million.

To date, only one problem has been solved. In 2010 a Russian mathematician called Grigori Perelman solved the Poincaré conjecture, but he refused the money as the prize ignored Richard S. Hamilton, whose work was crucial for Perelman's solution.

The Clay Mathematics Institute has yet to assess OpenAI's claims to confirm if the problems have actually been solved. Whether or not the solution is correct, OpenAI has already said that it does not intend to claim the Prize.

Is this the moment that math changes forever?
So, back to the beef. On September 8, OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, announced that they had solved the Navier–Stokes equations and, indeed, everything did blow up.

In a press release, OpenAI stated that on September 1 that they heard rumors that two Millennium Prize Problems had been resolved, so they themselves set a new Large Language Model (LLM) to look at all six unsolved problems.

In my view, there are certain biological limitations to human creativity, and if my theory is correct, we could one day create “enhanced humans” that could experience parts of reality that are otherwise hidden—expanding on our very consciousness.

My idea hinges on an equally mysterious topic: quantum physics, or the study of how the universe works at the smallest levels. The late American physicist David Bohm, PhD, argued that the basic features of quantum physics—like superposition, interference, and entanglement—are an extension of the workings of our brain. Bohm was here echoing one of the founders of quantum physics, the Dane Niels Bohr, PhD.

Bohr believed that particles (like electrons) and waves (like light) could each exhibit particle-like or wave-like properties, depending on the circumstances. Bohr thought this special wave-particle duality of quantum physics could mirror the duality between our own intuitive and logical thinking—or the wandering, uncertain mind and definitive decision making.

Thanks to advances in science, we’re now able to move beyond Bohm and Bohr’s theorizing; we can test their hypotheses through experiments. The famous double-slit experiment, for instance, is a quintessential test that proved a quantum object is capable of being in two places at the same time, a property known as quantum superposition.

In the most basic version of the experiment, a laser shoots photons (or light particles) at a plate pierced with two slits. Light passes through these openings and creates a pattern on a screen behind the plate. All quantum objects we have tested so far—such as electrons, neutrons, and larger molecules—are capable of passing through the two slits at the same time. When the particles pass through both slits at the same time, they produce a distinct interference pattern, indicating the particles are also acting as waves would. However, if we observe which of the slits the quantum object has gone through, then the interference effect disappears and the object suddenly behaves much like a tennis ball travelling through the air in a predictable arc.

Bohr saw this as an instance of the principle of complementarity, where the two features—waves and particles—can each manifest in quantum physics, but never at the same time in the same experiment. This is, in fact, the foundation of Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle, according to which, if we know one property of the particle (say, we measure its position accurately), then another one must become highly uncertain (its speed).

Indeed, Bohr and Bohm must have been onto something since our thinking process appears to obey a kind of uncertainty principle akin to Heisenberg’s. When we are making up our mind about what to do next—say, what to eat for dinner—we follow several paths in a seemingly dreamlike manner, unaware of the details of each road we explore. Finally, a definitive decision is made resembling the outcome of a quantum interference process in which several paths are enfolded simultaneously, only to yield a single, definitive outcome. This may look like deciding, “I will have chicken for dinner.”

Once you are aware of a definite thought (or which slit the particle goes through in the double-slit parlance), you can no longer think laterally—or about many outcomes at once—so there is no interference. Likewise, when you are thinking laterally, with a multitude of thoughts at the same time, you cannot be aware of definite thoughts.

I will here take a big leap and assume, despite lack of direct experimental evidence, that, at some fundamental level, our consciousness is the product of a huge number of quantum double-slit experiments executed within the circuitry of our brain. This model of consciousness is basically an interconnected array of micro quantum double-slit experiments, with the outcomes of some fed as inputs to others. In other words, it’s one big snowball effect, with interactions compounding each other. These interactions may also become entangled—or behave in perfect synchronicity—during their interfering actions.

So, when contemplating, we start with a definitive idea, then enter a superposition of thoughts, or a state of uncertainty. This might look like deciding to renovate a room in your house, and then having to decide which color to paint the walls. Then, presumably through introspection, we collapse this superposition into another definitive outcome; this then expands into yet another superposition, which lasts until the next definitive thought. In terms of remodeling, you may decide that the room will be blue, but then you must contemplate which shade, starting the cycle over again.

This seems to be a good model for the interplay between our conscious (definite states) and subconscious (interference of thoughts) states of mind. Our comprehension is basically a huge computational process in which we switch between the logical and intuitive pathways, or between particles and waves.

I imagine that most if not all of the creative power of human intellect resides in the parallel processing of the subconscious due to it being in many states at the same time. Notably, artificial intelligence is devoid of this feature; AIs follow a well-defined logical sequence of steps, a computational program, albeit a stupendously complex one. But there is no quantum uncertainty in any of it, not at the fundamental level. In contrast, most people report that a new idea, a flash of genius and originality, frequently comes on suddenly and unexpectedly, as if out of nowhere, when the mind is not even focusing on the problem to be solved. None of that happens with AI.

But what if our biological makeup limits how creative we can be? Maybe the timing of the clock that governs our introspections forces our intuitive periods—or the times of uncertainty—to be too brief. Could we use our quantum technologies to extend the wavelike processing inside our brains? I am here inspired by Aldous Huxley, who suggested in his famous book, The Doors of Perception, that drugs could alter our consciousness, revealing true reality. But rather than using drugs, I envision quantum chips designed to suppress the “noise” that induces introspection, allowing a longer interference period for our intuitive thoughts to develop. This has the potential to be far more potent than what Huxley could ever have imagined.

For my idea to work, we would first have to understand where and how these superpositions are stored and manipulated in the brain. The British physicist Roger Penrose, PhD, has speculated that this occurs within microtubules, which are dynamic, hollow, rod-like components of the eukaryotic cytoskeleton that are responsible for things such as intercellular transport. Despite some circumstantial evidence, we do not have a strong reason to believe that microtubules are capable of quantum interference, but they are certainly worth further investigation. Once we understand how our brain uses quantum effects, we could then design a quantum chip that interfaces with the relevant biological components. Theoretically, the device would be able to upload superposition states to store them for longer periods and shield them from collapse, helping us to enhance our creative wavelike thinking.

One wonders what kind of power would be unleashed by doing this. I imagine the change would not be purely quantitative, so that we merely become faster calculators or quicker problem solvers, although even that would be amazing. Instead, I think the change could be qualitative, expanding our perception into a completely different realm, effectively creating a new species. We might theoretically become more powerful than modern humans, just as we currently are with respect to other apes. Quantum-enhanced humans would see further domains of reality that would otherwise remain hidden forever from us ordinary humans.

Whether this is just wishful thinking of yet another optimistic physicist—only time will tell.

Chirality is a property that makes it impossible to superimpose an object perfectly on its mirror image, and chiral molecules are known as enantiomers.
Enantiomers that favor one “hand” or the other tend to dominate in the ingredients of life, but why that’s so has largely remained a mystery.
Now, scientists think that the spin of electrons inside a molecule makes the right- or left-handed version more efficient, which may have impacted the formation of RNA billions of years ago.
Hold your hands out in front of you, both palms facing the same way. For most people, it would be safe to say that your hands are pretty good mirror images of each other—identical, but flipped over a central vertical axis. Now, move your hands together, without flipping them, so that one rests on top of the other. While your middle three fingers might line up pretty well, your pinkies and thumbs are not going to have anything to perfectly map to. Even though your hands are mirror images of each other, when you superimpose them one over the other, they don’t perfectly line up. This property is called chirality, and in addition to making it possible for you to make weird-looking shadow puppets, it’s often observed in quantum physics.

Specifically, chirality pops up a lot in the molecules in your body, which tend to be right-handed or left-handed. In fact, most biological molecules are chiral, and the two chemically identical forms of a molecule that look like reflections are known as enantiomers. For over a century and a half, the reason why organisms have an unspoken preference for one or the other enantiomer—a phenomenon known as homochirality—has been confounding scientists. Seeking an answer, physicists Yossi Paltiel (from Hebrew University in Israel) and Ron Namaan (from the Weissmann Institute in Israel) led a research team that investigated why life consistently chooses one mirror molecule over the other.

“Over the years, several schemes have been proposed regarding the emergence of chirality in life, but none of them have explained a specific handedness,” the team wrote in a study recently published in the journal Science Advances. “On the basis of the identical energetics of the two enantiomers and symmetry-imposed mapping of their properties, it has been generally expected that the absolute value of any physical effect will be the same for the two enantiomers.”

To try and solve this conundrum, the team focused on an effect discovered decades ago—the chirality-induced spin selectivity (CISS) effect—which partially explains handedness in biomolecules. The CISS effect makes it seem as if electron motion through both enantiomers of a molecule would be the same, because it’s technically the same in every other way. As it turns out, however, the ways electrons spin through the enantiomers are often similar, but they’re not exactly symmetrical. And because the spin of an electron in one enantiomer may make it work more efficiently, it keeps appearing in living things over and over again.

The direction in which electrons spin within a molecule depends on their angular momentum (momentum of rotation, as opposed to moving forwards or backwards). The research team figured out that projecting the path of these electrons involves spin-orbit coupling—an interaction inside an atom that links an electron’s spin with its rotational momentum as it orbits the nucleus—and this spin is what causes enantiomers to act differently in certain biological processes. Despite sharing the same energy, enantiomers that are in motion or functioning as transporters behave slightly differently because of opposite electron spins. Usually, it turns out that one spin direction (and thus one enantiomer) performs more efficiently than another, which is why an organism uses that version. Amino acids, for instance, are almost exclusively left-handed, while sugars tend to be right-handed.

The team believes that this research could open a portal into the distant past, when the ancestor of everything alive on Earth today came into being in the crevices of an ancient rock or a blob of primordial ooze. It’s thought that ribose aminooxazoline (RAO) could have been the precursor to RNA, and that interactions with a magnetic surface could have shaped molecules in the first life-form’s genome to favor one handedness over another.

A magnetic rock has two poles (north and south), and depending on which pole faces upward, it will attract and hold one version of a chiral molecule on its surface while leaving the other version free. This happens because when a chiral molecule is drawn toward a magnetic surface, both its electrical charge and its electron spin become polarized. Using an elegant quantum physics trick, the team led by Philipp Schindler from the Department of Experimental Physics at the University of Innsbruck has now succeeded in measuring the spectrum of a single calcium hydroxide molecule.

The work is published in the journal Nature.

The basic idea draws from quantum information processing. In a technique known as quantum logic spectroscopy, two ions are coupled via their electric repulsion: a hard-to-access molecular ion and an easily controllable auxiliary atom move together in an ion trap, exchanging information in the process.

In this way, techniques originally developed for quantum computers can be applied to molecules that are otherwise difficult to study using conventional methods.

Putting Schrödinger's cat to work
When a molecule absorbs a single infrared photon, it gains a tiny amount of momentum—normally far too small to be measured directly. The Innsbruck team achieves the sensitivity required to detect the absorption of individual photons by using a quantum-mechanically entangled atom as a detector.

"The entanglement of the particles in a so-called Schrödinger's cat state makes the system extremely sensitive to the slightest disturbances," explains Zhenlin Wu, the study's lead author. "The tiny recoil of the absorbed photon is made measurable by this cat state."

Using this method, the research team identified a characteristic molecular vibration—the O-H stretching vibration—of the singly charged calcium hydroxide molecular ion. The spectrum measured by the group agrees well with theoretical calculations performed by collaborators at the University of Warsaw.

Molecule under a quantum probe
A key advantage of this method is that it is nondestructive: The molecular ion is neither destroyed nor chemically altered during analysis. As a result, it enables the measurement and preparation of quantum states across a wide variety of molecular species, paving the way for precise spectroscopy of complex molecules and a broad range of applications in future quantum technologies.

The work tackles one of the more elusive ideas in gravitational physics: critical collapse, a process in which matter and spacetime organize themselves into a repeating pattern just before black hole formation.

A narrow ledge between collapse and escape
The basic picture is oddly familiar. “Sometimes a tiny, seemingly insignificant cause is enough to trigger a huge and dramatic change,” said Prof. Daniel Grumiller of TU Wien. He compared it to water at the freezing point, where a tiny shift can lock molecules into the ordered pattern of ice.

“This spacetime crystal is a very peculiar and fascinating object,” Grumiller said. “It is a kind of intermediate state, an unstable point that can evolve in two different directions.”

One possibility is that it fades away, leaving ordinary spacetime and freely moving particles behind. The other is more dramatic. “But if a tiny amount of energy is added, the evolution takes a completely different path,” he said. “The inconspicuous spacetime crystal turns into a black hole.”

That possibility matters because the early universe may have passed through similar extreme conditions. If such critical states existed shortly after the Big Bang, they could have produced primordial black holes, hypothetical objects that remain a live topic in cosmology.

The broad idea is not new. Numerical work dating back to 1993 showed that black holes could appear spontaneously from these critical states. Those simulations revealed a repeating, self-similar structure near the threshold of collapse, with the same pattern echoing on smaller and smaller scales.

What proved much harder was turning that behavior into an exact analytic description.

Black hole formation is notoriously difficult to write down in closed form, even in simplified setups. In this case, the researchers focused on spherical symmetry and Einstein gravity coupled to a massless scalar field, a stripped-down model that still captures the essential collapse dynamics. Even then, the equations remain a tangled set of nonlinear partial differential equations.

For years, the best route was numerical construction, often with the repeating symmetry imposed from the start. The new study takes a different path.

The trick was to leave the real universe, briefly
“Our universe has four dimensions, three dimensions of space and one dimension of time,” said Christian Ecker from the Institute for Theoretical Physics at Goethe University Frankfurt. “But in principle, nothing prevents us from writing down physical equations for a larger number of dimensions, five dimensions, forty-two dimensions, or even infinitely many.”

That sounds like a move toward greater complexity. Instead, it became a shortcut.

The team studied the problem in the limit of very many dimensions, where a quantity proportional to 1 divided by the number of dimensions becomes a small control parameter. In that regime, some of the hardest parts of the equations simplify enough to solve analytically.

Rather than trying to attack the four-dimensional problem head-on, the researchers detoured through a hypothetical universe with infinitely many dimensions and then organized corrections step by step. At leading order, they found an entire family of exact discretely self-similar solutions. At higher orders, those solutions start to recover more of the detailed behavior already seen in simulations.

That matters because the earlier numerical work suggested a very specific geometry near the threshold of collapse: an ingoing null surface, a naked singularity at the center, and an outgoing Cauchy horizon. The new formulas reproduce the regularity of the critical state in the region between the center and the self-similar horizon, while also showing how the approximation can be sharpened order by order.

All fields of the NNLO solution evaluated at 𝐷 =300. (CREDIT: Physical Review Letters)

From elegant freedom to tighter constraints
At first, the leading-order solution is surprisingly flexible. It allows infinitely many repeating patterns, each determined by a free periodic function. If that were the end of the story, the theory would be too loose to match the expectation that the critical solution at finite dimension is effectively unique.

But the freedom does not survive unchanged.

Once the team included next-to-leading corrections, the equations began to impose a consistency condition on the echoing period, the interval over which the self-similar pattern repeats. By the time they pushed the expansion further, they were able to recover qualitative features known from numerical studies, including the downward bending of certain geometric lines tied to the null energy condition and the way the horizon-related function drops below its idealized maximum.

“Our technique turns out to be remarkably stable,” said Florian Ecker of TU Wien. “Depending on the desired precision, we can systematically improve our formulas using additional approximation methods.”

The researchers do not claim the series is convergent. Like other large-dimension expansions in gravity, it is expected to be asymptotic, meaning it may not settle into a final exact sum. Still, asymptotic methods can be very powerful if each added term improves the description over the range of interest.

The present framework also stays mostly within the “past patch” of the critical spacetime, the region up to the self-similar horizon. Extending the solution farther, toward and beyond the future Cauchy horizon, remains part of the open work.

A clearer analytic foothold
The result does not mean physicists can now write down every feature of black hole formation on a single page. It does mean they have something they lacked before: a controllable analytic foothold in a problem long dominated by numerics.

That foothold could help clarify how the repeating critical state changes as the number of dimensions varies, whether higher-order conditions squeeze the family of solutions down toward a unique form, and how quantities such as the echoing period and the Choptuik exponent behave in the large-dimension limit.

For a problem built on the brink between dispersal and collapse, that is a meaningful gain. The new formulas suggest that the threshold itself is not just a numerical curiosity, but a structure that can be studied, refined, and perhaps eventually understood with much greater precision.

Practical implications of the research
This research does not predict that microscopic black holes are about to be found in a lab or in space. Its value is more foundational. It gives physicists a new analytic tool for studying one of general relativity’s most delicate tipping points, the threshold where collapse either fizzles out or becomes a black hole.

That could sharpen theoretical work on critical collapse, primordial black holes, and the mathematical structure of Einstein’s equations under extreme conditions. It also offers a way to connect numerical simulations with exact formulas, which is often where deeper physical insight begins.

Research findings are available online in the journal Physical Review Letters.

Is a fourth dimension hiding right in front of us? Scientists think so
Story by Ankita Garkoti • 1mo
What would a 4D object even look like?
©ROBERT BROOK/SCIENCE PHOTO LIBRARY/Getty Images
German mathematician and theoretical astronomer August Ferdinand Möbius was among those who explored what an extra spatial dimension might look like. One idea was that, in four dimensions, you could rotate a 3D object until it becomes its own mirror image, something that simply can't happen in our world. 

To explain this, mathematicians came up with the tesseract, or hypercube. It's an extension of a cube into the fourth dimension. Even though no one can truly picture a tesseract, mathematicians use diagrams and models to better understand it. Later, Bernhard Riemann developed the math behind four-dimensional space, which eventually helped shape Einstein's theory of relativity. The above image is a model of a tesseract with glass panels that create internal reflections suggestive of multi-dimensional space.

The result closes meaningful ground on one of the last major unresolved structural questions in particle physics: whether the heaviest neutrino is the third mass eigenstate (Normal Ordering) or the lightest (Inverted Ordering). The preference for Normal Ordering reaches a Δχ² of 9.11 — the equivalent of roughly 3.02σ significance — achieved by fitting 839,048 events across 1,536 analysis bins with 91 free parameters.

What Neutrino Mass Ordering Is — and Why It Has Stayed Open This Long
Neutrinos are the most abundant massive particles in the universe and among the least understood. They come in three "flavors" — electron, muon, and tau — and they spontaneously shift between those flavors as they travel, a phenomenon known as neutrino oscillation between flavors. Oscillation was first compellingly demonstrated by the Super-Kamiokande collaboration in 1998, using neutrinos produced by cosmic-ray showers in Earth's upper atmosphere — a result that earned Takaaki Kajita the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2015 alongside Arthur McDonald of Canada's Sudbury Neutrino Observatory.

The quantum mechanics of oscillation reveals that neutrinos have mass, and it pins down the differences between the squared masses of the three mass eigenstates (designated ν₁, ν₂, ν₃). Solar neutrino measurements established that ν₂ is heavier than ν₁ — that much is settled. What remains unknown is whether ν₃ is the heaviest of the three (Normal Ordering, or NO) or the lightest (Inverted Ordering, or IO). The question matters beyond bookkeeping: the mass ordering determines the minimum possible neutrino mass, governs how future neutrinoless double-beta decay experiments probe whether neutrinos are their own antiparticles (Majorana fermions), and constrains theoretical models of how neutrinos got their masses in the first place.

The reason this question has resisted resolution for so long is partly experimental and partly structural. Measuring the mass ordering from atmospheric neutrinos requires detecting a subtle asymmetry produced by the Mikheyev–Smirnov–Wolfenstein (MSW) effect: as neutrinos travel through Earth's interior at energies above several GeV, they interact with electrons in the mantle through the weak charged current in a way that muon and tau neutrinos do not. For Normal Ordering, this creates a resonance enhancement for neutrinos (not antineutrinos); for Inverted Ordering, the roles are reversed. The signal is statistically weak in any one experiment — which is why no single detector has yet reached the 5σ threshold conventionally required for discovery in particle physics.

Four Experiments, One Model
Eller's analysis draws on publicly released data from four sources, each archived in an open repository.

Super-Kamiokande (Super-K) is a 50,000-metric-ton (55,116-US-ton) water Cherenkov detector located 1,000 meters (3,281 feet) beneath Mount Ikeno in Japan, operated by the University of Tokyo's Institute for Cosmic Ray Research. It has been running since 1996, and the dataset used here spans its Phases I through V — 6,511 days of exposure totaling 484 kiloton-years. Super-K's strength is its breadth: it detects atmospheric neutrinos across a wide energy range and is the only experiment in this analysis with meaningful sensitivity to the CP-violating phase δ_CP. In recent years it has added gadolinium to its ultrapure water, enabling it to tag neutrons produced in antineutrino interactions, which improves its ability to distinguish matter from antimatter events.

IceCube-DeepCore is a specialized sub-array of the IceCube Neutrino Observatory, buried 2,450 meters (8,038 feet) beneath the Antarctic ice at the geographic South Pole. DeepCore uses the ancient, optically clear Antarctic ice as its Cherenkov medium, with closely spaced strings of optical modules tuned to detect atmospheric neutrinos in the multi-GeV energy range where matter effects are most pronounced. The dataset used here represents 7.5 years of effective lifetime — 21,914 events across 200 analysis bins, drawn from the IceCube-DeepCore oscillation analysis published in 2023. As of early 2026, the IceCube Upgrade — five to seven additional instrumented strings with more than 600 new and enhanced photodetectors — was successfully deployed beneath Antarctic ice, marking IceCube's first significant hardware expansion since its completion in 2011.

KM3NeT/ORCA (Oscillation Research with Cosmics in the Abyss) sits 2,450 meters (8,038 feet) beneath the Mediterranean Sea off the coast of Toulon, France. ORCA's first configuration of six detection lines provided the dataset used here — 510 days and 433 kiloton-years of exposure, as reported in the KM3NeT/ORCA oscillation paper published in JHEP in 2024. ORCA has since grown to 38 detection lines and is continuing to expand, making it one of the fastest-growing detectors in atmospheric neutrino physics.

Daya Bay, a Chinese reactor neutrino experiment that completed its operational run in 2020, measured antineutrinos from six nuclear reactors at kilometer-scale baselines near Shenzhen. Its completed dataset of 5.55 million candidate events over 3,158 days provides the world's most precise measurement of the reactor mixing angle θ₁₃, as documented in the Daya Bay final oscillation results. Because Daya Bay's neutrinos are produced and detected through entirely different physical processes than atmospheric neutrinos, it shares no systematic uncertainties with the other three experiments — making it a clean, independent constraint rather than a correlated one.

What Did It Take to Combine Them?
Why Experts Said This Was Eventually Unfeasible Outside Collaborations
The conventional wisdom Eller overturned rested on a real problem: each experiment has its own detector simulation framework, its own reconstruction algorithms, and its own systematic uncertainties that are characterized internally over years of calibration work. Reproducing another experiment's analysis from the outside requires modeling all of that without access to the raw data or proprietary code.

Eller's solution was to build a fully forward physics model — computing expected event rates from first principles rather than trying to replicate each experiment's internal pipeline. The model draws on Honda et al.'s atmospheric neutrino flux tables (with 12 nuisance parameters for flux shape uncertainties), propagates oscillation probabilities through Earth's density profile using the Preliminary Reference Earth Model, and folds in neutrino interaction cross-sections. For Super-Kamiokande, the public data release provides summary statistics in two-dimensional bins rather than full detector response functions, so Eller reconstructed the smearing matrices — the mathematical description of how true neutrino properties are blurred by imperfect reconstruction — from two-dimensional log-normal and von-Mises-Fisher distributions. Fifty bins at very low reconstructed momentum, which showed unacceptable residuals in this external model, were excluded from the Super-K contribution.

Because Super-K uses the NEUT neutrino interaction code while DeepCore and ORCA use GENIE, the analysis reweights the latter two experiments to a common NEUT expectation, accounting for differences across five charged-current interaction categories plus neutral-current interactions — adding 21 cross-section nuisance parameters to the fit.

The full combined fit optimizes 91 parameters over 1,536 bins and 839,048 events simultaneously, as detailed in the published Eller analysis. The code — called Newtrinos.jl, implemented in the Julia programming language — is fully auto-differentiable, which means gradient descent optimization is available across the entire parameter space. This is not an incidental feature: standard sampling-based Bayesian inference at 91 dimensions would be computationally prohibitive. The framework uses the BAT.jl library and the entire codebase has been released on Newtrinos.jl GitHub repository under an open-source license.

What Did It Actually Find?
Results: No Tensions, and a 3-Sigma NMO Preference
The headline finding is consistency: no significant tension exists between any of the four datasets when fit with a single set of oscillation and physics parameters. The goodness-of-fit for each experiment degrades only modestly when moving from individual to combined fits — from 15.9% to 14.2% for Super-K, from 28.4% to 21.1% for DeepCore — indicating the underlying physics model can simultaneously describe all four experiments without forcing any into internal contradiction.

The combined best-fit values (assuming Normal Ordering) per the Eller Physical Review D paper are:

Parameter	Combined Best Fit	±1σ
δ_CP (rad)	3.78	+0.89 / −0.88
θ₁₃ (rad)	0.149	±0.0027
θ₂₃ (rad)	0.785	+0.032 / −0.041
Δm²₃₁ (10⁻³ eV²)	2.51	+0.046 / −0.044
The θ₁₃ measurement — dominated by Daya Bay — has achieved sub-2% precision. The atmospheric mass splitting Δm²₃₁ reaches roughly 1.8% precision in the combined fit, tighter than any individual experiment in the sample.

The Normal Ordering preference, expressed as the Δχ² between the best-fitting Inverted and Normal Ordering solutions, reaches 9.11 in the combined analysis — corresponding to approximately 3σ significance. The individual contributions are: 5.27 from Super-K alone, 1.55 from DeepCore, and 0.78 from ORCA. These add coherently — all three atmospheric experiments individually favor the same ordering — rather than partially canceling.

Does This Answer the Question?
Why 3-Sigma Is Not Discovery — and Why It Still Matters
Reaching 3σ is significant; reaching 5σ — the threshold for a formal discovery claim in particle physics — is what the field is waiting for. Dedicated experiments are still needed to get there. JUNO (Jiangmen Underground Neutrino Observatory), which began taking data in August 2025 and published its first oscillation results in Nature in June 2026, is the primary reactor-based NMO experiment. Its first 59 days of data achieved world-leading precision on θ₁₂ and Δm²₂₁ but have not yet produced an NMO determination. DUNE, the Deep Underground Neutrino Experiment at Fermilab, targets first beam in 2031 and would provide independent NMO sensitivity from accelerator neutrinos.

But the 3σ result from Eller's atmospheric combination matters for two reasons distinct from the NMO question itself.

First, it is independent of the deepening confusion in the accelerator sector. T2K and NOvA, the two leading accelerator-based oscillation experiments, individually prefer Normal Ordering — but when their results are combined, they actually prefer Inverted Ordering due to their disagreement on the CP-violating phase δ_CP, as reported in their joint oscillation analysis in Nature. This paradox — two experiments that each prefer Normal separately but together prefer Inverted — makes an independent atmospheric measurement especially valuable. Atmospheric detectors derive their NMO sensitivity from a completely different physical mechanism (matter effects in Earth's interior) and are structurally independent of the CP-phase ambiguities that complicate the accelerator-reactor combination.

Second, the combined atmospheric result demonstrated that 3σ significance is reachable from existing public data — not from a future dedicated megaexperiment. As the IceCube Upgrade comes online with its additional photodetectors, as ORCA expands from 6 to 38 detection lines, and as Super-K releases richer and more complete datasets including gadolinium-tagged antineutrino information, a future iteration of this same combined analysis could push the significance substantially further without requiring any new hardware to be built.

Was This Result Already Known?
How Does This Compare to Global Fits That Include JUNO?
One productive coincidence: the NuFIT group, which operates the leading global oscillation fitter and incorporates data from reactors, solar experiments, and atmospheric detectors, independently found in an April 2026 paper that combining JUNO's first data with Super-K and IceCube-24 atmospheric data produces a combined NMO preference of Δχ² = 9.4, as detailed in Lessons from the first JUNO results. Eller's result — Δχ² = 9.11 from atmospheric+Daya Bay, without JUNO — lands almost identically despite using a different experimental combination and an entirely independent analysis approach. That convergence is not coincidental; it reflects a physical truth about where the world's oscillation data collectively points.

Open Data as Multiplier: What This Result Proves
The Eller analysis would not exist without a deliberate data-sharing decision by four separate experimental collaborations — institutions operating in Japan, Antarctica, the Mediterranean, and China — to release their results as publicly reusable archives. Super-K's data lives on Zenodo. IceCube-DeepCore's data lives on the Harvard Dataverse. KM3NeT/ORCA's data sits in the KM3NeT collaboration's repository. Daya Bay's data appeared as supplementary material to its final published results.

The scientific return from those four decisions, taken individually, is significant. Taken collectively, it is something qualitatively different — because the combined analysis enables measurements that none of the four experiments could have produced from its own data alone. Super-K individually produces Δχ² = 5.27 for the NMO. DeepCore adds 1.55. ORCA adds 0.78. The sum is 7.6. But the combined result is 9.11 — exceeding the simple sum because the four datasets constrain shared parameters (atmospheric flux, cross-sections) jointly rather than separately, tightening each individual experiment's effective constraint in the process.

This is the argument for richer data releases, stated in measurement units: the combined result is better than the sum of its parts. A 2019 perspective piece in Nature Physics noted that open data in physics is a necessary but not sufficient condition for reuse — the data must be usable, documented, and released in a form that enables independent analysis. The Super-K release's current form — summary statistics rather than full detector response matrices — already forced Eller to make modeling approximations and exclude 50 bins. A more complete release could sharpen the result further and enable analyses the current release does not support.

What Does This Mean for Neutrino Physics' Next Five Years?
The combined analysis points toward an accelerating path to NMO resolution. Three of the four experiments in Eller's analysis are actively expanding their capabilities. The IceCube Upgrade — installed during the 2025–2026 Antarctic summer — adds more than 600 new and enhanced light sensors, dramatically improving reconstruction and selection efficiency at GeV energies, according to the IceCube Upgrade physics potential paper. KM3NeT/ORCA now encompasses 38 detection lines, up from the 6 used in this analysis. Super-K is operating with gadolinium loading for the first time, with datasets expected to include more powerful antineutrino tagging.

A future iteration of the Eller analysis — using the larger, better-calibrated datasets these upgraded instruments will produce — could plausibly reach 5σ before JUNO or DUNE deliver their primary NMO results. That would represent a decisive demonstration that the atmospheric route to mass ordering is not merely a complement to the reactor and accelerator approaches but a competitive one in its own right.

The deeper structural point is that this result was produced by one researcher working outside all four collaborations, funded by the German Research Foundation, using only data that the collaborations chose to make public. The physics output is competitive with internal collaboration analyses. That is not a critique of collaboration science — collaborations produce the detectors, the calibrations, and the data releases without which none of this would exist. It is an argument that those public releases carry more scientific value than they are sometimes credited with, and that the return on open-data investment in physics is measurable, not merely aspirational.

Frequently Asked Questions
What does the neutrino mass ordering result mean, and how certain is it?
The 3σ preference for Normal Ordering (the configuration in which the third neutrino mass eigenstate is the heaviest, not the lightest) means there is roughly a 1-in-740 chance that a statistical fluctuation in the data could produce this strong a preference if the Inverted Ordering were actually correct. In particle physics, 3σ is called "evidence" — strong enough to report and publish, but not yet at the 5σ "discovery" threshold. Dedicated experiments including JUNO and eventually DUNE are still required to cross that line definitively.

Why couldn't a single experiment already answer this question on its own?
Each individual atmospheric neutrino detector lacks sufficient statistical power: Super-Kamiokande alone reaches Δχ² = 5.27, IceCube-DeepCore adds 1.55, and KM3NeT/ORCA contributes 0.78. Only by combining all three atmospheric datasets with Daya Bay's precisely measured reactor constraints does the total reach Δχ² = 9.11 — exceeding the simple sum of parts because the joint fit tightens shared systematic uncertainties simultaneously. No single experiment can perform this joint constraint on itself.

How did a solo researcher achieve what major collaborations said was unfeasible?
Eller's key methodological insight was building a unified forward physics model — computing expected event rates from atmospheric neutrino flux calculations, oscillation probabilities including matter effects in Earth, and cross-section libraries — that could simultaneously describe all four detectors. Rather than trying to reproduce each experiment's internal simulation chain (which requires proprietary code and raw data), he parameterized the smearing between true and reconstructed neutrino properties analytically, using publicly released summary statistics as input. The analysis is implemented in Julia and is fully open-source, meaning any researcher can repeat or extend it.

What does this result imply for experiments like JUNO and DUNE?
Those experiments remain essential to reaching the 5σ discovery threshold. JUNO, operating in southern China since August 2025, uses reactor antineutrinos and will deliver its NMO determination with approximately 3 years of data. DUNE, targeting first beam in 2031, will measure the NMO via accelerator neutrinos with independent sensitivity to the CP-violating phase δ_CP. The Eller result demonstrates that the atmospheric route is a competitive, independent cross-check — if the atmospheric combination reaches 5σ before JUNO or DUNE, it would represent a genuine surprise and reshape how the community prioritizes future investments in atmospheric versus dedicated NMO experiments.

What Is DPF 2026 — and Why Does It Happen at Fermilab?

The conference alternates between universities and national laboratories; hosting it at Fermilab this cycle reflects the lab's centrality to the field's near-term future. Why Matter Exists: DUNE's Actual Scientific Mission
Of all the open questions in physics, few are more fundamental — or more consequential for the existence of anything — than why the observable universe contains matter. Under the laws of physics as currently understood, the Big Bang should have produced equal amounts of matter and antimatter. When matter and antimatter meet, they annihilate, leaving only energy. A universe born from a symmetric explosion should have no stars, no planets, no atoms, and no physicists asking the question. The fact that matter dominates the observable universe by an enormous margin is one of the deepest unexplained facts in all of science.

In 1967, Soviet physicist Andrei Sakharov identified three conditions necessary for matter to have survived: violation of baryon number conservation, departure from thermal equilibrium, and violation of CP symmetry — the combined symmetry that states the laws of physics should look the same if you swap all particles for antiparticles (charge conjugation, C) and mirror all spatial coordinates (parity, P). CP violation has been confirmed in several particle systems: in neutral kaon decays (Nobel Prize 1980), in B-meson systems (BaBar and Belle, 2001), and as recently as 2025 in baryon decays via the LHCb experiment at CERN. But the CP violation that has been measured so far falls short — by orders of magnitude — of what would be needed to explain the observed matter-antimatter asymmetry of the universe. A leading theoretical candidate for the missing source is CP violation in the neutrino sector, a phenomenon that has never been directly observed.

DUNE is designed to observe it. The Deep Underground Neutrino Experiment will fire a beam of neutrinos — and a separate beam of antineutrinos — 800 miles (1,300 km) from Fermilab through the earth to liquid-argon detectors being constructed 1.5 km underground at the Sanford Underground Research Facility in Lead, South Dakota, per the DUNE science program. The physics is contained in the oscillation: neutrinos shift between flavor states (electron, muon, tau) as they travel, and the rates of that shift differ between neutrinos and antineutrinos if leptonic CP violation exists. The parameter encoding that difference — called δ_CP (delta CP) — is DUNE's primary leptonic CP violation measurement target. Precisely measuring δ_CP would reveal whether leptonic CP violation exists and, if so, whether it is large enough to support leptogenesis — the theoretical mechanism by which an excess of matter could have been generated in the early universe. DUNE is, in other words, the experiment most likely to tell us why matter exists.

PIP-II: The Accelerator That Will Power DUNE
In a significant recent milestone, Fermilab announced on July 9, 2026 that crews had successfully installed the first major beamline component in the tunnel that will house the PIP-II superconducting linear accelerator — the machine that will eventually drive the world's most intense neutrino beam toward DUNE's South Dakota detectors.

The installed component is a radio-frequency quadrupole, or RFQ — a 4-meter-long copper device that handles what PIP-II's linac installation manager Curt Baffes described as "one of the trickiest regimes, where the beam's energy is low and the velocity is changing a lot." The RFQ is the point at which protons first enter the acceleration chain, before any of the superconducting structures take over. Because it is the largest single component in the warm front end of the linac, it had to be placed first to preserve maximum flexibility for what follows. During the installation, the RFQ was transported at a deliberate 5 miles per hour (8 km/h) to prevent damage before being craned 30 feet (9 m) down to the tunnel level and wheeled into position, per the Fermilab press release.

PIP-II — the Proton Improvement Plan II — is the first particle accelerator in the United States built with significant contributions from international partners, with institutions in France, India, Italy, Poland, and the United Kingdom contributing technologies and expertise. The completed linac will be 215 meters long, containing 23 superconducting cryomodule accelerating structures, and will bring H-minus ions to 800 million electron volts (MeV) using superconducting radio-frequency (SRF) technology.

SRF is why PIP-II represents a qualitative leap beyond conventional accelerator design. Normal-conducting RF structures generate heat as electric current flows through metallic walls — the same physics as a resistance heater. That heat limits how hard you can push the accelerating field and requires constant power input to sustain. Superconducting cavities cooled to near absolute zero eliminate electrical resistance entirely, achieving quality factors — a measure of how efficiently a resonator stores energy — of approximately 5 × 10¹⁰ for niobium cavities at 1.8 Kelvin. The result: particle beams can be accelerated to higher energies with far less power lost as waste heat, and SRF is the only technology compatible with the continuous-wave operation that the LBNF neutrino beam's design requires.

Teams will spend the rest of 2026 interfacing the RFQ with water-cooling, high-power radio-frequency, and vacuum systems. First beam commissioning is expected to begin in 2027. PIP-II Project Director Cristian Boffo placed the installation in institutional context: "2026 marks a significant milestone for the PIP-II project. As we accelerate cryomodule production across the United States, U.K. and France, our team is beginning installation of the warm front end in the newly completed facility." Steve Dixon, PIP-II's conventional facilities manager, noted the generational scale: "The last time we built a linac was in the 1960s, and it served the lab for all of this time. What we're building now will set Fermilab up for the next 50 to 60 years," per the Fermilab installation announcement.

Steel Underground: DUNE's South Dakota Detector Takes Shape
Meanwhile, construction at the South Dakota end of the experiment has been accelerating on its own schedule. In May 2026, Fermilab and the Sanford Underground Research Facility (SURF) celebrated the start of 10 million pounds of steel beams being lowered 1 mile (1.6 km) underground to form the structural skeleton of DUNE's far detectors. When complete, those structures will hold the first two DUNE far detector modules — each containing 17 kilotons of ultra-pure liquid argon cooled to approximately -186°C (-303°F) and maintained in continuous electric fields.

The liquid-argon time projection chamber (LArTPC) technology used in DUNE works because charged particles traversing the liquid argon ionize atoms along their path, freeing electrons that drift through the electric field to wire readout planes at one end of the chamber. The drift velocity, arrival time, and spatial pattern of those electrons allow reconstruction of the full three-dimensional trajectory of any particle that entered the detector. At DUNE's scale — and with DUNE's 800-mile baseline — the technology is sensitive to the sub-percent asymmetries in neutrino vs. antineutrino oscillation rates that would signal δ_CP differs from zero. Fermilab's top institutional priority is delivering first neutrino beam to DUNE by 2031.

What AI Is Doing to Particle Physics Itself

The talk argued that AI offers the opportunity to "scale the field" by synthesizing multidisciplinary ideas, while cautioning that the role of the physicist must evolve to steer progress in this new computational landscape, per the DPF 2026 timetable.

This is not aspirational rhetoric. Fermilab's Fermi Data Platform is already providing data infrastructure for the DOE's broader Genesis Mission in AI-driven scientific discovery. For ATLAS at the LHC, the imminent transition to the High-Luminosity LHC demands, in the words of a DPF 2026 parallel session abstract, "a step-change in throughput, scalability, and flexibility of its core computing infrastructure" — a step change being addressed largely through machine learning pipelines and field-programmable gate arrays capable of making event-classification decisions at nanosecond timescales, as detailed in the DPF 2026 sessions. The full HL-LHC program will generate more than five billion particle interactions per second at peak luminosity, requiring ML-based triggers to reject approximately 99.9% of events in real time before any data reaches permanent storage.

What Scientists Are Searching for in the Dark
The Cosmic Frontier and Dark Sector parallel track at DPF 2026 features a wide range of experiments designed to detect matter that neither emits nor reflects light — but that is known from its gravitational influence to constitute approximately 27% of the universe's total mass-energy content.

SENSEI (Sub-Electron Noise Skipper Experimental Instrument), operating at Fermilab's underground facility and at SNOLAB in Canada, is pushing the frontier of sub-GeV dark matter detection using a silicon Skipper-CCD with single-electron counting capability. The key innovation is a readout technique that measures the charge in each pixel non-destructively, many times in sequence, and averages the results to achieve noise levels below a single electron. This extraordinary sensitivity allows SENSEI to detect nuclear recoils from dark matter particles with masses as light as a few MeV/c² — candidates far lighter than the Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs) that were long considered the most natural dark matter explanation.

LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ), operating at the Sanford Underground Research Facility — the same site as DUNE's far detectors — uses a dual-phase xenon time projection chamber. WIMP scattering in the liquid xenon produces two signals: a prompt scintillation flash (S1) when the interaction occurs, and a delayed electroluminescence signal (S2) when the liberated electrons drift to the gas phase above the liquid. The ratio of S1 to S2, combined with the event's three-dimensional position, allows discrimination of genuine WIMP interactions from radioactive background events. LZ has set world-leading limits on spin-independent WIMP-nucleon scattering for WIMP masses above 5 GeV/c².

A DPF 2026 contribution from the University of Chicago and Fermilab explored how entangled GHZ (Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger) quantum states can achieve quadratic signal scaling in wave-like dark matter searches — a quantum sensing approach that outperforms classical cavity setups in sensitivity per unit time for ultralight dark matter candidates, per the DPF 2026 program.

Detector Hardware at the HL-LHC Frontier
As the LHC's Long Shutdown 3 upgrade — begun June 29 — proceeds at CERN, DPF 2026 parallel sessions are covering the U.S. contributions to the High-Luminosity LHC detector upgrades in technical depth, building on what TechTimes reported in our LHC Long Shutdown 3 coverage.

Among the most technically demanding contributions is the CMS MIP Timing Detector (MTD), which will enable four-dimensional tracking by providing per-particle timestamps. Within the MTD, the Endcap Timing Layer (ETL) uses Low Gain Avalanche Diodes (LGADs) — thin silicon sensors with a thin gain layer just below the surface that multiplies the ionization signal by a factor of roughly 10 to 50, enabling time resolution of 30 to 60 picoseconds per particle per layer, as presented in the DPF 2026 detector sessions. The readout chip for these sensors, ETROC2, is a custom 65-nanometer CMOS application-specific integrated circuit; its characterization and production readiness were confirmed at DPF 2026. Separately, work on the CMS Phase-2 High-Granularity Calorimeter upgrade at Fermilab was also featured.

At ATLAS, the HL-LHC transition demands a fundamental rethinking of computing infrastructure. Between 140 and 200 proton-proton collisions will overlap in every bunch crossing at design luminosity — "pile-up" that produces data rates far beyond any classical processing scheme. The computing challenge is addressed through a new two-level trigger architecture combining hardware ML classification at microsecond latency with software high-level triggers that reduce the event rate further before permanent storage.

Observing a Cosmic Frontier in Real Time
Thursday's CMB and Cosmic Surveys plenary is scheduled to feature two dedicated talks: CMB Experiments, presented by Eve Vavagiakis of Cornell University, and Cosmic Surveys, presented by Chihway Chang of the Kavli Institute for Cosmological Physics — talks that will arrive in the immediate wake of perhaps the most significant week for observational cosmology in years, per the DPF 2026 timetable.

The Simons Observatory (SO), high on Chile's Atacama Plateau, has commissioned both its Large Aperture Telescope — which achieved first light in February 2025 and is now mapping half the sky at arcminute resolution in six frequency channels — and its Small Aperture Telescopes, which probe the primordial gravitational wave signal encoded in CMB B-mode polarization. The South Pole Telescope (SPT-3G) program presented CMB lensing measurements from its D1 dataset, covering 1,500 square degrees of the 2019–2020 observing seasons and producing a lensing potential map dominated by polarization information.

Most immediately, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory launched its 10-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time on June 30, 2026 — one day before DPF 2026 opened. From its mountaintop in Chile's Atacama Desert, Rubin's 3,200-megapixel camera will image the entire accessible southern sky every few nights for a decade, generating approximately 20 terabytes of data per night and issuing an estimated seven million automated transient alerts each night via SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory's processing pipeline. Already, in six weeks of pre-survey optimization operations, the telescope discovered more than 11,000 previously uncatalogued asteroids, including 33 near-Earth objects. Rubin's highest-priority cosmological goal is a weak gravitational lensing survey — mapping the subtle distortion of background galaxy shapes by foreground dark matter to produce the most detailed map of the universe's mass distribution ever assembled.

The Radio Neutrino Observatory in Greenland (RNO-G), the first ice-based ultra-high-energy neutrino detector in the Northern Hemisphere, is also represented at DPF 2026. RNO-G uses the Askaryan effect: ultra-high-energy neutrino interactions in ice produce particle showers that generate radio-frequency coherent emission detectable by antennas buried in the Antarctic-analogous Greenland ice sheet. With eight of its 35 planned stations installed at NSF's Summit Station, RNO-G is pursuing neutrinos above 10¹⁶ electron volts — energies no accelerator can produce and that can only be generated by astrophysical sources.

Neutrino Results Incoming, From JUNO to Fermilab's Short-Baseline Program
Thursday's dedicated neutrino results plenary is scheduled to include two specific talks: results from JUNO (the Jiangmen Underground Neutrino Observatory in China), to be presented by Sindhujha Kumaran of UC Irvine, and results from the short-baseline oscillation experiments at Fermilab, to be presented by Justin Mueller of Fermilab, per the DPF 2026 timetable.

JUNO is a 20-kiloton liquid-scintillator detector collecting reactor antineutrinos and atmospheric neutrinos in southern China. Its primary goal is determining the neutrino mass ordering — whether the third, heaviest neutrino mass state is lighter than the other two (normal ordering) or heavier (inverted ordering). This question is also in DUNE's scientific portfolio, making JUNO and DUNE scientific competitors on the same measurement, with JUNO pursuing it first through a different experimental technique on a different baseline.

The Fermilab short-baseline program — comprising MicroBooNE, ICARUS, and SBND — has been pursuing anomalous oscillation signals first seen at the MiniBooNE experiment, searching for evidence of a hypothetical fourth ("sterile") neutrino flavor. Resolving this anomaly has been a priority of the Fermilab short-baseline neutrino program for years and remains scientifically open.

Wednesday's Funding Forum: The Politics of Particle Physics
Wednesday's forum-style sessions brought DOE and NSF representatives into direct dialogue with the DPF 2026 scientific community. Gina Rameika, Associate Director for High Energy Physics at the DOE Office of Science, was confirmed as a participant, per the DPF 2026 conference program. Rameika came to DOE from Fermilab in November 2022, where she spent most of her career in neutrino physics, including contributions to the DONUT experiment, and received the 2022 APS Panofsky Prize.

The funding context is challenging. In March 2026, the DOE merged its separate nuclear physics and high-energy physics programs into a single combined program with a budget of $2.101 billion — a structural change that has raised concerns in the high-energy physics community about competition between the Electron-Ion Collider program and LBNF/DUNE, which at its $3.4 billion total project cost is the largest U.S. science project supported by the DOE Office of Science. In the United Kingdom, a funding restructuring at UK Research and Innovation's Science and Technology Facilities Council announced in late March 2026 outlined approximately a 70% reduction in theoretical particle physics postdoctoral positions over the next four years — a development affecting roughly 45 researchers and drawing concern from the international community that attends DPF in significant numbers.

How Does the Field Know What Higgs Bosons Are Doing?
The Energy Frontier parallel track covers Higgs boson physics alongside other high-energy collider results. A contributed talk addresses new Higgs results from CMS, including work on non-resonant associated production of a Higgs pair alongside a top quark-antiquark pair — a topology where both Higgs bosons decay to bottom quark-antiquark pairs, per the DPF 2026 timetable. This process is a direct probe of the Higgs self-coupling, the parameter that determines the shape of the Higgs field potential and whether the electroweak vacuum is truly stable. Measuring it requires the kind of integrated luminosity that only the HL-LHC's decade of operation will provide, but the analysis methodologies being developed and tested now at the LHC will directly enable those measurements.

What Are the Options for Physics After the HL-LHC?
The "Future of HEP" plenary panel on Friday is the conference's culminating event and arrives in an unusually concrete strategic context. In May 2026 in Budapest, the CERN Council unanimously adopted an updated European Strategy for Particle Physics recommending the electron-positron Future Circular Collider (FCC-ee) as the preferred successor to the LHC — a 91-kilometer ring that would first operate as a precision "Higgs factory" and later potentially as a proton-proton collider at energies exceeding 200 TeV. A final decision on FCC-ee construction is expected around 2028, pending a proposal from CERN to member governments. The FCC-ee would require resources beyond CERN's current operating budget, and its estimated cost runs into the tens of billions of dollars.

The U.S. physics community's own strategic roadmap, produced by the Particle Physics Project Prioritization Panel (P5) in 2023, endorsed DUNE/LBNF and HL-LHC participation as the near-term foundation while calling for aggressive R&D toward a muon collider as the U.S.-hosted energy-frontier option — what the P5 explicitly called the country's "muon shot." A muon collider at 10 TeV center-of-mass energy could, in principle, fit within Fermilab's existing site, whereas a proton collider with equivalent physics reach would require a tunnel tens of kilometers longer. The U.S. Muon Collider community, coordinated through a dedicated group with Fermilab and university participation, has been submitting to the 2026 European Strategy process and held its inaugural meeting in August 2024. An active debate about the relative scientific and cost merits of the FCC-ee, a muon collider, and other options will run through Friday's panel.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the DUNE experiment actually trying to find?
DUNE is designed to measure leptonic CP violation — whether neutrinos and antineutrinos oscillate between flavors at different rates. If the asymmetry is large enough, it would support leptogenesis, the theoretical mechanism by which the early universe could have generated more matter than antimatter. DUNE is also sensitive to the neutrino mass ordering and to exotic signatures from beyond-Standard-Model physics including proton decay and supernovae neutrino bursts.

Why is the PIP-II accelerator milestone significant for DUNE?
PIP-II is the accelerator that will eventually power the LBNF neutrino beam to DUNE's South Dakota detectors. The July 9 installation of the radio-frequency quadrupole (RFQ) — the first major beamline component placed in the new tunnel — marks the beginning of the linac's installation phase, a process expected to continue through 2026 and into beam commissioning in 2027. PIP-II must be complete before the world's most intense neutrino beam can reach DUNE's far detectors; Fermilab's stated target for first beam is 2031.

What does the Future Circular Collider decision mean for U.S. physics?
The CERN Council's endorsement of the FCC-ee in May 2026 represents a European commitment to build a next-generation collider in Switzerland — a decision with direct implications for where the global physics community invests over the next 40 years. The U.S. strategy, by contrast, centers on a potential muon collider at Fermilab, which would give the U.S. an independent energy-frontier capability. DPF 2026's Friday panel will address whether the two strategies are complementary or competing, and what federal investment is required to keep the U.S. at the table.

What did Rubin Observatory discover in its first weeks of operation?
In approximately six weeks of pre-survey optimization operations before the Legacy Survey of Space and Time officially launched on June 30, 2026, Rubin discovered more than 11,000 previously uncatalogued asteroids — including 33 near-Earth objects and 380 trans-Neptunian objects beyond Neptune's orbit. The full LSST survey, now underway, will image the entire accessible southern sky every few nights for a decade, producing more astronomical data than all prior observatories combined and generating the most detailed map yet of how dark matter is distributed across the universe.

Neutrino Fog Confirmed: LZ and XENONnT Prove Dark Matter Detectors Now Test Electroweak Physics
Back-to-back PRL papers show 93% more exposure yields 10% sensitivity gain; fog enables weak-force tests
By Clark Johnson
Published: Aug 31 2026, 6:56 AM EDT
Share on Facebook
Share on Twitter
Share on LinkedIn

LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ) Experiment
Lz.lbl.gov
The underground labs built to catch dark matter have discovered something they were not designed to find: that the very particles blocking their view of dark matter are now measuring fundamental constants of the Standard Model. On August 28, 2026, the LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ) and XENONnT collaborations published simultaneous companion letters in Physical Review Letters — confirming that liquid-xenon detectors have entered the "neutrino fog," the regime where solar neutrinos impose a hard ceiling on dark matter sensitivity, and demonstrating for the first time that this fog is itself a physics instrument.

LZ's result, first presented publicly in December 2025 and now published as the journal of record, stands as the strongest evidence yet that boron-8 solar neutrinos interact with xenon nuclei via coherent elastic scattering — at 4.5 sigma statistical significance, the threshold particle physicists formally call "evidence." The companion XENONnT paper, published simultaneously, adds a finding with long-term consequences for every dark matter experiment on Earth: after nearly doubling its data sample, XENONnT's sensitivity to the most promising class of dark matter candidates improved by only 10 percent. That number — 93 percent more exposure, 10 percent more power — is the empirical proof that the field has crossed a threshold it can no longer engineer its way past without a fundamentally different approach.

What the Neutrino Fog Actually Means for Future Experiments
For decades, dark matter detectors improved by a simple formula: more xenon, more running time, better sensitivity. That relationship is now broken for the most contested region of dark matter parameter space.


The obstacle is coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering (CEvNS) — a process predicted theoretically in 1974 by physicist Daniel Freedman and first observed experimentally in 2017 by the COHERENT collaboration at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. When a boron-8 neutrino produced in the Sun's core passes through the Earth and strikes a xenon nucleus, the entire nucleus recoils as a single unit — not one proton or neutron, but all of them together, absorbing momentum coherently. The energy deposited is tiny, in the 1–6 keV range, and it is physically indistinguishable, event by event, from the nuclear recoil a dark matter particle would cause in the same mass range physicists most want to probe: roughly 3–9 times the mass of a proton.

Unlike radioactive contamination — which engineers can reduce through purer materials, better shielding, and cleaner construction — solar neutrinos pass through the Earth as if it were not there. No engineering eliminates them. No shielding stops them. The Sun produces approximately 5 × 10⁶ per square centimeter per second of the relevant boron-8 variety, a flux that will not decline on any timescale relevant to laboratory physics.

The quantitative consequence, documented by XENONnT, is stark. The collaboration used 6.77 tonne-years of data from three consecutive science runs — a 93 percent increase over its prior analysis. For a 5 GeV/c² spin-independent dark matter candidate (roughly the mass of five protons), median sensitivity improved by just 10 percent.

Read more: XENONnT Sets World Records Hunting Axions and Dark Photons Below WIMP Barrier

The physics behind this number is the replacement of linear scaling by square-root scaling. Before the neutrino fog, running longer and collecting more data improved sensitivity roughly proportional to exposure. Inside the fog, where the CEvNS background has the same energy spectrum as the dark matter signal being sought, statistical separation of two look-alike populations improves only as the square root of the number of events — so doubling the data yields only about 40 percent more statistical power, and that gain is further constrained by the spectral degeneracy between signal and background. The result is shrinking returns, empirically confirmed for the first time by XENONnT's own data.

LZ's 4.5 Sigma Detection: What the Number Means
LZ's paper, accepted by Physical Review Letters on July 16, 2026, and now published in the journal of record, uses 5.7 tonne-years of data collected between March 2023 and April 2025. In the 1–6 keV energy window where boron-8 CEvNS events are expected, the collaboration found no significant excess attributable to dark matter nuclear recoils — but found exactly the CEvNS solar neutrino signal the Standard Model predicts, at 4.5 sigma statistical significance.

In particle physics, 3 sigma is the threshold for "evidence" and 5 sigma for "discovery." LZ's 4.5 sigma result sits in the evidence tier — the strongest such evidence ever produced by any liquid-xenon detector for this specific process. Earlier indications at 2–2.7 sigma from PandaX-4T and XENONnT in November 2024 had suggested the signal was there; LZ's result confirms it with greater statistical weight.


On dark matter, the news is a null result — but a record-setting one. LZ now sets world-leading limits on WIMP-nucleon interactions for particle masses down to 5 GeV/c² — roughly five times the proton mass. Every point in that parameter space that LZ's data rules out is a point where dark matter, if it exists, cannot be hiding.

Rick Gaitskell, a professor at Brown University and spokesperson for LZ, noted at the December 2025 presentation that preceded the formal publication that the collaboration had "been able to further increase the incredible sensitivity of the LUX-ZEPLIN detector with this new run and extended analysis."

Dark Matter Detectors Are Now Electroweak Precision Labs
The finding that has received the least attention in coverage of the December 2025 LZ announcement is buried in XENONnT's companion paper: the same boron-8 CEvNS signal that limits the dark matter search has been used to measure a fundamental constant of the Standard Model at an energy scale no collider can easily access.

The quantity is the weak mixing angle — also called the Weinberg angle, or sin²θ_W — a parameter that governs how much the weak nuclear force "mixes" with electromagnetism. The Standard Model running prediction for this parameter shows: its value at the Z boson mass (around 91 GeV) differs from its value at the energy of a stopped-pion beam (~50 MeV), which in turn differs from its value at very low momentum transfer. Confirming this running is a precision test of the theory.

XENONnT's boron-8 CEvNS interactions transfer momentum at approximately 0.02 GeV/c — lower than any stopped-pion experiment and lower than any previous high-statistics electroweak precision measurement. The rate at which boron-8 neutrinos scatter coherently off xenon nuclei depends mathematically on the weak nuclear charge Q_W = N − (1 − 4sin²θ_W)Z, where N and Z are the neutron and proton numbers. By measuring the CEvNS event rate against the known solar neutrino flux, XENONnT extracted a measurement of sin²θ_W at this previously unmeasured momentum transfer, and confirmed consistency with Standard Model predictions.

Prior measurements of the weak mixing angle at low energies came from atomic parity violation experiments (essentially zero momentum transfer), Møller scattering at SLAC, the Qweak experiment using proton weak charge, and CEvNS measurements by COHERENT at ~50 MeV. XENONnT fills a gap between atomic parity violation and stopped-pion-scale experiments — a region where the Standard Model's running prediction can be independently verified and where new physics (non-standard neutrino interactions, additional neutral weak currents) could produce detectable deviations. A broader cross-experiment CEvNS physics analysis underscores the complementarity of these approaches.

The paper also used the dataset to constrain physics beyond the Standard Model more broadly. In one sentence, the XENONnT abstract summarizes a decade of experimental ambition: a detector built to find dark matter has become a precision instrument for Standard Model tests at an energy scale where dedicated electroweak experiments are scarce.

The Choreography Behind the Dual Publication
The pairing of the two letters as consecutive articles in the same journal issue — PRL 137, 091806 (LZ) and PRL 137, 091807 (XENONnT), cross-citing each other — was deliberate. Physics collaborations competing for the same experimental territory have historically raced to publish first, with each result potentially diminishing the apparent novelty of the other. The coordinated simultaneous release avoids that dynamic and presents a unified picture: two independent detector technologies, different underground sites, different collaboration memberships, arriving at consistent results from different angles. Details of the coordinated publication appear in PRL Volume 137, Issue 9.

LZ operates at the Sanford Underground Research Facility (SURF) in Lead, South Dakota, 1,480 meters (4,856 feet) underground. It uses 10 tonnes of liquid xenon in total, with a 5.5-tonne active detection region. XENONnT is located at the Gran Sasso National Laboratory (LNGS) in central Italy, beneath 1,400 meters (4,593 feet) of Apennine rock. Its active xenon volume is 5.9 tonnes. Both collaborations span dozens of universities and national laboratories across North America, Europe, Asia, and Australia.

Physical Review Letters selected LZ's paper as an Editors' Suggestion — a designation the journal reserves for exceptional interest — awarded to a small fraction of published letters judged to be of exceptional interest or significance by the journal's editors.

Where the Search Goes From Here
Both experiments continue to operate. LZ aims to reach a projected spin-independent sensitivity of 3 × 10⁻⁴⁸ cm² for a 40 GeV/c² dark matter candidate over its full planned 1,000 live-day run, expected to conclude around 2028. That run will accumulate more CEvNS events, allowing increasingly precise measurements of the weak mixing angle and solar neutrino properties as the field navigates the fog.

The next-generation successor — XLZD, a proposed 60–80 tonne active xenon observatory being designed as a joint program of the XENON, LZ, and DARWIN collaborations — is explicitly conceived to operate in the neutrino fog era. Its XLZD design book projections assume the fog as a baseline, and it is designed to statistically separate a potential dark matter signal from the CEvNS background using the directional and temporal signatures that current-generation detectors lack the exposure to resolve.

Professor Chamkaur Ghag of University College London, a senior figure in the XLZD planning effort, described the present moment bluntly in an earlier TechTimes report on the XLZD project: "We are entering the last-chance saloon to show that these particles are the cause of dark matter, and we want to make sure Britain is at the heart of that work by building the final generation of these detectors."

That last-chance saloon now comes equipped with electroweak measurement capability that nobody put in the blueprints.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the neutrino fog, and why does it limit dark matter experiments?
The neutrino fog is the region of dark matter parameter space where boron-8 solar neutrinos — produced in the Sun's core and streaming continuously through the Earth — scatter off xenon nuclei through a process called coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering (CEvNS), producing nuclear recoils that are physically identical, event by event, to what a dark matter particle would produce in the same 3–9 GeV/c² mass range. Unlike radioactive contamination, solar neutrinos cannot be shielded, filtered, or chemically removed. The XENONnT paper published August 28, 2026 quantified its effect precisely for the first time: a 93 percent increase in exposure improved sensitivity by only 10 percent. This is the empirical signature of the fog — the point where adding more xenon data stops improving the search linearly and instead degrades into square-root scaling. Future experiments must either grow large enough to statistically disentangle the two populations, or develop directional sensitivity that CEvNS background events lack.

How can dark matter detectors measure a Standard Model constant like the weak mixing angle?
The rate at which solar neutrinos scatter coherently off xenon nuclei depends on a quantity called the weak nuclear charge, which is a function of the weak mixing angle (sin²θ_W) — the parameter governing the electroweak force's mixing at low energies. By observing how many CEvNS events occur and comparing that count to the known solar neutrino flux, XENONnT effectively ran a precision electroweak measurement. Because solar boron-8 neutrinos transfer momentum at roughly 0.02 GeV/c during xenon scatters, this measurement probes the Standard Model's electroweak running at an energy scale below all previous stopped-pion experiments and above atomic parity violation — filling a gap in the precision physics map. The result is consistent with the Standard Model prediction and provides independent constraints on physics beyond the Standard Model, including non-standard neutrino interactions.

If both experiments found no dark matter, what does that tell us about what dark matter might be?
Each null result tightens the constraints on specific theoretical models. LZ's limits now rule out dark matter particles interacting with neutrons at cross-sections above roughly 10⁻⁴⁷ cm² for masses down to 5 GeV/c² — an area of parameter space that simple WIMP models predicted should have been visible. The absence of a signal at that sensitivity means: if dark matter exists and interacts via the weak force, it either has a much smaller coupling than the simplest WIMP models assumed, or it has properties (mass outside the searched range, spin-dependent-only interactions, or entirely different interaction channels) that conventional xenon detectors are not optimally positioned to detect. The two companion papers explicitly acknowledge that the diminishing returns from xenon exposure growth are redirecting the community toward alternative dark matter candidates — axion-like particles, dark photons, and primordial black holes — and toward new detector technologies capable of measuring directionality.

What is the XLZD detector and when will it operate?
XLZD is the proposed next-generation liquid xenon dark matter observatory, currently in the design phase as a joint effort of the XENON, LZ, and DARWIN collaborations. Its active xenon volume is planned at 60–80 tonnes — roughly an order of magnitude larger than either LZ or XENONnT. XLZD is designed specifically to operate inside the neutrino fog: at that scale, the statistical separation of a dark matter signal from the CEvNS solar neutrino background becomes feasible using differences in their spectral shapes and annual modulation patterns. Site selection among SURF (South Dakota), Gran Sasso (Italy), and the Boulby mine (UK) is ongoing. No construction start date has been announced publicly as of August 2026.

Sagittarius A* is a supermassive black hole at the center of our galaxy. Its mass is 4 million times that of the sun; meanwhile the smallest known black hole is only about 3.8 solar masses. And both pale in comparison to TON 618, the biggest black hole researchers have seen at 66 billion times in mass. One misconception about these cosmic bodies having such a strong gravitational pull is that they pull stars and other matter into them. On the contrary, they exert the same gravity as other matter of equal mass. If the sun in our solar system was replaced with a black hole with the same mass, the planets' orbits wouldn't change. The theory about wormholes

Reds and whites used to create the concept of a wormhole in space
Reds and whites used to create the concept of a wormhole in space
© Valentina Shilkina/Shutterstock
When it comes to wormholes, the concept of such a cosmic phenomenon has an interesting evolution. It started with a paper originally published in Physikalische Zeitschrift in 1916 by Austrian physicist Ludwig Flamm, aiming to make theoretical physicist Albert Einstein's and German astronomer Karl Schwarzschild's solutions regarding general relativity and black holes clearer and more accurate. He found another mathematical solution that led to the opposite of a black hole — a theoretical white hole (as it was later termed) that ejects matter instead of absorbing it. With that finding, he proposed the possibility that a black hole is connected to a white hole by a space-time conduit.

Then, in 1935, Einstein and American-Israeli physicist Nathan Rosen published a paper in Physical Review. They proposed the possible existence of a "bridge" — which became the Einstein–Rosen bridge — that stretches across space-time to connect two distant points. However, it wasn't until a 1957 paper in Annals of Physics written by American physicist Charles W. Misner and nuclear physicist John Archibald Wheeler that the term "wormhole" was coined. Flamm's contributions to the scientific community weren't widely appreciated until the images that his work conjured became commonplace in texts related to blackholes, neutron stars, wormholes, and more around the mid-1970s.

Breaking down all the research, the basic understanding is that wormholes could reduce space travel times between galaxies from millions of years to just hours or minutes. Another idea is that they may act like time machines, so you would arrive at point B at a time earlier than when you entered point A. As for whether wormholes could actually exist or not, scientists are divided. Skeptical scientists believe that the bridge's own gravity would make it collapse in the middle, unless enough internal negative energies are present to counteract it.

Most common approaches assume that gravity must also be described using quantum ideas. As physicist Richard Feynman once said, "We're in trouble if we believe in quantum mechanics but don't quantize gravity." Yet quantum theory itself has deep unresolved problems. It does not clearly explain how measurements lead to definite outcomes, and it relies on strange ideas that clash with everyday experience, such as objects seemingly behaving like both waves and particles, and apparent nonlocal connections between distant systems.

These puzzles become even sharper because of Bell's theorem. This theorem shows that no theory based on ordinary ideas—such as locality, an objective reality, and freely chosen measurements—can fully match the predictions of quantum theory within our usual four-dimensional view of space and time. These quantum predictions have been repeatedly confirmed in tests of entanglement, first discussed by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen (EPR). As a result, simple classical explanations limited to ordinary four-dimensional spacetime cannot fully account for what we observe.

These serious conceptual issues raise a fundamental question: Do quantum experiments truly reveal a bizarre universe, or do they suggest that we are interpreting them from the wrong perspective?

A classical theory beyond four dimensions
In a recent work, published in Scientific Reports, I explore a different way of thinking about quantum physics and gravity. Instead of trying to make gravity quantum, I propose that both quantum effects and gravity may arise from a deeper, classical structure that exists in more than four dimensions.

My motivation is straightforward: If Bell's theorem tells us that intuitive, classical explanations of quantum effects cannot work within ordinary four-dimensional space and time, then perhaps the problem lies with spacetime itself. I therefore extend spacetime by adding a fifth dimension that acts as an evolution parameter. This allows familiar four-dimensional spacetime to evolve in a new way and opens up new possibilities for explaining both quantum behavior and gravity using classical ideas.

A central idea of the theory is that particles are not fixed objects from the start. Instead, they are built from paths, called "worldlines," that gradually form as this extra parameter advances. While these worldlines may initially show a variety of dynamics, they slowly "lock in" as the evolution progresses, until a stable, classical world emerges—the world we experience in everyday life. These dynamics are crucial for producing the strange outcomes of quantum mechanics from the perspective of an observer. However, in the deeper five-dimensional picture, the underlying processes remain entirely classical.

To illustrate these concepts, I construct theoretical models that reproduce two famous quantum experiments within the proposed framework. EPR-type correlations arise because influences are now allowed to propagate along worldlines as functions of the additional evolution parameter. Although particles themselves never exceed the speed of light, these effects can appear almost instant to observers. Additionally, in a model for the double-slit experiment, a single particle is described by many interacting worldlines. Together they create wave-like patterns, while the single worldline that reaches the detector gives the particle-like outcome.

Gravity can also be included. Gravitational effects arise through the gradual relaxation of the gravitational potential in a weak-gravity regime, or more generally of spacetime curvature, with respect to the additional evolution parameter. Because both matter and gravity develop gradually in the forward direction of time, the theory also offers a natural explanation for why time seems to flow in one direction.

Implications and predictions
What I find most surprising is that key quantum phenomena, such as entanglement and double-slit interference, can be explained in such a clear, classical-like way within this five-dimensional theory. Such an intuitive understanding of quantum phenomena is commonly thought to be impossible within standard quantum frameworks. Since basic aspects of gravity also fit into the same framework, this approach may point toward a new route to quantum gravity.

The theory is designed to avoid long-standing problems in quantum physics, such as the measurement problem and the need for ideas like objects being in many states at once or influencing each other instantly at a distance. Therefore, following Ockham's razor, I argue that such a clear five-dimensional classical description of reality may be preferable to quantum gravity theories that abandon intuitive understanding altogether.

Crucially, the theory is not just philosophical. It makes testable predictions that differ from those of standard quantum-gravity approaches. For example, effects such as gravity-induced entanglement—often expected if gravity itself is quantum—do not arise in this framework. The theory also suggests that information about which path a particle takes in a double-slit experiment could, in principle, be obtained through gravitational measurements without destroying the interference pattern. These differences offer clear opportunities for future experimental tests.

Conclusions and future directions
In summary, the new theory suggests that both quantum effects and gravity might be explained in a single, classical-like way if we are willing to move beyond the idea that reality is limited to four dimensions of space and time. From this view, the strange behavior of quantum physics may not be a property of nature itself, but rather a result of our limited perspective on a deeper, higher-dimensional classical reality.

At present, the theory is only a first step. Further work is needed to see whether it can fully reproduce the successes of quantum theory, including the Standard Model of particle physics, and whether it remains valid in extreme settings such as black holes. If it does, this approach could open a new direction in the search for a theory of quantum gravity.

Talagrand's convexity conjecture
In 1995, Michel Talagrand came up with his famous mathematical problem, which asks whether convexity can be "created" in a fixed, uniform number of steps (using operations called Minkowski sums) in any number of dimensions. In mathematics, convexity means that a shape or function bends outward, ensuring no gaps or inward dents exist. So, any line drawn from two points on the perimeter or inside of the shape should lie entirely within the shape. For example, a circle or square in two dimensions, or a sphere or cube in three dimensions, would be considered convex.

Talagrand's convexity conjecture requires Minkowski sums, which are mathematical operations that combine two sets of points or geometric shapes by adding every single point in the first set to every point in the second set. All of this gets more complicated as the number of dimensions increases. Some refer to this problem as the "curse of dimensionality," which causes both the geometric complexity and the computation time of the resulting shapes to explode exponentially.

Talagrand himself didn't think the convexity conjecture was solvable and offered $2,000 to anyone who could come up with the proof. He told Scientific American, "I made this bold conjecture really without any ground for it, you know—it's just a shot in the dark. When you say something like that, you feel it cannot possibly be true."

Talagrand originally showed in his 1995 paper that two Minkowski additions are not enough to guarantee the creation of a large convex subset. In 2025, another mathematician proved that replacing the Minkowski sum with convex operations makes this stronger version of the convexity problem false. But this still didn't solve Talagrand's more general version.

Finding proof in probability
The new proof was worked out by Dongming Hua and Antoine Song from the California Institute of Technology, and Stefan Tudose from Princeton University, who joined the other authors after hearing about their work. Together, the mathematicians reformulated Talagrand's geometric conjecture to a problem of probability theory and random vectors. In their paper published on the arXiv preprint server, they proved an equivalent conjecture for probability, showing that any 1-subgaussian random vector in n dimensions can be expressed as the sum of three standard Gaussian random vectors.

This result solves Talagrand's convexity problem, proving that for any large enough set in Gaussian space, a convex set of significant measures can be found inside a triple sum of the original set. The solution also confirms a combinatorial analog of the problem, which is important for discrete mathematics.

Initially, Song and Hua say they attempted to work out a solution with the help of ChatGPT. However, while the LLM helped to answer some of their questions and move them closer to a solution, it was Tudose who provided the final proof. Ultimately, the team did not use the work done with ChatGPT. In their paper, the team writes that Tudose's proof was "more general and conceptual."

The solution to this decades-old mathematical mystery bridges geometry, probability and combinatorics, and provides some surprising connections between continuous and discrete worlds. Although these kinds of mathematical problems may feel obscure, many technologies involved in our everyday lives rely on complicated mathematical tools and algorithms. The solution to Talagrand's conjecture may impact data science, machine learning and things like logistics optimization, where similar models involving complex randomness are common.
