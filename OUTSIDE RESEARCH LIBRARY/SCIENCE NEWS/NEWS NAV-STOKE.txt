We’re sharing a solution to the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the Millennium Prize Problems. This proof, produced by an internal OpenAI system, shows that the dynamics of the Navier-Stokes equations for fluid motion can develop a singularity in finite time. We’re sharing both a writeup of the proof and a formalization in Lean.

The Millennium Prize Problems⁠(opens in a new window) represent some of the deepest questions at the frontier of mathematics. The question of whether smooth three-dimensional fluid motion can break down has remained unresolved for roughly 90 years.

A major goal of our work is to empower scientists to advance research and technology that benefits all of humanity. To solve the Navier–Stokes problem, we used an internal model that is significantly more capable than GPT‑6 Astra. We believe it is important to inform the world about the pace of AI progress and what to expect from upcoming models.

The problem
The Navier–Stokes equations use Newton’s second law of motion (“F=ma”) to describe how fluids move. Importantly, they treat a fluid as a continuous medium rather than tracking individual molecules. These equations are used for aircraft design, weather forecasting, and the study of blood flow.

A fundamental open question for these dynamical equations has been whether the continuum approximation of the fluid can break down. Specifically, can the Navier–Stokes equations for a three-dimensional incompressible fluid with constant density develop a “singularity,” even when the motion starts smoothly? Here, a singularity means the dynamics lead to speeds in the fluid growing without bound within a finite amount of time. The development of a singularity would have to happen despite the presence of viscosity, which tends to smooth out motion. Because a real fluid cannot move infinitely fast, this would mark a breakdown in how the equations model the fluid. To continue modeling the system, one would then need to track the behaviour of each particle individually.

The equations date to the nineteenth-century work of Claude-Louis Navier and George Gabriel Stokes. In 1934, Jean Leray proved that solutions exist in a generalized sense, but whether they always remain smooth became a central unanswered question. In 2000, the Clay Mathematics Institute named the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem one of seven Millennium Prize Problems.

The result
Our system produced an analytical proof and a Lean formalization that an initially smooth fluid at rest can develop a singularity in a finite time. The fluid has a smooth force applied to it, and its energy remains finite through the entire dynamics, from rest to the formation of the singularity. This resolves the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem by establishing statement “C” (and also “D”) in the official Millennium Prize formulation⁠(opens in a new window).

The solution is a vortex, a spinning swirl of fluid, that spirals inward and gets increasingly elongated, like spaghetti. This central region shrinks while it speeds up in such a way that its energy still stays finite, as required by the laws of physics. The technical challenge is for the equations to develop the breakdown through the motion of the fluid itself, rather than, for example, us putting in an infinite force by hand. More mathematically, the terms in the Navier–Stokes equations that describe the motion—acceleration, pressure gradients, momentum transfer, viscosity—must both become big yet cancel in a precise way. This detailed balance leaves a smooth external force even as the velocity of the fluid grows without bound.

On Tuesday, September 1, we heard rumors that two Millennium Prize problems had been resolved. Inspired by these rumors and by the step change in performance of our internal model, we launched an effort to evaluate it on all open Millennium Prize problems and a few other high-impact problems.

We used a system of coordinating agents powered by our internal model. The agents had access to tools such as the ability to read from a cached version of the internet and the ability to run code. Agents were subdivided into groups with the ability to communicate within the group. The groups varied in size, and the group that produced the Navier–Stokes resolution involved on the order of 10,000 concurrent agents. At all times we maintained the same strict safeguards that we apply to all our frontier model evaluations, including monitoring and isolation.

For each problem, we prompted different groups of agents with different variants of the problem statement, covering all variants of the problem. For the Navier–Stokes problem, we suggested versions “A” and “B” (particular forms of the Navier–Stokes problem which would result in a proof) and versions “C” and “D” (which would result in a disproof) to separate groups of agents.

In addition to the full Millennium Prize problems, we asked our multiagent system to try a set of “easier” problems. One of these problems was a similar blowup question for the limit of the Navier–Stokes problem with the viscosity term removed. This is known as the regularity problem for the Euler equations, and our agents surprised us by resolving this question. The specific variant of the question that they resolved was the unforced version, where no external force is applied to the fluid. Nearly 100 agents worked together for approximately 50 hours to produce our Euler regularity disproof.1

Once we saw the Euler solution, we thought that Navier–Stokes was the most promising problem to work on. Thus, we decided to devote our resources to Navier–Stokes. To do so, we shifted agents away from the other Millennium Problems and prompted these agents with the Euler resolution. When a further trained version of our internal model became available over the course of the effort, we updated our agents to that model.

We encouraged different groups of agents to explore a diversity of approaches. After some time, we cross-pollinated the agent groups by using Codex to consolidate the most useful insights from each agent group. These follow-up prompts drew on the agents’ own intermediate results. The group that found the solution to Navier–Stokes was guided in such a way.

The agents arrived at their resolution on Saturday, September 5, about 88 hours after the first agents were launched. Lean formalization and verification took an additional 17 hours via GPT‑6 Astra.

Across all attempted problems, the agents sent 4.9 million messages and used about 300 billion output tokens. In the process of resolving the Navier–Stokes problem, the agents sent 2.7 million messages and used approximately 130 billion output tokens.

Concurrent work
Our effort began on September 1st after hearing a rumor which we later realized was related to Levent Alpöge, an Anthropic employee, and Tristan Buckmaster, a math professor at NYU. After the completion of our full project and Lean verification (on September 6th), believing from the rumor they also had a solution of Navier–Stokes, we reached out to them to offer a concurrent release of our result and to recognize their priority in a joint announcement. At that point we found out that they had a resolution of the forced Euler problem. In these discussions we offered them visibility into all of the prompts we used and later to see the proof. We recognize the priority of their work on forced Euler and congratulate them on their remarkable mathematical achievement.

We (the researchers and the agents) did not see any of their work through any means until they released it publicly — in particular, no specific user data was accessed in order to solve this problem. While unlikely, we cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models⁠. However, our proofs differ significantly and even the precise results proved are different in the Euler case (forced vs unforced).

Progress and responsibility
Our goal in releasing this result is to report on the substantial progress of our AI models. We do not intend to claim the Millennium Prize for this result.

This milestone represents substantial work by mathematicians and AI researchers. However, this is not a culmination, but rather a snapshot in time, of progress on AI development.

We believe we are now in the next period of AI progress⁠, and today’s results provide further evidence of this. We are focusing on understanding this model, and using what we learn to help us guide and pace how we pursue further advances in capability. One of our key goals⁠ is to build AI systems which are steerable, accountable, and connected to people, which may require more deliberate choices about the pace of progress, as we continue our mission to ensure AGI benefits all of humanity.

This is going to be harsher than my usual posts for #ComplexityThoughts. I am tired of seeing basic standards of scientific communication discarded as soon as AI enters the story.

The latest news is really remarkable: on September 8, OpenAI announced a proposed solution to the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, produced with a large (about 10,000 units) internal multi-agent system and accompanied by a formalization in Lean. OpenAI itself writes “We’re sharing a solution” and says the result “resolves” the Millennium Prize problem by establishing cases C and D in the official formulation.

I agree: if the argument survives scrutiny, this is potentially historic. And that should be enough for now, at least. Unfortunately it is not.

Background
A little context. In 2000, the Clay Mathematics Institute selected seven exceptionally difficult open questions as the Millennium Prize Problems and assigned a $1 million prize to each. Those problems are a way to mark important mathematical frontiers, emphasize work on deep problems and remind the public that mathematics still has fundamental unanswered questions.

In fact, only one has been officially resolved: the Poincaré conjecture. Grigori Perelman posted the decisive preprints in 2002 and 2003, and the mathematical community then spent years checking and reconstructing the argument before Clay awarded him the Millennium Prize in 2010.


[→ Intriguingly, Perelman declined the money, and four years earlier he had also declined the Fields Medal he really deserved for solving this outstanding problem.]

Navier–Stokes concerns equations describing the motion of fluids such as water and air. In three dimensions, the problem is whether an initially smooth flow must remain regular for all time, or whether the equations can develop a singularity in finite time. OpenAI claims to have constructed the latter in the forced case, with a smooth forcing function.


Source: OpenAI
As of September 10, 2026, the Clay Mathematics Institute still lists Navier–Stokes among the unsolved Millennium Prize Problems.


It is worth remarking that Clay does not accept direct submissions: its rules require a proposed solution to be published in a qualifying outlet, at least two years to pass after publication, and the result to achieve general acceptance in the global mathematics community before the Institute will consider it.

Deal with the flood of pseudo-factual headlines we are reading today.

The (real) problem
So the accurate statement today is (be careful, I am not writing “should be”) simple:

OpenAI has produced a proposed solution, with a formal Lean verification. It has not yet become an officially recognized solution under Clay’s process.

Really, journalists, AI influencers, gurus: that’s not very difficult. It takes 5 minutes to verify and find the relevant links, if you really work in this business and know what you are talking about.
The whole scientific communication depends on preserving this distinction, because an announced result and an established scientific result do not carry the same evidential status.

Yet the information environment immediately started compressing those stages. Quanta, a source I normally regard very highly, ran the headline: “AI Has Solved One of Math’s $1 Million Millennium Prize Problems”. The article itself is more careful: it says the result still has to withstand further scrutiny and notes that humans must verify that the theorem formalized in Lean is the theorem mathematicians intended to prove. But this is not enough: in the era of attention economy, where online users dedicate less than 10 seconds to news, the headline is what they will read at best. The headline is everything.
The New York Times used the more defensible “OpenAI Says It Has Cracked One of Math’s ‘Millennium Problems”: that’s way better.

From influencers who until last week might reasonably have thought Navier and Stokes were a tennis doubles team, to serious media outlets that know how scientific validation works, the incentive is always obvious: publish the largest possible claim and leave the qualifications for later. That gains a click, at least.

As a reader, demand better. As a scientist, demand much better.


Terence Tao. Source: UCLA
Terence Tao pointed to a different cost five days before OpenAI’s announcement. Writing on September 3, Tao used Navier–Stokes to explain how an AI-generated solution to an important open problem could inhibit the future development of a field. His argument is about what open problems do inside science.

The value of Navier–Stokes is not exhausted by the final answer: decades of failed attempts, partial results, new inequalities, regularity criteria and unexpected connections have produced mathematics precisely because researchers did not know the destination in advance. A failed ansatz can expose a hidden obstruction, and an unsuccessful route can create a theorem that becomes important somewhere else.
The search space is productive.

Tao’s concern is that a sufficiently powerful autonomous system could perform much of that exploration internally and expose only the final successful route. Yes, mathematics would receive the answer but at the price of losing access to much of the path that generated it. He describes the risk of an open problem becoming “contaminated” as a source of further advances.

Five days later, OpenAI announced almost exactly the kind of event Tao had treated as hypothetical, and the scale matters. OpenAI reports around 10,000 concurrent agents, 2.7 million agent messages and roughly 130 billion output tokens for Navier–Stokes. The agents reached their result after about 88 hours, followed by 17 hours of Lean formalization. Quanta reports Sébastien Bubeck’s estimate that the compute cost was several million dollars.

OpenAI says it does not intend to claim Clay’s $1 million prize, so the comparison with the prize money would be mostly a distraction. The issue is what happens when one of mathematics’ most productive long-term research problems also becomes a corporate capability benchmark.

Another (real) problem
The events surrounding the announcement also raise several ethical questions. NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster published a statement¹ describing work he and Levent Alpöge had been doing, with several LLMs, on a program initiated by Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa.
Buckmaster is explicit that the basic ideas behind this line of attack belong to Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa. He also describes the days preceding OpenAI’s announcement: concerns about whether private Codex sessions could have affected model training, discussions about timing and authorship, as well as a conversation in which, according to Buckmaster, after he said he would make the events public, he was asked: “Why would you ruin your career?”

Buckmaster explicitly writes that he has not seen OpenAI’s proof, does not know how its model arrived at the result, does not know whether his data were used, and is “not accusing anyone of anything”.

However, OpenAI gives a different account: it says neither its researchers nor its agents saw Buckmaster and Alpöge’s work before public release, and that no specific user data were accessed to solve the problem. It also says it cannot entirely rule out that de-identified data derived from their use of OpenAI products contributed to model improvement. This is insane, I agree, but it is in perfect agreement with the OpenAI terms of service.

Whatever emerges from that dispute, the governance question is already real. Researchers need clear rules about unpublished work, model training, confidentiality, priority and scientific credit when the provider of a research tool can also become a participant in the same race for discovery.

Take-home
I recently wrote about propAIganda: the process by which a technically interesting AI event is repeatedly compressed and amplified until caveats, boundary conditions and uncertainty disappear. No conspiracy is required, because incentives are enough.

This new episode is almost a textbook case:

A proposed proof with a formal verification becomes “AI solved Navier–Stokes”

That becomes “AI solved a Millennium Prize Problem”

From there, we are one headline away from “AI has conquered mathematics”

The original scientific achievement may be extraordinary, but the inflation around it still deserves criticism.

If the proof survives scrutiny and receives the recognition it deserves, I will gladly celebrate it. But I want to celebrate the mathematics that was actually done, with the history, uncertainty, attribution and ethical questions still attached.

If AI is going to transform science, science cannot afford to lower its standards just to make AI look transformative.
