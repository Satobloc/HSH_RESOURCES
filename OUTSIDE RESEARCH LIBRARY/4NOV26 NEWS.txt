The quantum world is bursting with chaos—scientists just found what's holding it together
Story by Caroline Delbert • 1h • 3 min read


By mixing classical nudges with quantum circuits, scientists found rhythms hiding in many-body chaos that could help build better quantum computers.
By mixing classical nudges with quantum circuits, scientists found rhythms hiding in many-body chaos that could help build better quantum computers.
© bestdesigns - Getty Images
In new research, scientists in China and England have sketched out and tested a setup that allows them to pick out and boost the longest strands of quantum potential within a chaotic area of particles. The secret, they explain, is to combine quantum and classical ideas into one assembly in which behaviors are easier to see. By doing this, they’re able to jump from few-body quantum mechanics to the more complicated many-body quantum mechanics, with an updated understanding of what causes the quantum phases in their experiment to either change or stay the same.

“The coexistence of regular and chaotic motion is a universal feature of dynamical systems, ranging from planetary orbits and ocean waves to turbulence and transport in complex materials,” the team writes. In this context, chaos is a measure of predictability, or the lack thereof. Remember when Jeff Goldblum’s Dr. Ian Malcolm suggestively dripped water onto the waiting hand of Laura Dern’s Dr. Ellie Sattler in Jurassic Park? It was as unpredictable as their romantic tension.

Sometimes, the appearance of chaos is misleading: ancient people would have guessed that many phenomena, like weather or the germination of a seed, were chaotic, but with today’s knowledge we know that those things can be accurately modeled and predicted. Today, physicists are even more incentivized to understand chaotic systems, because these include the cutting-edge fields of quantum computing and quantum networks.

There’s a huge jump in the complexity of physics between a few-body quantum system and a many-body one. The goal of the new research is to pin down the dynamics of chaos and order in a many-body system. To do that, the team used a superconducting circuit, meaning an arrangement of logical gates cooled to near-absolute zero in order to reach zero resistance.

A circuit runs your light switches, where different positions connect or disconnect the wire that carries current to your lightbulb. In a quantum setup, the logic is controlled by entangled particles, for as long as that entanglement can be maintained. In the experiment, the quantum circuitry was chosen and designed because it’s relatively easy to observe (by quantum mechanics standards) and the researchers could continue measuring it after repeated short periods of activity in the circuit.

By noting how the different entangled “gates” changed between those short windows of time, the scientists could also tune the gates using classical physics. This, they write, represents a hybrid approach that shows how a many-body quantum system shifts between pockets of order and chaos. The classical interventions moved the circuit toward a more ordered state. The researchers’ conclusions differ from the simpler way that a few-body quantum system shifts from order to chaos—and offers a definitive theory that other teams can continue to test and explore.

Their research appears now in the peer reviewed journal Nature Physics. “Our approach gives us a practical way to explore this landscape experimentally,” senior author Zlatko Papić told Phys.org. “Over successive rounds, this process can home in on motion that repeats regularly, without our having to specify that motion in advance.”

Who ordered Starlink, Starshield slowdown in Ukraine? What we know
Story by Laerke Christensen • 2w • 10 min read


U.S. President Donald Trump presents the National Medal of Science to Elon Musk during the 
U.S. President Donald Trump presents the National Medal of Science to Elon Musk during the

The claim that U.S. President Donald Trump or another White House official ordered the slowdown of Starlink and Starshield services in Ukraine in October is unproven.
Serhii Beskrestnov, a defense technology adviser to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, reported on Oct. 14 that SpaceX, the spaceflight and telecommunications company Elon Musk founded, had imposed speed restrictions on its Starlink and Starshield satellite internet services in Ukraine. Beskrestnov warned that the slowdown severely limited the flight speed of Ukraine's drones and "disrupted" a number of its military missions.
Reuters reported on Oct. 15 that Pentagon officials received an order to limit Starshield, SpaceX's military-grade satellite internet service, "from inside the White House." That same day, British newspaper The Telegraph reported that it was Musk who first approached Trump with the idea to slow down SpaceX services in Ukraine. The paper reported that one anonymous source said Trump "agreed" with Musk's idea. On Oct. 20, The Wall Street Journal also reported that one anonymous source said Trump and Musk spoke about slowing down Starshield speeds before Musk reportedly "ordered" restrictions.

Snopes could not independently verify Reuters', The Telegraph's or the Wall Street Journal's reports because they relied on anonymous sources. Considering the fact that Trump and members of his cabinet publicly and repeatedly denied having given the order to slow down Starlink or Starshield in Ukraine, we cannot give this claim a rating. The Pentagon and the White House did not provide relevant comment when asked about the above news reports.

Scientists find loophole that lets black holes split apart
Story by Mary Dada • 15h • 3 min read



Scientists are exploring whether a single black hole could theoretically split into two smaller black holes under extreme conditions | ©Image Credit: Unsplash / NASA Hubble Space Telescope
Scientists are exploring whether a single black hole could theoretically split into two smaller black holes under extreme conditions | ©Image Credit: Unsplash / NASA Hubble Space Telescope
When two black holes collide, they can merge into one enormous black hole. But what if one black hole could do the opposite and split into two? A new theoretical study suggests that this strange possibility isn’t completely forbidden by physics. Under extraordinarily specific conditions, a black hole spinning close to its maximum possible speed could potentially divide into two smaller black holes.

The research, published in Physical Review D under the title “When Black Holes Admit Pair-of-pants Topologies,” examines the physical laws that normally keep black holes intact and what might happen when those rules are pushed to their limits. According to Francesco Sannino, a co-author of the study and professor of physics at the University of Naples Federico II and the University of Southern Denmark, the team wanted to understand which laws prevent black holes from splitting and whether certain circumstances could get around those restrictions.

Why black holes don’t normally split
The biggest obstacle is a concept called entropy. In simple terms, entropy describes the number of possible microscopic arrangements within a system. Black holes have entropy too, and it plays an important role in determining how they can change.

For a non-rotating black hole, splitting into two smaller black holes would reduce the total entropy. That makes the process thermodynamically unfavorable. In other words, the physics doesn’t naturally allow a black hole to break itself into smaller pieces.

This is one reason black holes are generally expected to merge rather than fragment. But the researchers found that rotation could complicate that rule.

Spinning black holes may have a loophole
According to the team’s calculations, a black hole rotating at an exceptionally high speed could potentially get around the usual thermodynamic restriction. There is a catch, though. Producing two black holes is not enough. They would also need to move far enough apart to avoid immediately falling back together and merging again.

Sannino explains that once the researchers account for this separation requirement, the window for a split becomes extremely narrow. It may only remain open for black holes spinning close to their maximum possible rate.

Extra dimensions could make splitting easier
The researchers also explored more exotic possibilities involving theories that propose additional dimensions beyond the three dimensions of space we experience. Some theoretical models already contain black-hole-like objects that can become unstable and begin fragmenting.

Under certain conditions, splitting could even become thermodynamically favorable, particularly when one of the resulting black holes is much smaller than the other. That doesn’t mean the process would automatically happen. The researchers still need to establish what conditions would make fragmentation possible.

Catch a black hole splitting?
The researchers say they cannot yet assign a probability to black holes splitting. According to them, the answer depends partly on how gravity behaves beyond Einstein’s theory of general relativity. They suspect that small primordial black holes, which may have formed in the early universe, could be more prone to splitting than the much larger black holes astronomers observe today.

There may also be a way to look for indirect evidence. Sannino points to gamma-ray observatories such as NASA’s Fermi Large Area Telescope (Fermi-LAT), which could search for signals from tiny black-hole fragments potentially produced during mergers.

For now, nobody has confirmed that a black hole has ever split apart. The study is a theoretical exploration, not an astronomical discovery. Still, it sure challenges the usual picture of black holes as objects that only grow by swallowing matter or merging with other black holes.

Hubble observations challenge common wisdom on how the universe lifted its cosmic fog
Story by Dr. Alfredo Carpineti • 20h • 3 min read


What was the major driver of reionization? - Image Credit: MPIA graphics department
What was the major driver of reionization? - Image Credit: MPIA graphics department
© IFL Science
After the Big Bang, hydrogen and helium existed in an ionized state. That means the electrons that usually stick tight to atomic nuclei wouldn't stay put, instead moving around freely and making the universe opaque to light.

Around 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe had expanded and cooled enough for these electrons to partner up with atomic nuclei, forming neutral hydrogen and helium atoms. Light was free to move, and the universe became transparent... well, up to a point.

Neutral hydrogen still absorbs some light, especially ultraviolet light, so as the first stars formed from those hydrogen clouds at an uncertain point around 100 million years later, it was like they were surrounded by a fog. Everything was shrouded.

Clearing the fog
Nowadays, however, the fog has lifted, and ultraviolet light can easily travel through intergalactic space, so what gives? Well, ironically, the answer is hydrogen becoming ionized once again.

With the universe no longer so densely packed, this doesn't pose the opaqueness problem it once did, and instead allows starlight to travel further, producing the current state of the universe, in which most hydrogen exists in ionized form and we can clearly the see light from distant galaxies.

Astronomers have previously considered supernovae to be key players in this reionization of hydrogen because they create powerful winds that spread the gas about so it is easier for ionizing radiation – which includes some forms of ultraviolet light – to access hydrogen and knock those electrons back off. But new research is challenging this idea.

“Basically, how these galaxies were leaking ionizing radiation isn’t what we thought,” lead author Cody Carr, from the University of Michigan, said in a statement. “Our observations show that these photons are escaping before galactic winds form.”

What's more, the presence of those winds actually seems to suppress the ability for the ionizing light to escape.

“It appears that energy input from supernovae has stirred up the surroundings and increased, rather than decreased, the coverage of neutral gas and dust that suppresses ionizing radiation escape,” added Stephan McCandliss, senior author of the new study and director of the Center for Astrophysical Sciences at Johns Hopkins University.

If not supernovae, then what?
The finding means some alternative scenarios for how the universe reionized will have to be revisited. One idea is that young stars, which produce massive amounts of ionizing radiation and their own powerful stellar winds, may have more of a role to play.

“We don’t know exactly what objects caused reionization or how it evolved,” Carr continued. “We know that it took place, but we don’t have complete answers to those questions.”

Lyman continuum escape during the onset of galactic winds in compact starbursts

Abstract
The abundance of UV-bright, compact star-forming galaxies in the early Universe indicates that high-energy (Lyman continuum (LyC); λ < 912 Å) photons from massive stars alone could have re-ionized the intergalactic medium. However, the efficiency with which LyC photons escape these systems—and the relative roles of radiation, stellar winds and supernovae in regulating that escape—remain uncertain. Here, using medium-resolution spectra of six otherwise similar local star-forming galaxies spanning a range of starburst ages, we directly trace the launch of a multiphase galactic wind through individual spectral lines alongside measurements of the LyC escape fraction. We find that LyC escape peaks early (<3 Myr), during a period dominated by intense radiation and strong stellar winds but lacking a fast galactic wind. In more evolved systems (>3 Myr), we detect faster galactic winds of greater equivalent width and dust content. These observations suggest that LyC escape peaks early and is later suppressed by the launch and subsequent mass loading of a supernova-driven wind during the first ~10 Myr of star formation in compact star-forming galaxies.

Over one hundred years ago, a pioneering thinker changed how we understand space and time.

He told us that they're both intertwined in a fabric that permeates the cosmos, comprising three dimensions of space and one dimension of time. 

This now-famous visionary's name? The mustachioed Hermann Minkowski, of course – Albert Einstein's math teacher.

And no, he did not flunk Einstein, who then added gravity to the flat "Minkowski spacetime" and 'birthed' a curved spacetime. 

In fact, one can say that Einstein flunked Hermann, as Einstein deemed Hermann's way of thinking "superfluous learnedness." At first, anyway. 

But why does the Universe have three spatial dimensions and one temporal dimension? A weird new mathematical theory offers a surprising possibility: 

It's all due to the invisible building blocks, like puzzle pieces or LEGOs (or even K'NEX) that form the cosmos. 

In a preprint paper posted to arXiv, which has not yet been peer-reviewed, a trio of physicists from the University of Belgrade, in Serbia, simulated different cosmic geometries, out of virtual four-dimensional triangles, to see if they could give rise to our familiar mix of three dimensions of space and one of time.

The answer is yes, absolutely. Kind of.

The authors used a toy model of quantum gravity – which is itself mysterious and debated – to probe some complex cosmic mechanics without the messy real-Universe complications, like the laws of physics.

This framework modeled what one may consider an immensely miniature Universe, decreasing from four to zero dimensions, like a hierarchical house of cards.

It began with the four-dimensional 4-simplices, which are made of three-dimensional tetrahedra, which are made of two-dimensional triangles, which are made of one-dimensional edges, which join together at zero-dimensional vertices. 

Like a famous quote about world-supporting turtles, it's simplices all the way down!

The researchers then randomized the edge lengths for these pieces, setting the minimum at the Planck length, a scale of 10-35  meters that represents the breakdown of our physical understanding.

The upper bound of this scale is the point at which the Universe stops behaving like a smooth continuum and starts acting like a jagged jigsaw puzzle. Observations from the Large Hadron Collider and the LIGO-Virgo gravitational wave network help set the size range: from the size of the whole Universe, around 1026 meters, down to a scale of 10-20 meters.

So any jaggedness would only appear at a scale way lower than the size of an atom, which is not even close to visible. 

The researchers also considered three different topologies, or cosmic landscapes, made of approximately 100,000 simplices in three configurations.

First, a four-dimensional ball with a boundary.

Second, a four-dimensional sphere without a boundary, because it loops back on itself.

And, thirdly, a disconnected set of four-dimensional simplices, like 100,000 scattered puzzle pieces used as a control.

Finally, the researchers treated all the edges of the pieces either as space-like – akin to physical distances, represented using real numbers – or as time-like – akin to time intervals, represented using imaginary numbers, or a combination of the two. 

Altogether, their final data pool included over 20 billion evaluations of these various simplices-based simulations.

So, did a four-dimensional 'Universe' emerge? 

Amazingly, yes. When you toss a bunch of randomized puzzle pieces in a mixer, strip away complex cosmic dynamics and forces, and let them assemble geometrically, up to about 85 percent of the time they organize into a spacetime with three spatial dimensions and one temporal dimension – according to mathematical signatures the researchers assigned to these dimensions.

Some other weird configurations also pop out. Around 6 percent of the trials give a Euclidean signature: a frozen Universe with four spatial dimensions and no time.

Minkowski metric signature as a statistical prediction of piecewise-flat quantum gravity
Mihailo Djordjevic, Igor Prlina, Marko Vojinovic
We study an ab initio calculation of the signature of the spacetime metric, within the framework of piecewise-flat models of quantum gravity. Applying numerical techniques, we evaluate the probability that any 4-simplex in a triangulation of a spacetime manifold has a given metric signature. For this we use a toy-model of quantum gravity based on quantum Regge calculus, where we ignore any effects due to dynamics and focus only on the kinematics of the configuration space. Our results may hold even for other QG models, as long as they behave similarly in the same approximation. We obtain that for a suitably chosen spacelike domain of edge lengths and the UV cutoff, the probability of observing Minkowski signature reaches approximately 85%. In contrast, allowing both spacelike and timelike edge lengths leads to the split signature being dominant, with probability between 72% and 81%. The results are essentially independent of the choice of topology and triangulation of the spacetime manifold. Our findings demonstrate that the signature of the metric does not necessarily need to be postulated in a given model of quantum gravity, but can instead be predicted from it, and we estimate the relative size of the UV cutoff and the domain for edge lengths that a given model should incorporate in order to predict the Minkowski signature with high probability. There is a potential to further increase this probability by taking into account the dynamics of the theory.

This material remains elusive, but Cornell researchers have demonstrated that uranium ditelluride can exhibit both flavors at once, setting it apart as the leading candidate.

"No one has yet convincingly demonstrated a topological superconductor, but now we know that uranium ditelluride at least has the right ingredients to make it happen," said Brad Ramshaw, associate professor of physics in the College of Arts and Sciences and director of the Laboratory of Atomic and Solid State Physics.

"Thermodynamic Discovery of Tetracriticality and Emergent Multicomponent Superconductivity in UTe2" was published in Nature Physics on Oct. 30 with Ramshaw as the corresponding author. Doctoral student Sahas Kamat, M.S. '25, is the first author.

Two superconducting flavors can coexist
In uranium ditelluride, one type of superconductivity emerges under regular conditions: extreme cold. Another shows up only in very strong magnetic fields or under squeezing pressure.

In experiments redrawing and correcting uranium ditelluride's phase diagram—the map of where superconductivity emerges as a function of temperature and pressure or a magnetic field—researchers in the Ramshaw lab show the potential is there, although not proven yet, for both flavors to mix together to form a topological superconductor, settling years of uncertainty surrounding this much-studied material.

"The map people had in their heads about what the superconductor does as a function of temperature and pressure—it turns out it's not consistent with thermodynamics," Kamat said. "That bugged me quite a bit."

Thermodynamics is about as solid a theory as there is in all of science, Ramshaw said, so there had to be another answer.

"So, we went about making this map on our own, independent of what people had done in the past," Kamat said. "That led us to finding another kind of superconductivity in this material under pressure."

The pressure was extreme, about 18,000 pounds focused on five millimeters, "like nine cars balanced on your pinky," Kamat said. The pressure had to be locked in at room temperature. Then the temperature went down and down, to between 2 and 1 kelvin.

The resulting phase diagram shows a section of superconductivity caused by deep cold, a separate section of superconductivity caused by pressure (the "special flavor"), and then a section where the two coexisted and interacted during the experiment.

"We showed that one type of this orbital state exists on one part of the map and another type of this orbital state exists on another part of the map, and they intersect," Kamat said. "In that intersecting region, you have both happening at the same time."

When cooling drives superconductivity away
Moreover, the two types of superconductivity compete as they interact, leading to one competing with the other and making it disappear as the temperature goes down. It's remarkable, Ramshaw said, that one type of superconductivity actually goes away as the temperature drops. It's theoretically possible, but this is the first time it has been demonstrated in an experiment.

"It can happen here because another superconductor comes in and pushes it out of the way," Kamat said.

Quantum computing remains several steps away
An application to quantum computing, although possible, is several steps away, Ramshaw said. At least one tech company has spent billions of dollars trying to engineer a topological superconductor, without success so far, the researchers said.

"The parallel approach is that nature has at least 6,000 superconductors we've found. One of them might be that superconductor," Ramshaw said. "We've established that this is the most likely one we have."

“That means if you know what your building blocks are and how they’re arranged, you can explain what properties materials have,” said Rob Macfarlane. He is the Richard P. Simmons Professor in Metallurgy in MIT’s Department of Materials Science and Engineering. “Controlling that is how you make materials that have new properties.”

One obstacle is the tendency of attracting particles to pack densely. Such arrangements provide many neighboring contacts, but they restrict the available architectures. Scientists call repeating, ordered nanoparticle arrangements superlattices. Making a more open superlattice requires some way to limit how many neighbors each particle connects with.

Earlier strategies have included changing particle shapes or making selected surface regions sticky. Those features can guide particles into particular arrangements. But producing them can require elaborate synthesis tailored to a specific material, size or condition. A successful laboratory recipe therefore does not automatically become a broadly usable manufacturing method.

Letting the connectors move
The team focused on ligands, molecules attached to nanoparticle surfaces that influence how particles interact. In many directional assembly strategies, these connectors must preserve particular orientations. The researchers instead explored the opposite extreme. They wanted connectors flexible enough to reorganize as neighboring particles approached one another.

A schematic of PEG chain alignment at the interface between assembled NCTs. (CREDIT: Rob Macfarlane et al, Nature Materials 2026)
“We thought, ‘Let’s do the exact opposite: make the bonds so flexible it doesn’t matter what direction they’re initially pointing.’ The ligands can then reorganize as the particles come together, and that reorganization itself limits how many neighbors each particle can bind,” Macfarlane said. That shifts part of the design problem from fixed geometry to molecular behavior. The connections emerge through assembly itself.

The system uses flexible polymer ligands with electrically charged tips. Some tips carry positive charges, while others carry negative charges. Opposite charges attract, encouraging the chains to bend toward compatible partners. Their flexibility allows the connecting molecules to respond to the surrounding particles rather than retain a predetermined direction.

This is a form of self-assembly, in which interactions among components produce an organized structure. The particles are not individually placed into position. Instead, their surface chemistry shapes how they come together. The important question is which arrangements those interactions favor when the connectors can deform substantially.

Fewer neighbors through flexibility
As the particles assemble, their flexible outer coatings can reorganize along the interfaces between them. This produces an effective valency, meaning a preferred number of neighboring connections. The number need not be specified through the particle’s shape or a fixed pattern of sticky patches. It can arise from the behavior of the coating.

The researchers found that greater ligand deformability could lower the lattice coordination number, another term for the number of neighboring particles. That matters because fewer neighbors can produce less densely packed structures. The finding links a molecular property, flexibility, to the architecture of an entire particle network. It supplies another variable for designing that architecture. Researchers can therefore consider the polymer coating alongside the particle core when deciding which network to pursue.

In experiments led by MIT doctoral candidate Nicholas Sbalbi, the team changed nanoparticle sizes and ligand-chain lengths. Those adjustments altered how the particles connected and the structures they formed. The researchers also assembled crystal structures described in the announcement as previously unseen. The central result is structural control, rather than a demonstrated improvement in a finished device.

“Without having to predesign where the particles connect or what their shapes are, we can control the number of bonds each particle has,” Macfarlane said. “The amount of flexibility of the polymer dictates how many bonds it has. It allows us to lower the number of bonds each particle has and make lattices we have never seen before.”

Testing a different design principle
The project brought experimental and computational work together. Sbalbi, MIT undergraduate Hana Shinzawa and Matthew Ye designed and performed experiments and analyzed experimental data. Stephen Kronenberger, a doctoral student at the University of Delaware, conducted and analyzed molecular dynamics simulations. These calculations examined how the molecular components behaved during assembly.

Macfarlane supervised the experimental work, while Arthi Jayaraman supervised the computational research. Jayaraman is a former University of Delaware professor whose affiliation on the paper is IBM France. Combining the two approaches helped establish ligand flexibility as a design parameter. The result does not mean every flexible coating will produce any desired structure.

The distinction is important for interpreting the advance. The team demonstrated a route to structures with lower coordination, packing density and symmetry than hard-sphere packing would predict. That expands the available design choices. It does not establish that rigid connectors or shaped particles have become unnecessary across all nanoparticle systems.


A schematic of functionalized nanoparticles used in the MIT study. (CREDIT: Rob Macfarlane et al, Nature Materials 2026)
A schematic of functionalized nanoparticles used in the MIT study. (CREDIT: Rob Macfarlane et al, Nature Materials 2026)
From structures to useful materials
The researchers see potential applications in photonics, which involves controlling light, and chemical catalysis, which accelerates reactions. Medicine and agriculture are other proposed directions. Those possibilities depend on matching a structure to a particular task. Demonstrating an assembly method is an earlier step than demonstrating performance in those settings.

Nanoparticles and polymers already support diverse approaches to drug delivery. This study adds a different kind of control over how particles organize. Whether that control translates into a useful medical material remains to be tested. The same distinction applies to the other proposed applications.

For Macfarlane, a longer-term goal is separating the choice of particle from the choice of assembled structure. Researchers would then have more freedom to select building blocks for their useful properties. Flexible connectors offer another route toward that goal. The next practical challenge is establishing which less dense arrangements deliver a measurable advantage for a specific application.

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is a faint glow in the universe dating back to roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang, long before stars or galaxies emerged. Detecting this light and mapping its fluctuations provide valuable insights into the age and composition of the universe.

Researchers at Columbia University recently analyzed CMB data and gathered evidence of a CMB signal that could be produced by internal gas motions in galaxy clusters, large collections of galaxies that are held together by gravity. Their findings, published in Physical Review Letters, strengthen earlier clues of this gas motion and a phenomenon called the rotational kinematic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (rkSZ) effect reported by other astronomers.

"This project began after a meeting with my Ph.D. adviser, J. Colin Hill, in which we discussed signals in the CMB that hadn't yet been observed," Samuel Goldstein, first author of the paper, told Phys.org. "One of these is the rkSZ effect. As CMB photons pass through a galaxy cluster, they scatter off its ionized gas, and if the gas is rotating, one side of the cluster appears slightly hotter and the other slightly colder."

Goldstein and Hill's study drew inspiration from earlier work by Eric Baxter and collaborators at the University of Pennsylvania and other institutions. This previous study offered initial hints of the rkSZ signal in galaxy clusters.

"We built on their work by analyzing a larger sample of clusters and developing several new data-analysis techniques to improve our sensitivity to the rkSZ effect," said Goldstein.

Searching for moving gas in CMB
Gas moving inside galaxy clusters would leave several subtle imprints on the CMB. For instance, theoretical predictions suggest that it would slightly change the radiation, producing a temperature shift.

"Due to the Doppler effect, gas moving toward us makes the CMB appear slightly hotter, while gas moving away makes it slightly colder," explained Goldstein. "As a result, a cluster whose rotation axis lies perpendicular to our line of sight will appear slightly hotter on the side approaching us and slightly colder on the side receding, producing a dipole pattern across the cluster."

The signal resulting from the flow of gas is typically too faint to be detectable in a single galaxy cluster. Goldstein and Hill therefore aligned and averaged CMB observations collected from 25 different clusters that displayed signs of internal motion, such as cluster rotation and mergers.

Each of the 25 clusters they examined was made up of at least 50 member galaxies that were recorded by the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS). The SDSS is a large astronomical survey aimed at mapping the sky, capturing images and light spectra of stars, galaxies and other objects in the universe.

The researchers processed Planck CMB maps, maps of the CMB in the sky derived from measurements by the European Space Agency's Planck satellite, to reduce unrelated fluctuations. They then aligned, rescaled and averaged maps of the CMB around the 25 clusters.

"We estimated each cluster's rotation axis from the motions of its member galaxies, cut out the Planck CMB map around each cluster, rotated the cutouts so that the rotation axes were aligned, and averaged over all 25 clusters," said Goldstein. "We also developed several techniques to improve our sensitivity, including optimally weighting each cluster and using polarization data to reduce noise in the maps."

Inspiring more investigations of galaxy-cluster dynamics
This study provides new evidence for the rkSZ effect, hinting at internal gas motions in galaxy clusters. Specifically, the team detected a dipolar temperature pattern consistent with those motions.

In simpler terms, in the combined CMB map they created, the researchers found that one side of the clusters appeared warmer and the other cooler. This signal is consistent with the kSZ effect, a tiny change in the CMB's temperature caused by its light scattering off electrons in gas moving toward or away from us.

"The kSZ effect has become quite an active area of research recently because it probes the momentum of ionized gas throughout the universe, which depends on both where the gas is located and how fast it is moving," said Goldstein.

"To date, most kSZ analyses have focused on measuring the distribution of gas in and around galaxies and clusters. In contrast, our measurement probes gas motions within individual clusters and in merging systems. Our main result is 3.6σ evidence for a signal consistent with gas motions associated with galaxy cluster rotation and mergers."

Goldstein and Hill introduced a new promising approach for studying galaxy cluster dynamics by analyzing CMB data. Other astronomers could soon build on their findings and conduct similar analyses that consider an even larger number of clusters.

"I'm excited to search for this signal with new galaxy and CMB data from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) and the Simons Observatory (SO), respectively," added Goldstein.

"Together, these data sets should make it possible to distinguish coherent rotation from other small-scale gas motions, particularly those driven by mergers. I also hope to apply some of the techniques we developed to extract this signal from existing data to improve searches for other faint effects in the CMB."

Such studies have provided important insight into the independent, diffusion-based dispersion of ions. The 4D-QENS technique involves rotating a single-crystal sample as it is exposed to the neutrons. This results in data that allow the researchers to generate a complete picture not only of how ions are moving but also of interactions that drive correlated motion.

"The idea of doing these sample rotations has been around at pulsed neutron sources for about 15 years, but it has not been applied to QENS before," said Raymond Osborn, senior physicist at Argonne and lead author of a study unveiling this new method. "With 4D-QENS, we show new effects that we would never have seen without such large, contiguous volumes of data."

To test the method's potential, the researchers performed 4D-QENS on single-crystal samples of strontium chloride, a salt that becomes a fast-ion conductor at temperatures above 1,200°F. Though strontium chloride is not a candidate for future applications, its well-studied properties make it an ideal model crystal on which to test the technique.

Ion traffic jams come into view
The study delivered a refined understanding of ion movement from one site to another within the crystal lattice structure—hopping—that agreed with previous studies. But it also revealed direct evidence of correlated motion among ions that can either facilitate movement through the material or lead to traffic jams that would be detrimental in a battery. A deeper understanding of such correlated motion through 4D-QENS is crucial to help improve solid-state electrolytes.

The unique properties of neutrons, which are electrically neutral with a mass similar to the atoms and ions, are key for this technique. Minute changes in neutron velocity, accompanied by significant changes in direction, allow researchers to chart ion movements in considerable detail. That led researchers to the Spallation Neutron Source at ORNL, the world's most powerful pulsed neutron source.

Richer data demand greater computing power
The volume of data from such measurements further increased with the recent power upgrade of the Spallation Neutron Source, along with ever more powerful DOE computing resources such as the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility (ALCF), also a DOE Office of Science user facility.

"The questions that we were able to ask about ion conductivity even a few years ago were much more limited in scope. Now we're measuring 3D volumes plus energy transfer, making it 4D," said Stephan Rosenkranz, Argonne Distinguished Fellow and group leader in the Materials Science division. "These comprehensive data sets are allowing new modes of data analysis. Those modes demand computing resources that can match the quality of the data."

This type of analysis would be appropriate in future applications of 4D-QENS to more complex materials that are directly relevant to solid-state electrolytes and other energy technologies, the researchers said.

Abstract
Methods of elucidating the mechanisms of fast-ion conduction in solid-state materials are pivotal for advancements in energy technologies such as batteries, fuel cells, sensors, and supercapacitors. In this study, we examine the ionic conduction pathways in single crystal Sr⁢Cl2, which is a fast-ion conductor above 900 K, using four-dimensional quasielastic neutron scattering (4D-QENS). We explore both coherent and incoherent neutron scattering at temperatures above the transition temperature into the superionic phase to explore the correlated motion of hopping anions. Refinements of the incoherent QENS yield residence times and jump probabilities between lattice sites in good agreement with previous studies, confirming that ionic hopping along nearest-neighbor directions is the most probable conduction pathway. However, the coherent QENS reveals evidence of de Gennes narrowing, indicating the importance of ionic correlations in the conduction mechanism. This highlights the need for improvements both in the theory of ionic transport in fluorite compounds and the modeling of coherent 4D-QENS in single crystals.

Popular Summary
State-of-the-art experimental techniques can enable a deeper understanding of energy materials’ structure and properties. Advances in time-of-flight neutron scattering from single crystals now allow the energy broadening of ionic conductors to be measured over large volumes of momentum transfer, a technique known as four-dimensional quasielastic neutron scattering (4D-QENS). Article Text
I. INTRODUCTION
Ionic conductivity plays a critical role in a wide range of energy applications, including batteries, fuel cells, sensors, and supercapacitors [1]. Fast-ion conductors, which exhibit high ionic mobility, are particularly valuable for enabling efficient charge transport in these applications [2,3]. Understanding the mechanisms of ionic conduction, including the interplay between structural dynamics, correlated motion, and defect-mediated pathways, is therefore pivotal for the design of next-generation materials [2–4]. Exploring these mechanisms is essential for advancing the design of safer, more efficient, and scalable energy-storage systems [2,3,5,6].

In many solid-state ionic conductors, mobile ions undergo random hopping processes, moving between vacant sites created either by doping or thermally activated Frenkel defects [6,7]. However, there is evidence in some materials of the importance of metastable interstitial sites along the hopping pathway [2,8,9] or cooperative ionic motion, where the movement of one ion facilitates the motion of nearby ions by lowering the energy barrier for subsequent hops [2,10]. While simulations have highlighted the significance of ionic correlations in conduction mechanisms, experimental techniques capable of directly detecting and characterizing this phenomenon remain underdeveloped [2,11–14].

One of the primary microscopic techniques for probing ionic dynamics in solid-state conductors is quasielastic neutron scattering (QENS) [15–17]. QENS is most frequently used to study the hopping processes of mobile ions in polycrystalline materials and analyzed using jump diffusion models, such as the Chudley-Elliott model [18], to yield spherically averaged values of hopping rates. There are fewer single crystal studies, and these have been confined to measurements along high-symmetry directions due to instrumental limitations [6,7]. However, the discovery of ionic conductors with more complex hopping pathways [2] and correlated ionic motion [19,20], require these limitations to be overcome in order to advance our understanding of ionic diffusion mechanisms in next-generation conductors.

We propose that 4D-QENS offers a promising avenue for addressing this gap. Over the past decade, it has become increasingly common to perform time-of-flight inelastic neutron scattering with continuous or quasicontinuous sample rotation to generate four-dimensional hypervolumes of scattering in reciprocal space, Q, and energy transfer,  [21]. This allows the energy dependence of scattering at constant-Q to be extracted, unlike conventional time-of-flight spectroscopy measured at a fixed sample angle. So far, these measurements have been used to generate 4D-S(Q, ) for the analysis of excitation spectra, providing methods of exploring, for example, phonon anharmonicity or intraband transitions in correlated electron systems [22,23]. However, in this paper, we show that it is just as valuable in the analysis of QENS measurements, allowing the Q dependence of quasielastic linewidths to be determined over substantial reciprocal space volumes, not just high symmetry directions, enabling more stringent comparisons with theoretical models.

While conventional neutron-scattering methods have been effective in analyzing incoherent QENS in terms of jump diffusion models [7], measurements of coherent QENS have so far provided much more limited information. Unlike incoherent scattering, which probes the hopping of individual ions, coherent scattering can reveal the impact of ionic correlations on the hopping process. The coherent QENS intensity, (Q), measures two-particle correlations, which are sensitive to local relaxations around both the hopping ions and the vacancies they may leave behind. On the other hand, coherent QENS linewidths, (Q), are sensitive to the lifetimes of these correlations. If the local relaxations are much faster than the residence times, then it may be valid to analyze the coherent linewidths in terms of jump diffusion models in the same way as incoherent scattering [24]. However, the linewidths may also reveal a much more complex interplay of time scales, especially when there are metastable sites along the hopping pathway. It is well known from studies of liquids and amorphous materials that there can be a narrowing of (Q) close to peaks in (Q), a feature known as de Gennes narrowing [25], which reflects the increased stability of more probable ionic correlations. There are far fewer observations of de Gennes narrowing in crystalline materials, although it has been predicted to apply to a model of dilute interstitials [26]. In this paper, we propose that that the observation of de Gennes narrowing in ionic conductors provides evidence of metastable ionic configurations in the ionic hopping process.

This study uses 4D-QENS measurements to investigate ionic conductivity in the superionic phase of a fluorite compound, , which has been investigated using more conventional neutron scattering methods in the past [6,7]. Our results confirm the applicability of the Chudley-Elliott jump diffusion model to describe the incoherent scattering of hopping anions [7,18], which dominates the scattering at low Q, with improved accuracy because of the continuous three-dimensional coverage in reciprocal space. However, this work provides fundamentally new insights into the role of ionic correlations revealed by the Q-dependent coherent linewidths, in particular the observation of de Gennes narrowing, providing evidence of metastable ionic configurations during the hopping process that were not predicted by previous theoretical studies [14,27]. This demonstrates the power of 4D-QENS in revealing details of ionic conduction mechanisms that are impossible to determine by other experimental techniques.

A. Incoherent scattering
When analyzing 4D-QENS, it is not possible a priori to separate the coherent and incoherent components in the scattering cross section. However, as discussed by Hutchings et al. [7], the energy broadening of incoherent scattering is predicted to be much smaller than coherent scattering. This is because incoherent energy broadening results from the time dependence of the self correlation function, so it reflects the average residence times of all the anions. On the other hand, the energy broadening of coherent diffuse scattering results from the time dependence of two-particle correlation functions involving defects in the anion sublattice, either vacancies or interstitials or both, along with relaxations of neighboring ions, during the hopping process. If, for example, only 10% of the anion sites are involved in hopping at any one time, i.e., they host a vacancy or interstitial anion, the energy scale of coherent QENS will be an order-of-magnitude larger than incoherent QENS (although both are Q dependent, so the ratio is not precise).

IV. DISCUSSION
Examples of de Gennes narrowing have mostly been confined to liquids, including molten  [37], and glasses [38], but the only microscopic derivation in crystalline materials has been provided by Sinha and Ross [26], who modeled the solid-state diffusion of a dilute concentration of hydrogen interstitials. Using linear response theory, they predicted that the coherent linewidths could be derived from the incoherent linewidths by the following equation [16,26]:

(3)
where  is the concentration of interstitials. However, Eq. (3) is inconsistent with the global Q dependence shown in Figs. 4(b)–4(d). We suggest that this is due to the neglect of ionic correlations in the dilute limit [17]. Nevertheless the observed coincidence of a maximum in  with a minimum in  is consistent with the intuitive interpretation of de Gennes narrowing that more probable interionic correlations are associated with an increased lifetime of the corresponding ionic configurations.

The reason for the increased lifetimes goes to the heart of the ionic conduction mechanism. In the Hutchings study [7], the diffuse scattering was modeled in terms of anion clusters, comprising an interstitial anion, located half-way between the regular anion sites but shifted towards a neighboring empty cube center, as well as a number of neighboring anions relaxing away from it. Whether these clusters arose from thermally activated Frenkel defects that were not associated with vacancy hopping or were part of the hopping process itself could not be determined from their measurements. This cluster model was, however, challenged by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations based on realistic pair potentials [27,39], which were able to reproduce the measured S(Q) from an average over the instantaneous ionic configurations, without seeing any evidence of the proposed interstitial clusters in the anion trajectories. Instead, Gillan proposed that anions randomly hopped directly from one regular site to another [11], producing an equal number of vacancies and doubly occupied sites (which Gillan called “interstitial” sites), where the occupation number is defined by a large sphere centered on each site [39]. According to the MD simulations, the strong peaks in diffuse scattering beyond the (200) Bragg peaks are produced predominantly by correlations between anions on the doubly occupied sites, both of which are presumably strongly relaxed from the site center. On the other hand, relaxations of neighboring ions around the vacancy sites mostly contribute to (Q) in regions where the scattering is relatively weak.

The strong Q dependence of the coherent linewidths supports the conclusion that the peak in (Q) is associated with the hopping process itself, since thermal activation of noninteracting clusters would produce a Q-independent linewidth corresponding to their average lifetime. Furthermore, the observation of de Gennes narrowing implies that there is a finite probability for anions to be trapped on doubly occupied sites until one of them is able to hop to a neighboring vacancy site. The MD simulations show evidence of chains of correlated hops consistent with this scenario (e.g., see Fig. 11 of Ref. [40]), resulting from short-range interactions within a unit cell rather than the longer range correlations postulated in other materials [38]. With a dilute concentration of thermally activated vacancies, the residence time of doubly-occupied sites could be substantially longer than the residence time of vacancies determined from the coherent broadening below the (200) Bragg peaks [Fig. 4(f)].

V. CONCLUSION
Our study shows that 4D-QENS, a variation of QENS, in which single crystal samples are continuously rotated through  while collecting data as a function of neutron time-of-flight, is a viable method for characterizing ionic hopping mechanisms in fast-ion conductors such as . It allows the reconstruction of S(Q, ) in four dimensions to facilitate comparisons with models of both incoherent and coherent cross sections as a function of wave-vector transfer. The high-resolution measurements of incoherent linewidths presented here largely confirm the results of earlier investigations of the incoherent scattering, which were analyzed in terms of the Chudley-Elliott jump diffusion model [7]. However, the previous measurements were confined to high-symmetry directions in reciprocal space, which would not have been as sensitive to additional hopping terms, such as to hypothetical interstitial sites. Conclusions that the only significant hopping probabilities are between nearest-neighbor and next-nearest-neighbor anion sites are therefore more robust in the present study where off-symmetry directions are included.

Our measurements of the coherent diffuse scattering are also broadly consistent with the earlier studies, with the most significant intensity observed at wave vectors above the (200) Bragg peaks. However, 4D-QENS has elucidated the Q dependence of the linewidths in unprecedented detail, covering a broad dynamic range of diffuse scattering intensities in a fine Q mesh. This has provided evidence of de Gennes narrowing, resulting from the enhanced lifetimes of defects, in which two anions are centered on the same site. We hope that this work will stimulate an extension of the theory of de Gennes narrowing in crystalline materials to systems, in which there are strong interactions between the diffusing ions, as well as future experiments utilizing the 4D-QENS method to investigate ionic diffusion pathways in more complex ionic conductors.

Scientists have uncovered billions of years’ worth of secrets buried beneath the surface of the moon.

Our celestial companion has been a source of awe and mystery since time immemorial, but now, thanks to China’s space programme, we’re starting to piece together its past.

In 2018, the Chang’e-4 lander, of the Chinese National Space Administration (CNSA), became the first spacecraft ever to land on the far side (or the dark side, if you'd prefer) of the moon.

The universe should be empty. A single trapped antiparticle might finally reveal why it isn’t.
Story by Darren Orf • 1h • 3 min read


Scientists chilled a single positron to its quantum ground state, chasing physics’ biggest mystery.
Scientists chilled a single positron to its quantum ground state, chasing physics’ biggest mystery.
© vchal - Getty Images
The conventional picture of Big Bang cosmology holds that 13.8 billion years ago, all mass and energy was packed into a singularity, a state that predated even atoms, protons, and quarks. Then, a lot happened in a very short time, including the rapid inflation of the universe and the creation of the fundamental building blocks of matter, which eventually coalesced into, well, everything.

Except that shouldn’t have happened at all, according to the Standard Model, otherwise known as Lambda-Cold Dark Matter, or ΛCDM model. During that immense Big Bang (which, of course, didn’t produce any “bang” at all), matter and antimatter created mere fractions of a second after the cosmic event should have annihilated one another in equal measure, leaving behind only a rapidly expanding void filled with radiation—no stars, no planets, no us. But, as evidenced by your eyeballs scanning these very words, that very much didn’t happen, and instead we live in a universe dominated by matter.

So…what gives?

Our existence is the result of what scientists call the “matter-antimatter asymmetry”: for every billion matter-antimatter pairs created at the universe’s birth, one particle of matter somehow survived. That suggests that the Standard Model, our best theory of how the universe works, is incomplete. Now, a new study led by Northwestern University scientists details a promising technique that could help solve this cosmological conundrum.

In an article in the journal Physical Review Letters, the researchers explain how they essentially “trapped” an anti-electron, otherwise known as a positron, for months, creating what they call a “one-positron quantum cyclotron.” Antimatter annihilates on contact with ordinary matter, as it did in the first moments after the Big Bang, so holding even a single particle requires a nearly perfect trap.

“If we want to study antimatter, we must handle it carefully,” Northwestern physicist Gerald Gabrielse, senior author of the study, said in a press statement. “To be clear, it’s not dangerous. We don’t have enough antimatter to make an explosion.”

To capture the positron, researchers relied on radioactive decay from sodium-22, a rare isotope of sodium. Once the positrons emerged, the researchers guided a single one into the trap’s near-perfect vacuum, then cooled it to just 300 millikelvins above absolute zero. The whole process took less than 12 hours.

This process lowered the particle to its quantum ground state, and the scientists could then deliver energy via finely tuned microwaves to study the particle’s quantum transitions. The goal was to eventually gain a complete understanding of the particle’s magnetic properties and compare the results to the team’s previous work with a one-electron quantum cyclotron.

“Current theories suggest that there will be no difference in the two measurements,” Gabrielse said in a press statement. “If it turns out they are not equal, that would be the biggest discovery in physics in the last century.”

Paolo Crivelli, a physicist at Swiss Federal Institute of Technology (ETH) Zurich, who’s unaffiliated with the study, writes for Physics that this research is an “experimental breakthrough,” but it’s not enough on its own to provide high-precision measurements of a positron’s “g factor,” which describes the relationship between the particle’s magnetic moment and its angular momentum.

“Achieving such a measurement will require detailed spectroscopy and exquisite control of systematic effects,” Crivelli cautions.
