Ten Papers: Physical Formulas Are Not Discovered, but Defined 
Paper One — Following Einstein, Introduction, Addition, and Subtraction 
Songtao Zhao 
Independent Researcher 
Abstract 
Following the attempts of Einstein and other predecessors to establish the foundation of a 
unified physical theory, this series of studies puts forward a conjecture: physical formulas 
are not merely discovered through observation, but defined following logical rules. This 
viewpoint inherits Einstein’s conventionalism in physical theories and is grounded in 
academic philosophy, rather than pseudoscience. 
This is the first paper in the ten-paper series. The whole research aims to construct a 
standardized framework based on original insights, revealing the meta-rules governing 
the construction of physical formulas and reducing blind empirical fitting and heuristic 
speculation. Due to the complexity of axiomatic deduction, each type of arithmetic 
operation is discussed in an independent paper. 
This paper focuses on independent physical subsystems and proves the logical necessity 
of addition and subtraction when aggregating independent physical components. Only the 
relationships among scalar and vector independent components are analyzed here. The 
ultimate conclusion of the entire series will be presented as a conjecture in the final paper, 
which does not affect the logical rigor and validity of the arguments in this article. This 
paper only discusses addition and subtraction. Subsequent papers will extend the research 
to multiplication, division, exponents and other arithmetic operations. 
1. Introduction 
Physical equations are mathematical mappings of the interactions between matter, motion 
and spacetime, instead of arbitrary numerical fitting (Feynman, Leighton, & Sands, 1964). 
Traditional physical modeling largely relies on trial-and-error observation and personal 
intuition to determine equation structures, which raises the learning threshold for students 
and young researchers (Knight, 2022). To establish a repeatable, rule-driven process for

generating physical formulas, each fundamental arithmetic operation needs to be verified 
as an irreducible primitive rule. 
Physicists and philosophers of science including Einstein and Poincaré  have long argued 
that the fundamental concepts and principles of physics are not completely determined by 
experience, and theoretical systems contain prominent conventional elements. Re-
examining Kant’s theory of the a priori, Einstein interpreted "the a priori" as "the 
conventional". He repeatedly stated that the logical framework and internal structure of 
physical theories are free creations of the human mind. The value of such theories lies in 
their consistency with empirical facts and internal logical unity (Einstein, 1949). 
At an earlier stage, Poincaré  proposed a classic view on the foundations of geometry: 
geometric axioms are neither synthetic a priori judgments nor pure empirical facts, but 
human conventions. Subject to empirical constraints, people choose geometric systems 
based on simplicity and practicality (Poincaré , 1902/1952). Einstein further extended this 
methodology from geometry to the whole field of physics. 
Based on the above theoretical background, this series explores the underlying rules of 
formula construction starting from the expression of physical formulas. This paper 
addresses the most fundamental question: Why do we use addition and subtraction, rather 
than multiplication and division, to aggregate independent physical components? 
This paper first formalizes the definition of independent physical components, deduces 
the intrinsic logic of using addition for components acting in the same direction and 
subtraction for opposing components, and verifies the rules with classical physical 
scenarios and conclusions from mainstream textbooks. 
2. Formal Definition of Independent Physical Subsystems 
Suppose a composite system is divided into measurable scalar physical quantities 
𝑄1, 𝑄2, … , 𝑄𝑛. A pair of components (𝑄𝑖, 𝑄𝑗) is defined as independent if all three criteria 
below are satisfied: 
1. Invariance of magnitude: Adding or removing component 𝑄𝑗 will not change the 
intrinsic magnitude of 𝑄𝑖 (Halliday, Resnick, & Walker, 2021).

===== PAGE 3 =====
2. Zero interaction: No transfer of mass, energy, momentum, charge or field energy 
exists between components (Serway & Jewett, 2020). 
3. Full contribution: Each independent component contributes its complete 
magnitude to the total observable of the system, without amplification, attenuation 
or occupation of the contribution of other components. 
Addition is adopted to aggregate independent components acting in the same direction, 
while subtraction is used for independent components with opposite directions and offset 
effects. 
For the independent system defined in this paper, multiplication and division are logically 
invalid. If we apply multiplication 𝑇 = 𝑄1 ⋅ 𝑄2, the total magnitude will become zero 
when 𝑄2 = 0. This violates the invariance criterion that the contribution of remaining 
components shall stay unchanged after one component is removed. Similarly, division 
cannot meet the constraints of independent systems. Therefore, addition and its signed 
extension (subtraction) are the only logically consistent operations for independent 
components. 
Note: This conclusion is only applicable to fully independent subsystems defined in this 
paper. Systems with coupled components will be discussed exclusively in the second 
paper of this series. 
3. Axiomatic Deduction of Addition Rules 
3.1 Two-component parallel independent system 
Consider two fully independent physical components 𝑄1 and 𝑄2 acting in the same 
direction. 
1. State 1 (Only 𝑄1 exists): Total output 𝑇1 = 𝑄1 
2. State 2 (Only 𝑄2 exists): Total output 𝑇2 = 𝑄2 
3. State 3 (Both components coexist): In accordance with the independence criteria, 
the magnitudes of 𝑄1 and 𝑄2 remain unchanged, and both deliver full 
contributions. The only logically consistent aggregation is summation: 
𝑇 = 𝑄1 + 𝑄2

3.3 Supplement: Orthogonal vector independent components 
For independent components expressed as spatial orthogonal vectors (e.g., components 
along the 𝑥-axis and 𝑦-axis in a plane rectangular coordinate system), components on 
each axis still follow the rules of addition and subtraction. The resultant vector is finally 
obtained through geometric vector composition. The operation rules for individual axes 
are identical to those for scalar quantities, and orthogonality only changes the final form 
of results without altering the fundamental logic of addition and subtraction. 
4. Axiomatic Deduction of Subtraction Rules 
Mathematically, subtraction is the signed extension of addition for components with 
opposite polarities. From a physical perspective, positive and negative signs correspond 
to specific physical meanings such as acting directions, energy budget and charge 
properties, rather than pure mathematical symbols.

4.1 Two-component opposing independent system 
Let 𝑄+ denote the component with positive contribution and 𝑄− denote the offset 
component with negative contribution. The two components still satisfy all independence 
criteria: neither will change the magnitude of the other, and only their overall effects 
counteract each other. 
1. Isolated positive contribution: 𝑇+ = 𝑄+ 
2. Isolated offset contribution: 𝑇− = 𝑄− 
3. Net output with both components coexisting: The net magnitude equals the 
positive contribution minus the offset part: 
𝑇net = 𝑄+ − 𝑄− 
4.2 Generalization for net offset of multiple components 
Divide all independent components into a positive group {𝑄+1, 𝑄+2, … } and an offset 
group {𝑄−1, 𝑄−2, … }. The net total magnitude of the system is written as: 
𝑇net = ∑𝑄+ − ∑𝑄− 
Essentially, subtraction is not an independent primitive operation, but an extension of the 
aggregation rule for independent components under opposing polarities. 
Scope of application: Net collinear forces, net charge of a system, energy inflow and loss, 
and counteracting currents. 
5. Cross-disciplinary Physical Verification and Benchmark Cases 
5.1 Mechanics: Collinear independent forces (Addition and Subtraction) 
A wooden block is acted upon by a rightward pulling force 𝐹1 and a leftward kinetic 
friction force 𝐹𝑓. The two forces are independent, and their magnitudes will not be 
affected by each other (Halliday et al., 2021, p. 89). 
The net force of the system is 𝐹net = 𝐹1 − 𝐹𝑓. If two pulling forces act in the same 
direction, the total force is 𝐹total = 𝐹1 + 𝐹2. Experimental measurements from force 
sensors verify the validity of addition and subtraction for linear force superposition 
(Knight, 2022, p. 126).

5.2 Conservation of mass (Only addition is applicable) 
Consider two solid objects with no interaction or material exchange, with masses 𝑚1 and 
𝑚2 respectively. Negative mass does not exist in classical physics, and mass is an 
inherently non-negative physical quantity. Thus the total mass is calculated only by 
addition: 𝑀 = 𝑚1 + 𝑚2 (Serway & Jewett, 2020, p. 201). 
5.3 Thermodynamics: Decomposition of internal energy 
The internal energy of an ideal gas can be decomposed into mutually independent 
translational energy 𝑈𝑡, rotational energy 𝑈𝑟 and vibrational energy 𝑈𝑣 (Feynman et al., 
1964, Vol. 1, p. 412). The total internal energy is 𝑈 = 𝑈𝑡 + 𝑈𝑟 + 𝑈𝑣. If heat loss 𝑄loss is 
regarded as an independent offset term, the net retained energy of the system is 𝑈net =
𝑈in − 𝑄loss. 
5.4 DC circuits: Current in parallel branches and net charge 
In a DC parallel circuit, currents 𝐼1 and 𝐼2 in different branches are independent of each 
other. The total supply current is 𝐼 = 𝐼1 + 𝐼2. If leakage current 𝐼leak causes energy loss, 
the net current at the load end is 𝐼net = 𝐼supply − 𝐼leak (Young & Freedman, 2020, p. 603).

Special Cases and Boundary Limitations 
1. Restriction of operations: In classical physics, subtraction is only applied to 
physical quantities that can take both positive and negative values, such as force, 
current and energy variation. It is not suitable for inherently non-negative 
quantities including mass and time interval. 
2. Deviation in real systems: Fully independent ideal systems barely exist in nature. 
Weak residual coupling will generate high-order perturbation terms, but will not 
change the dominant addition-subtraction structure of the system. Relevant 
analysis on high-order corrections will be presented in follow-up papers. 
Scope of the framework: The model established in this paper only applies to addition and 
subtraction for fully independent subsystems. Coupled systems, relativistic systems and 
quantum systems involve modified or extended operation rules, which are beyond the 
scope of this paper. 
7. Boundary Statement of the Ideal Model 
The deduction of addition and subtraction in this paper is based on the ideal model of 
fully independent systems, which describes the dominant structure of physical formulas. 
Weak coupling commonly found in real physical systems leads to minor deviations as 
high-order perturbations, which do not invalidate the core rules of addition and 
subtraction (Feynman et al., 1964, Vol. 1, p. 450). Corrections for residual coupling will 
be discussed in subsequent papers of this series.

8. Conclusions of Paper 1 
1. Addition is applied to superposition of parallel, co-directional and mutually 
independent physical quantities. 
2. Subtraction is applied to counteraction of parallel, opposing and mutually 
independent physical quantities. 
3. For the independent component system defined in this paper, multiplication and 
division violate the independence criteria. Therefore, addition and subtraction are 
the only logically consistent operations. 
4. A large number of fundamental formulas in classical physics follow the above 
rules: conservation of mass 𝑀 = 𝑚1 + 𝑚2, net opposing force 𝐹net = 𝐹1 − 𝐹2, 
total current in parallel circuits 𝐼 = 𝐼1 + 𝐼2, and superposition of multi-mode 
internal energy 𝑈 = ∑𝑈𝑖. 
5. The application of subtraction is limited by the properties of physical quantities. 
Weak coupling in real systems only produces high-order corrections and does not 
affect the main structure of formulas. 
6. Addition and subtraction form the first set of primitive operations in the 
operational system of physical formulas. The rules have been verified through 
axiomatic deduction, mathematical induction and cross-disciplinary case analysis. 
9. Preview of Paper 2 
The second paper will focus on coupled physical systems and answer the core question: 
When physical quantities are no longer independent and interact with each other, how 
does the multiplication rule in physical formulas come into being? Subsequent papers 
will prove that multiplication becomes the only logically consistent operation when 
physical quantities meet specific coupling criteria.
