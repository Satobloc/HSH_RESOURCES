Abstract
Relativistic phenomena admit two complementary perspectives: describing what
happens through spacetime geometry and explaining why it happens through
physical mechanisms. This paper develops a mechanistic framework,Universal
Specificity, that seeks to explain why those phenomena occur while recovering
the observable regularities described geometrically by relativity. The framework
is grounded in theaxiom of universal presentism: the recognition, induced from
universal observation, that only the present physically exists, all existence and
interaction are confined to it, and time is the interval over which physical change
occurs. Because observed physical change is mediated by interactions between
spatially separated entities, the rate at which those interactions are completed,
termed thespeed of causality, determines how much physical change occurs dur-
ing a given interval. Applying Mill’s Methods of Agreement and Difference, the
speed of causality is identified as the metric-independent causal condition gov-
erning both kinetic and gravitational time dilation. This condition is equivalently
represented by specific internal energy, providing a common physical basis for
unifying time dilation, gravitation, and inertia and yielding anequivalence law
that mechanistically explains the empirical success of the equivalence principle.
The framework reproduces the observations described geometrically by relativity
while opening revealing questions concerning deeper physical mechanisms thus
opening new avenues toward a quantum theory of gravity, a unified field theory,
a theory of everything, and ultimately a constitutional theory of everything.

1 Introduction:Descriptio an Explicatio
Scientific inquiry advances through two complementary pursuits. It seeks both to
describewhatregularities are observed in nature through increasingly precise math-
ematical relationships and to explainwhythose regularities occur through physical
mechanism. These complementary objectives answer fundamentally different scien-
tific questions: description characterizes relationships among observable quantities,
whereas explanation seeks the physical processes responsible for those relationships. A
mathematical framework may therefore reproduce observations with remarkable pre-
cision with or without capturing the underlying physical mechanisms that give rise to
them. Likewise, different explanatory frameworks may account for the same observed
phenomena yet differ fundamentally in their ontology and physical interpretation. The
distinction between successful description and successful mechanistic explanation has
consequently played a central role throughout the historical development of physics [1].
History provides numerous examples in which increasingly precise mathematical
descriptions of observed regularities eventually reached a point where deeper physical
explanation became necessary for further scientific progress [1]. Ptolemy’s epicyclic
model of planetary motion, for example, accurately reproduced astronomical observa-
tions through an intricate geometric construction that made no appeal to underlying
physical mechanisms. Kepler’s laws replaced independent geometric constructions with
a single governing influence centered on the Sun, and Newton later unified terrestrial
and celestial motion under a single physical law governed by mass. This progres-
sion illustrates that predictive success alone does not uniquely determine whether the
underlying physical mechanisms responsible for observed phenomena were properly
identified [2, 3].
The relationship between geometric description and mechanistic explanation is not
unidirectional. Just as successful geometric descriptions may later be complemented by
deeper mechanistic explanations, successful mechanistic theories may also be reformu-
lated geometrically without altering their empirical predictions. A canonical example
isgeometrized Newtonian gravitation(Newton–Cartan theory), in which Newtonian
gravitation is represented through spacetime structure rather than as a force acting
within Euclidean space. Force-based and geometric formulations therefore constitute
alternative descriptions of the same observable dynamics rather than distinct empirical
theories [4–6]. This demonstrates that successful geometry need not uniquely deter-
mine physical interpretation and naturally raises the converse question: might the
geometric description of gravitation provided by relativity likewise be complemented
by a coherent mechanistic explanation?
Einstein himself repeatedly emphasized the distinction between mathematical for-
malism and physical content. InGeometry and Experience, he argued that geometry
acquires empirical significance only through its connection with physical laws rather
than through mathematics alone [7]. Throughout his career, he pursued increasingly
unified physical descriptions of gravitation and electromagnetism, reflecting his endur-
ing pursuit of deeper physical explanation beyond geometric description [8, 9]. His
early investigations of variable-speed-of-light (VSL) models prior to general relativ-
ity [10–16], together with his later reconsideration of related ideas [17, 18], illustrate

2.1 On Geometric Description and Mechanistic Explanation
Throughout the history of science, two complementary approaches have repeatedly
advanced our understanding of nature:geometric descriptionandmechanistic expla-
nation. Both can achieve predictive success, yet they answer fundamentally different
questions. Mechanistic explanation is fundamentally grounded in entities acting under
specific physical conditions. It seeks to explain phenomena by identifying the entities
involved, how they act, and the physical conditions under which those actions change.
The defining hallmark of mechanistic explanation is thereforeentities acting. By
action, we mean a change of state of an entity—either internally or in its relations
to other entities. Mechanistic explanation therefore rests upon the law of causality,
understood as the law of identity applied to action. The law of identity states that
each thing has a specific nature that determines what it is; the law of causality states
that each thing must act in accordance with that nature. An entity cannot act in
contradiction to what it is. Causality, in this sense, is not merely temporal succession,
but the necessity of a particular change of state following from a thing’s identity under
given conditions [23–25].1
The law of causality is therefore the fundamental reason predictive success is pos-
sible at all. Given identical essential conditions, identical entities must act identically.
This is what makes successful geometric descriptions and mechanistic explanations
possible in the first place. Mechanistic explanations therefore do not replace the regu-
larities captured by successful geometric descriptions; rather, they seek to explain why
those regularities exist by identifying the entities, interactions, and physical conditions
from which they arise.
This distinction is crucial. A geometric description can achieve remarkable empir-
ical success while remaining agnostic about the underlying physical mechanisms
responsible for those regularities. It answers the questionhow do phenomena behave?
without necessarily answeringwhat produces that behavior?Mechanistic explanation,
by contrast, is explicitly concerned with the latter. It seeks to identify causes, not
merely correlations or patterns.
Importantly, the distinction is not one of correctness versus error. History contains
many cases in which geometric descriptions and mechanistic explanations are empir-
ically equivalent while differing in explanatory content. A geometric description may
organize phenomena with extraordinary precision, yet still leave open the possibility

2.2 Scientific Progress Through Description and Discovery
Successful geometric descriptions advance scientific understanding by capturing sta-
ble observational regularities in precise mathematical relationships, thereby enabling
reliable prediction. By organizing observations into precise mathematical relation-
ships, they also expand the range and precision of empirical evidence available for
causal discovery. Discovering the physical mechanisms responsible for those regulari-
ties extends this scientific progression by providing deeper understanding of physical
entities, broader unification through common causes, and new avenues for further sci-
entific discovery. This progression forms a continuing cycle of scientific progress, as
each mechanistic discovery reveals new opportunities for the next. Together, these
advances ultimately yield greater scientific control through prediction with inter-
vention, the principal practical benefit of scientific progress. The history of science
repeatedly illustrates this progression.
The first scientific benefit of discovering physical mechanisms is a deeper under-
standing of physical entities. Entities are known only through how they act and
interact. An entity that never interacted with anything would be undetectable and
therefore of no possible scientific significance. It is through interactions that we dis-
cover what entities exist, infer their identities, and determine the physical conditions
under which their actions change. Mechanistic explanations therefore deepen under-
standing by identifying the entities involved, the interactions they undergo, and the
physical conditions responsible for those interactions.
The history of astronomy illustrates this progression. The Ptolemaic system
provided genuine scientific understanding by accurately characterizing how planets
appeared to move across the night sky. Through increasingly sophisticated geomet-
ric constructions, it revealed stable observational regularities and enabled increasingly
reliable prediction of celestial events and seasonal cycles useful for navigation, agri-
culture, and calendar keeping. This descriptive understanding proved invaluable in
practice. What it did not explain, however, was why those regularities existed. Plane-
tary motion remained a geometric pattern rather than the consequence of interactions
among physical entities. Consequently, the entities responsible for planetary motion
and the physical conditions governing their behavior remained unknown.
Kepler derived mathematical laws describing planetary motion and interpreted
them mechanistically by proposing that the Sun exerted a governing influence on the
planets. Although the physical nature of that influence remained unknown, plane-
tary motion was no longer understood merely as a geometric regularity but as the
consequence of a physical interaction. Newton later identified universal gravitation

for each planet with a common set of mathematical laws applicable to all plane-

The third scientific benefit of discovering physical mechanisms is that each discov-

may continue to improve predictive accuracy while simultaneously masking the
physical questions whose answers lead to deeper discovery. The mature Ptolemaic sys-

improved prediction, planetary motion remained a geometric fact rather than the con-
sequence of physical interactions. As a result, further progress largely consisted of
refining the geometric description itself rather than investigating underlying physical
causes. Kepler’s mechanistic interpretation transformed that situation by proposing
that the Sun exerted a governing influence on planetary motion. The central scientific

ratherWhat physical mechanism produces the Sun’s influence?Newton’s discovery of

next: by what physical mechanism does gravitation itself act at a distance? Mechanis-

the frontier of scientific discovery by revealing new entities, interactions, and physical
conditions that themselves become subjects of investigation.
The fourth scientific benefit of discovering physical mechanisms is greater scientific
control. The preceding benefits describe how mechanistic discovery advances scientific
knowledge through deeper understanding, broader unification, and continued discov-
ery. Their ultimate practical significance, however, lies in the greater scientific control
that such knowledge makes possible. The ability to predict, intervene, and intentionally
shape future outcomes is the principal practical benefit of scientific progress. Geomet-
ric descriptions enable reliable prediction by revealing observational regularities that
can be exploited in practice. The Ptolemaic system, for example, supported increas-
ingly accurate calendars and seasonal planning despite providing no causal account
of planetary motion. Mechanistic explanations extend that capability by revealing
the physical conditions that produce those regularities, making it possible to under-
stand what must be changed to achieve new outcomes. Newtonian gravitation did

2.3 Epistemic Foundations: Deduction and Induction
The epistemic method appropriate for scientific inquiry depends upon the kind of
claim one seeks to validate. If the objective is to validate a mathematical description
of observational regularities, the hypothetico–deductive method provides a power-
ful means of constructing and refining descriptive frameworks. If the objective is to
establish that a proposed physical mechanism is the true cause of those regularities,
however, induction becomes indispensable. Because geometric descriptions and mech-
anistic explanations pursue different scientific objectives, they consequently rely upon
different epistemic methods for validating their central claims. Geometric descrip-
tions can be validated against observation through hypothetico–deductive reasoning,
whereas mechanistic explanations ultimately require induction to establish that their
proposed mechanisms correspond to reality. Mechanistic hypotheses may certainly
be proposed through hypothetico–deductive reasoning, but deduction alone cannot
establish that the proposed mechanism is the true cause of the observed regulari-
ties. Recognizing this methodological distinction is essential for faithfully developing a
mechanistic framework that identifies physical causes while remaining mathematically
consistent with observational regularities.

2.3.1 Geometric Validation: Hypothetico–Deductive Method
Geometric frameworks are characteristically developed using the hypothetico–
deductive (HD) method. In this approach, candidate principles are proposed, their
mathematical consequences are deduced, and those consequences are compared with
observation. Frameworks whose predictions agree with observation are retained and
refined, whereas those that fail are revised or discarded [27, 28].
This method has proven extraordinarily effective because it systematically evolves
descriptive frameworks toward increasingly accurate mathematical representations of
observational regularities. Through repeated comparison with observation, mathemat-
ical descriptions progressively improve while preserving only those formulations that
successfully account for the empirical evidence. In this sense, the method functions
as an iterative process of empirical refinement, progressively retaining the descriptive
principles that best correspond with observation while discarding those that do not.
Its strength therefore lies in optimizing descriptive adequacy rather than establishing
that its adopted principles correspond to the underlying physical causes [24, 25, 29].
However, its limitation follows directly from the nature of deduction itself. Deduc-
tion can preserve the truth of its premises but cannot establish them. At least one
premise in every deductive framework must be a generalization by definition, and gen-
eralizations cannot themselves be validated deductively. Consequently, hypothetico–
deductive frameworks may achieve extraordinary predictive success while leaving open
whether their foundational assumptions correctly identify the mechanisms responsible
for the observed regularities.
Within such frameworks, explanatory depth is therefore often constrained by the
initial assumptions. Once a postulate is adopted, the explanatory burden shifts to the
mathematical structure it generates. Questions concerning why those postulates hold,
whether the resulting ontology corresponds to reality, or what physical entities and
interactions give rise to the observed regularities typically remain outside the scope
of the framework itself. Geometry and mathematical description consequently become
the principal focus of the theory [7].
2.3.2 Mechanistic Validation: Axiomatic and Causal Induction
Because mechanistic frameworks seek to identify the entities, interactions, and physical
conditions responsible for observed regularities, they require a different epistemic foun-
dation for validating their explanatory claims. Rather than beginning from assumed
postulates, they can begin from facts that are directly and universally accessible
and that cannot be coherently denied. These foundational recognitions are estab-
lished throughaxiomatic induction: the identification of truths that are implicit in all
observation and cognition.
Chief among these are the axioms of existence and identity: that something exists,
and that whatever exists is something specific. From these follows the law of causal-
ity, here understood as identity applied to action—that entities act in accordance
with what they are. These principles are not hypotheses adopted for convenience, nor
conclusions inferred from theory; they are recognitions grounded in the conditions

of awareness itself. Without them, the very possibility of explanation, prediction, or
controlled experiment would collapse [23, 24, 30].
Causal induction builds directly upon this axiomatic foundation. Because entities
act according to their identities, and because identical essential conditions produce
identical actions, causal relationships can be discovered by systematically varying con-
ditions and observing corresponding changes in behavior. This logic is formalized in
Mill’s Methods of Induction [31, 32], which provide explicit procedures for isolating
causal relationships.
Mill articulated two complementary methods that play a central role throughout
the analysis that follows [31, 32]:

cause must lie in what remains invariant.
• Method of Difference (MoD):If a change in conditions is accompanied by

changed.

2.4 The Conceptual Foundation of this Mechanistic Paradigm
Having established the epistemic foundations appropriate for discovering physical
mechanisms, we next establish the foundational concepts upon which this mecha-
nistic paradigm rests. Unlike the geometric paradigm, they are not consequences of
relativistic postulates, but foundational concepts of the mechanistic paradigm itself.
The discussion proceeds from existence (simultaneity) to the evolution of existence
(time), to the alteration of that evolution (time dilation), and finally to the means
by which those changes are observed (measurement). Each concept is first established
within the mechanistic paradigm and then contrasted with its counterpart in the geo-
metric paradigm. Together, these concepts provide the conceptual foundation required
for the causal discoveries developed in the sections that follow.
2.4.1 Simultaneity
Mechanistic Paradigm: Universal Presentism
The mechanistic paradigm developed in this paper begins from a foundational recog-
nition: all observation, interaction, and measurement occur in the present. Every act
of awareness, every experiment, and every physical interaction takes place now. Ref-
erences to the past exist only as present records or memories, and projections of the
future are formed only from present facts.
From this universally accessible observation follows an axiomatic recognition:
whatever exists, exists now. This principle—universal presentism—is not a hypoth-
esis or assumed postulate, but an axiom discovered through axiomatic induction. It
is implied in all cognition and cannot be coherently denied without presupposing
it [23, 24, 29, 30].
Universal presentism is a metaphysical claim, not an epistemological one. It
does not assert that distant simultaneity can be operationally determined; rather,
it asserts that all existence is confined to the present regardless of our ability to
determine distant simultaneity operationally. If two spatially separated entities both
exist, their existence is confined to the same present. Disagreement between observers
about simultaneity therefore reflects limitations of measurement and knowledge, not
indeterminacy in existence.
Geometric Paradigm: Relativity of Simultaneity
The geometric paradigm adopts a different definition of simultaneity. Within relativity,
simultaneity is not treated as a universal fact but as a frame-dependent relation. It is
deduced from the postulate that the speed of light in vacuum is invariant for all inertial
observers. Because distant clock synchronization depends on light signals, observers
in relative motion assign different simultaneity relations to the same events. In this
view, planes of simultaneity rotate through spacetime according to an observer’s state
of motion or position within a gravitational potential [33–36].

This conception is internally consistent within the geometric paradigm, but simul-
taneity is not treated as a fact about what exists. Rather, it is a coordinate-dependent
relation that emerges from the mathematical structure required to preserve light-speed

The distinction therefore lies not merely in how simultaneity is described, but in
what simultaneity is taken to be. Within the mechanistic paradigm, simultaneity is a
fact of existence; within the geometric paradigm, it is a coordinate-dependent relation
emerging from the theory’s mathematical structure.
2.4.2 Time
Mechanistic Paradigm: Time as the Interval Over Which Change Occurs
If universal presentism confines all existence to the present, then a crucial task remains:
to account for change. The present is not static. What exists now is continually
transforming—what we observe, experience, and interact with at one moment differs
from what exists moments later. Yet if only the present exists, then this transformation
must occurwithinthe present itself.
To remain consistent with the law of identity, change cannot be instantaneous—an
entity cannot simultaneously be in a state of existence and not be in that same state.
From universally accessible observation, processes unfold through intermediate states,
and these transformations occur over measurable intervals. Time is therefore induced
asthe interval over which change occurs.
This conception of time is grounded in causality. Entities act according to their
nature, and when identical essential conditions recur, identical changes unfold over
identical durations. Time is not an entity or dimension that acts on matter; it is a
relational measure that compares one process of change to another.
Clocks do not measure an independent substance called time. They compare the
progression of one physical process to another. If all physical change were to cease,
time would cease with it.
This conception integrates naturally with universal presentism: if only the present
exists, and change occurs within the present, then time is nothing more—and nothing
less—than the measured duration of such change.
Geometric Paradigm: Time as a Spacetime Dimension
The geometric paradigm adopts a different definition of time. Within relativity, time
is treated as a coordinate within a four-dimensional spacetime manifold. Events are
located by temporal and spatial coordinates, and physical processes are represented as
worldlines. Time is not defined by change, but by its role within the metric structure
that relates events [33–36].
Within this conception, physical processes do not unfoldintime so much as trace
paths through spacetime. Time is a geometric parameter, interchangeable with space
under Lorentz transformations [34–36].
The distinction therefore lies not merely in how time is described, but in what
time is taken to be. Within the mechanistic paradigm, time is the interval over which
physical change occurs. Within the geometric paradigm, time is a coordinate within
spacetime that relates events.

2.4.3 Time Dilation
Mechanistic Paradigm: Slowing of Physical Processes
The mechanistic paradigm developed here defines time dilation as a physical effect that
alters the rate at which an entity’s internal processes operate, rather than a change in
time as an independent entity. Because time is not taken to exist independently, but
rather as a relational measure of physical change, it cannot itself be altered directly.
When we say time dilates, we therefore mean that the rates of physical processes are
reduced such that the same physical processes require longer intervals to complete.
Consider the following example. Identical clocks that remain co-located under the
same physical conditions operate at the same rate. When identical clocks are subjected
to different physical conditions, the rates of their internal processes can differ, so
less physical change occurs during the same interval. Once reunited under common
conditions, the clocks again operate at the same rate, but they generally indicate
different accumulated intervals resulting from the period of separation. It is not time
itself that has changed; rather, one clock underwent less physical change during the
same interval because its internal processes operated more slowly.
What distinguishes time dilation from other changes affecting measuring instru-
ments is that it does not selectively alter some processes while leaving others
unchanged. Rather, the rates of all physical processes are altered uniformly under the
same physical conditions. Under this conception, kinematic and gravitational time dila-
tion are naturally unified: what differs is the source of the altered physical conditions,
not the nature of time itself.
Because time is the interval over which change occurs, time dilation corresponds
to a real lengthening of the interval required for the same physical change to occur,
caused by altered physical conditions acting upon physical entities.

Geometric Paradigm: Differences in Spacetime Path Lengths
The geometric paradigm adopts a different definition of time dilation. Within rela-
tivity, time dilation appears as a difference in proper time along different worldlines.
Within the geometric paradigm, nothing physically happens to clocks or processes.
Different trajectories simply possess different invariant lengths in spacetime [34–36].
For example, one observer may traverse two events in one year while another tra-
verses the same events in six months. Within the geometric paradigm, this difference
is attributed to one worldline possessing a shorter proper-time interval through space-
time. Time dilation is therefore not treated as a physical effect acting upon clocks
or physical processes, but as a geometric consequence of spacetime itself. Within the
geometric paradigm, the observed differences in elapsed time are fully accounted for
by the geometry of spacetime, without introducing an additional physical mechanism
acting upon clocks or other physical processes [34–36].

2.4.4 Measurement
Mechanistic Paradigm: Measuring Devices as Physical Systems
Within the mechanistic paradigm, measuring devices are themselves physical systems
and are therefore subject to the same causal influences as every other physical entity.
If altered physical conditions slow physical processes or change the distances over
which they occur, then clocks, rods, and every other instrument used to perform
measurements must be altered as well.
Measurements therefore reflect not only the external phenomena being observed,
but also the physical state of the instruments performing the observation. Apparent
invariances must consequently be interpreted with care, since they may arise either
from genuine invariance in the external phenomenon or from corresponding changes
in the measuring systems themselves.
Every measurement is fundamentally a comparison between physical systems. Con-
sequently, an observed difference alone cannot establish whether the measured system
changed, the measuring system changed, or both changed together. Conversely, an
unchanged measurement does not establish that neither system changed, since both
may have changed by the same amount.
This recognition is essential for causal analysis. Because measurement alone cannot
determine which physical system changed—or whether both changed together—causal
identification requires accounting for possible changes in both the measuring system
and the system being measured. Only then can genuine physical invariance be dis-
tinguished from apparent invariance produced by compensating changes within the
measuring instruments or the measured system.
Geometric Paradigm: Measuring Devices as Local Standards
The geometric paradigm adopts a different role for measurement. Within relativity,
rods and clocks serve as local standards by which spacetime intervals are operationally
defined. The measured constancy and isotropy of the speed of light are incorporated
directly into this operational framework through the light postulate and the associated
synchronization procedures. Consequently, observed differences in measurements are
attributed to the geometric relationships between observers rather than to additional
physical mechanisms acting upon the measuring devices themselves [33–36].
The distinction therefore lies not merely in how measurements are interpreted, but
in the causal role assigned to measuring devices. Within the mechanistic paradigm,
measuring devices are ordinary physical systems whose behavior must itself be
explained by the same physical mechanisms affecting everything else. Within the
geometric paradigm, measuring devices provide the operational basis for defining

The order in which these concepts were developed is itself a consequence of the
mechanistic paradigm. Beginning from existence, simultaneity provides the founda-
tion for understanding time; time provides the foundation for understanding time

direction: operational measurement provides the starting point for the relativistic pos-

are deduced, while relativity of simultaneity emerges alongside them as a consequence
of the same relativistic structure. With these contrasting conceptual foundations
established, the mechanistic paradigm is now equipped to proceed from the observed
relativistic regularities toward the discovery of their physical causes.
2.5 Conclusion:Via ad Inventionem ab Inductione Incipit
This section has established the methodological and conceptual groundwork required
for mechanistic discovery. We first distinguished geometric description from mechanis-
tic explanation and showed that each scientific objective requires an epistemic method
appropriate to the claims it seeks to validate. Whereas the hypothetico–deductive
method provides a powerful means of constructing and validating mathemati-
cal descriptions against observation, validating physical causes ultimately requires
induction.
On this epistemic foundation, we established the foundational concepts of the
mechanistic paradigm: simultaneity as a fact of existence through universal presen-
tism, time as the interval over which change occurs, time dilation as the slowing of
physical processes, and measurement as a physical interaction involving measuring
systems that are themselves subject to causal influence. Contrasting each with its
geometric counterpart revealed that these conceptual differences reflect a deeper dis-
tinction in scientific objective. The geometric paradigm seeks to describe observed
regularities through spacetime geometry, whereas the mechanistic paradigm treats
those regularities as effects whose physical causes remain to be discovered.
The groundwork for causal discovery is therefore complete. Relativity already
provides extraordinarily successful quantitative predictions of the observational reg-
ularities under investigation; those predictions can consequently serve as empirical
constraints upon the mechanistic explanations developed here. The task ahead is not
to replace those successful predictions, but to determine what physical conditions pro-
duce the effects they describe. The following section begins this process by applying
Mill’s Methods of Agreement and Difference to isolate the physical condition respon-
sible for time dilation, establishing the first causal link in the mechanistic progression
toward the unification of time dilation, gravitation, and inertia.

3 Inducing the Cause of Time Dilation
In the preceding section, time dilation was defined within the mechanistic paradigm
as a real physical effect: the uniform slowing of physical processes such that the same
processes require longer intervals to complete. The task now is to discover the physical
condition responsible for that effect in both kinetic and gravitational contexts.
The investigation proceeds inductively using Mill’s Methods of Agreement and
Difference. Rather than assuming the relevant causal parameter at the outset, com-
peting candidates are progressively isolated and tested against the conditions under
which time dilation occurs. The analysis begins with kinetic time dilation, where the
physical conditions associated with time dilation are simplest, then extends to include
gravitational time dilation. Through this progression, a single causal relationship is
sought that accounts for time dilation across all cases.
Before this causal analysis can proceed, however, the observationally successful
predictions of relativity must first be placed within the mechanistic paradigm devel-
oped in the preceding section. This requires establishing why the geometric paradigm
cannot itself isolate the physical cause of time dilation, and how the same empiri-
cal predictions can instead provide the quantitative constraints required for causal
discovery.
3.1 Opening the Door to Causal Discovery
Relativity provides extraordinarily successful quantitative predictions of time dilation.
Within the geometric paradigm, these predictions are expressed through relationships
between spacetime intervals and require no additional physical mechanism acting upon
the systems involved. Once the observed regularities are accounted for by the metric,
the descriptive objective of the geometric paradigm has been satisfied. Within the
mechanistic paradigm, however, the same predictions acquire a different role. If time
dilation represents a real slowing of physical processes, then the predicted change
becomes an effect for which a physical cause remains to be discovered. Relativistic
predictions may therefore be retained as empirical constraints without requiring their
geometric interpretation to be retained with them.
The difficulty in discovering the physical cause of time dilation directly within the
relativistic framework arises from the symmetry built into its description. Mill’s Meth-
ods of Agreement and Difference require causal factors to correspond systematically
with the effect across the cases being compared so that those factors can be isolated.
In special relativity, however, the reciprocity of inertial frames prevents the effect
from being universally assigned to either system as a physical change. For two sys-
tems in uniform relative motion, either may regard itself as stationary and the other
as moving, and each assigns the corresponding time dilation effect to the other. More
generally, for any given system, a continuum of inertial frames exists with different
relative velocities, each assigning a different magnitude of time dilation to that sys-
tem. No inertial frame is privileged within the geometric paradigm as identifying the
system’s physical condition. Consequently, the reciprocal relationships alone cannot
universally identify which system is physically slowed or assign a unique magnitude to
15

===== PAGE 16 =====
that slowing as a physical property of the system. Without such agreement, the con-
ditions producing the effect cannot be varied and isolated in the manner required by
Mill’s Methods. An analogous obstacle to causal isolation arises in the gravitational
context and will be addressed when the analysis is extended to gravitational time
dilation. Thus, while the geometric paradigm successfully predicts time dilation, its
symmetries leave the causal conditions producing that effect underdetermined: within
the geometric paradigm, the door to causal discovery remains closed.
The key to opening that door lies within the mechanistic paradigm established
in the preceding section: the additional independent physical constraint supplied by
universal presentism removes this causal ambiguity. Universal presentism establishes
that all existence and interaction are confined to a single physically real present. Spe-
cial relativity, by contrast, derives relativity of simultaneity from its postulates, with
different inertial frames assigning different and mutually incompatible simultaneity
relations [33–35, 37]. There is only one physically real present, while the continuum
of inertial frames defines a corresponding continuum of different planes of simultane-
ity. These simultaneity relations cannot all correspond to the single present required
by universal presentism. Consequently, only one of these simultaneity relations can
correspond to physical reality.
Absent gravitational effects, the inertial frame possessing this simultaneity relation
is defined here as thepreferred frame: the inertial frame whose plane of simultaneity
corresponds to the physically real present. In gravitational settings, the same require-
ment generalizes to a preferred foliation whose simultaneity plane corresponds to the
universal present, together with the local preferred frame associated with that folia-
tion at each location. Where gravitational effects vanish, these local preferred frames
reduce to a single global preferred inertial frame.
Introducing such a frame immediately raises an empirical question: if one inertial
frame is physically preferred, why do measurements performed in uniformly mov-
ing frames continue to exhibit the observational equivalence predicted by special
relativity?
The answer follows from the mechanistic conception of measurement established
in the preceding section. In the absence of gravitational effects, the physical one-way
speed of light is isotropic in the preferred frame and anisotropic relative to frames
moving with respect to it. This physical anisotropy does not imply that observers in
those frames will measure an anisotropic speed of light. Measurements of the one-way
speed employing Einstein synchronization continue to returncin each direction, while
two-way measurements independently returnc. Because rods and clocks are themselves
physical systems, systematic changes in the measuring systems can compensate for
differences in the underlying physical quantities and thereby preserve these observed
invariances.
The null result of the Michelson–Morley experiment provides the classic experimen-
tal example of this measured two-way isotropy and therefore does not by itself exclude
a preferred frame. Lorentzian dynamical interpretations historically demonstrated how
physical changes to moving measuring systems can render a preferred frame empirically
indistinguishable through such measurements while reproducing the same observable
relationships described by special relativity [21, 22, 37, 38]. Appendix A develops the
16

===== PAGE 17 =====
corresponding mechanistic derivation used here, showing how the Lorentz transforma-
tions and the null Michelson–Morley result are recovered when the measuring systems
themselves are permitted to change.
The preferred frame is therefore an ontological reference frame without necessarily
being an operationally identifiable one. Directly identifying motion relative to it would
require access to a calibrated one-way speed of light without first assuming a synchro-
nization convention, which remains operationally inaccessible [39–41]. Its operational
inaccessibility does not prevent it from supplying the physical reference required for
causal analysis, just as the inability to identify that frame experimentally does not pre-
vent the Lorentz transformations from supplying empirically successful quantitative
predictions.
The result is precisely the combination required for causal discovery. The pre-
ferred frame does not remove the observational reciprocity captured by the Lorentz
transformations; it removes thecausal ambiguitythat prevents the physical conditions
associated with time dilation from being isolated. Motion relative to the preferred
frame supplies a nonreciprocal physical condition between otherwise observationally
equivalent systems, while the Lorentz transformations provide the quantitative rela-
tionships that any successful mechanistic explanation must reproduce. Mill’s Methods
can therefore be applied without abandoning the empirical predictions of special
relativity.
With these conditions established, causal discovery can now begin. We first con-
sider kinetic time dilation, where controlled comparisons between systems at rest and
in motion relative to the preferred frame allow competing causal candidates to be
progressively isolated and eliminated.
3.2 Evaluating Competing Hypotheses via Mill’s Methods
The causal analysis now proceeds by testing competing hypotheses against controlled
cases using Mill’s Methods of Agreement and Difference. Candidates inconsistent with
the presence, absence, or magnitude of time dilation are progressively eliminated, while
those remaining viable are refined and carried forward into increasingly general cases.
The plausible causal factors can be organized into three broad families. The first
concerns themotion of the system: whether time dilation is caused by relative motion
generally or by some specific property of motion with respect to the preferred frame.
The second concernschanges in the motion of the system: whether the effect orig-
inates during acceleration or in the physical action required to change the system’s
state of motion. The third concerns therate of causal interaction within the system:
whether changes in the rate at which interactions between spatially separated enti-
ties are completed alter the rate of physical change itself. Each family begins with its
simplest plausible candidate and is progressively refined as competing hypotheses are
eliminated.
We begin with kinetic time dilation, where these physical conditions can be
examined in their simplest form, before introducing gravitational effects to separate
candidates that remain observationally coupled in the kinetic case.

3.2.2 Gravitational Cases Only
The kinetic analysis left one viable candidate from each of the three causal fami-

provides the cases required to separate them.
Before these hypotheses can be tested, however, the causal ambiguity identified
previously for general relativity must be resolved. As in the kinetic case, the difficulty
is not a failure to predict elapsed proper time, but the absence within the geometric
paradigm of a uniquely identified amount of time dilation that can be assigned as a
physical property of a system in a given state.
The Gravitational Causal Ambiguity
Measurements such as the Shapiro time delay establish that the round-trip propagation
of light through a gravitational field requires more time than corresponding propa-
gation outside the field [42]. What such measurements do not establish is how that
additional propagation time is distributed between the two one-way directions. The
measured round-trip delay is therefore compatible with multiple underlying one-way
descriptions.
This freedom is reflected in different coordinate representations of the same grav-
itational system. In Schwarzschild coordinates, the radial coordinate speed of light
decreases symmetrically in the inward and outward directions (see Appendix C).
In Painlev’e–Gullstrand coordinates, the same gravitational system can instead
be represented by an asymmetric inward–outward propagation structure, as illus-
trated physically by the river model (see Appendix D). The inward-flowing and
outward-flowing Painlev’e–Gullstrand descriptions provide limiting alternatives, with
a continuum of intermediate choices between them.
These differences in one-way light speed are not merely differences in propagation
coordinates; they also determine how spatially separated clocks are synchronized and
therefore which distant events are assigned to the same simultaneity plane. They
also assign different amounts of time dilation to a given system in a given state of
motion, just as the continuum of inertial frames does in the special-relativistic case.
The physical conditions responsible for time dilation therefore cannot be uniquely
isolated within the geometric paradigm.

4 Unifying Time Dilation, Gravitation, and Inertia
The previous section established that time dilation, whether kinetic or gravitational,
is governed by the speed of causality,c⋆, an equivalent representation of the frequency
with which interaction cycles are completed between physically separated entities.
Time dilation was therefore shown to arise from a specific physical condition rather
than from geometry alone.
One immediate consequence of this result is that the internal motion of entities
undergoing time dilation must also slow. Whenever time dilation occurs, internal pro-
cesses take longer to complete relative to systems experiencing less time dilation, or
equivalently, they proceed more slowly. Because clocks are themselves physical sys-
tems whose operation depends upon those same internal processes, measurements of
time dilation necessarily reflect changes in internal motion.
Around gravitating bodies, these reductions in internal motion vary systematically
with position, implying the existence of a measurable field that modulates the local
value ofc ⋆ and, consequently, the rate of all physical change, including motion. The
physical agent responsible for producing this field remains unknown, much as Kepler
did not yet know what property of the Sun governed planetary motion [3], or Newton
what local mechanism produced gravitation itself [26]. What matters at this stage
is that the field’s effects are measurable and systematic. As the local value ofc ⋆
decreases, time dilation increases and internal processes slow proportionally. The field
therefore represents the present boundary between known effect and unknown cause
while simultaneously pointing toward the next frontier of discovery.
The purpose of this section is to establish a unified causal account of time dilation,
gravitation, and inertia. We proceed in four stages. First, we develop a scalar quantity
that characterizes the internal motion inferred from time dilation. This quantity, which
serves as an equivalent representation ofc ⋆, is identified as specific internal energy.
Second, we demonstrate that this scalar naturally reproduces the correct equations of
motion through a Lagrangian formulation, confirming that the induced mechanistic
quantities remain consistent with relativistic predictions. Third, guided by this pre-
dictive correspondence, we hypothesize that spatial gradients in this scalar constitute
the causal origin of both gravitation and inertia. In the fourth stage, we apply Mill’s
Methods of Agreement and Difference to the phenomena of weightedness and weight-
lessness to isolate the true causal factor inductively. Finally, having identified the
causal origin of gravitation and inertia, we induce the Equivalence Law, explaining the
physical basis for the observational regularity captured by the equivalence principle.

5 New Questions: Toward Quantum Gravity, a
Unified Field Theory, and a Theory of Everything
The preceding sections established a unified causal mechanism for time dilation, gravi-
tation, and inertia. Through axiomatic and causal induction, phenomena traditionally
treated as distinct were shown to arise from variations in a common physical quantity:
causal potential.
Historically, the identification of a common cause has rarely represented the end
of scientific inquiry. Instead, it has often marked the beginning of new avenues of
investigation.
Kepler’s laws revealed a previously unrecognized unity governing planetary motion,
motivating the question of what property of the Sun governed that motion [3]. New-
ton’s law of gravitation answered that question while simultaneously unifying celestial
and terrestrial motion, yet it left unanswered the question of the local mechanism
responsible for gravitational interaction itself [26]. In each case, unification transformed
previously independent phenomena into different manifestations of a common cause
while simultaneously revealing questions that could not previously be formulated.

===== PAGE 43 =====
The present work follows the same historical progression. It identifies a com-
mon causal mechanism for time dilation, gravitation, and inertia, thereby resolving
the question of the local mechanism responsible for gravitational interaction left
unresolved by Newton.
Yet, as with Kepler and Newton before it, this unification simultaneously reveals
new questions concerning the quantum origin of gravitation, the relationship between
gravitation and electromagnetism, the possibility of a unified force, and the potential
constitutional relationship between light and matter.
These questions should not be viewed as shortcomings of the present framework,
but as natural consequences of the unification it provides. From this foundation, four
major directions for future research emerge.
5.1 Toward Quantum Gravity
One of the most intriguing questions raised by the present framework concerns the
relationship between gravitation and quantum theory.
The preceding analysis identified gravitation as a consequence of variations in
causal potential, itself determined by variations in the speed of causality,c ⋆, which
is represented through the local propagation of light. Because light is fundamentally
quantum in nature [46–51], it is natural to ask whether the Velocity Damping Field
responsible for these variations might also originate from quantum processes.
This possibility suggests a different perspective on the quantum-gravity prob-
lem. Rather than asking how to quantize spacetime itself, one may instead ask what
quantum processes govern local variations in the speed of causality.
If quantum processes influence light propagation, and if light propagation deter-
mines the local speed of causality, then quantum fluctuations affecting light may also
induce fluctuations in causal potential and, consequently, in gravitation itself.
Within this framework, the central challenge of quantum gravity becomes iden-
tifying the quantum origin of the Velocity Damping Field and determining how
microscopic variations in causal propagation give rise to the macroscopic phenomena
associated with gravitation.
Whether such a connection ultimately proves correct remains an open question.
Nevertheless, the unification developed in the present work permits a question that
could not previously be formulated: might gravitation itself emerge from the quantum
behavior of light? The mechanistic relationship established between time dilation,
gravitation, and causal propagation suggests that this possibility warrants further
investigation.
5.2 Toward a Unified Field Theory
The present framework also raises the possibility of a deeper relationship between
gravitation and electromagnetism.
Traditionally, these interactions have been treated as fundamentally distinct. Elec-
tromagnetic interactions are described by electromagnetic fields, whose radiative
excitations propagate as light, whereas gravitation is typically described either as a
43

===== PAGE 44 =====
fundamental interaction or as a consequence of spacetime geometry. The mechanistic
interpretation developed here suggests a different possibility.
The preceding analysis identified gravitation as a consequence of variations in
causal potential, itself determined by variations in the speed of causality. Both are
alternative representations of changes in the local propagation of light. If light par-
ticipates in the mechanism governing gravitation, then a natural question emerges:
might gravitation and electromagnetism represent different manifestations of the same
underlying interaction?
An intriguing physical distinction motivates this possibility. Radiative electro-
magnetic fields possess transverse degrees of freedom relative to their direction of
propagation, whereas the gravitational effects identified in the present framework arise
through longitudinal variations in causal propagation. This distinction suggests that
these phenomena may correspond to different physical aspects of a deeper causal
process.
This possibility motivates the development of an electromagnetic–gravitational
framework in which both interactions are treated within a common causal model.
Unlike gravitoelectromagnetism (GEM), which establishes mathematical analogies
between the two interactions [52], such an approach would seek a unified causal
account.
Interestingly, one self-consistent extension of GEM known to the author employs
complex-valued representations to preserve the orthogonality between gravitational
and electromagnetic contributions while expressing both within a common mathemat-
ical framework [53]. A summary of this formulation is provided in Appendix K.
Einstein devoted considerable effort to the development of a unified field the-
ory combining gravitation and electromagnetism [54–59]. Yet, without an identified
causal mechanism connecting these phenomena, the path toward unification remained
unclear.
Whether a mechanistic framework based on causal potential can ultimately achieve
this goal remains an open question. Nevertheless, the present analysis identifies a
previously unexplored causal relationship between gravitation and light, suggesting a
new conceptual direction for future investigation.
5.3 Toward a Theory of Everything
If an electromagnetic–gravitational unification were ultimately confirmed, then an
even broader question would naturally follow: could all known interactions arise from
different manifestations of that same underlying mechanism?
Within the present framework, this possibility emerges from a simple observation.
Within this speculative picture, radiative electromagnetic supply transverse degrees
of freedom, whereas gravitation is associated with longitudinal variations in causal
propagation. Together, these effects span all mutually orthogonal directions in three-
dimensional space.
If these phenomena are indeed different manifestations of the same mechanism,
then it is natural to ask whether this directional decomposition already exhausts the
possible forms that a force can assume. If so, the traditional classification of the
fundamental interactions may require reconsideration.
44

===== PAGE 45 =====
As a deliberately speculative example of the type of unification this possibility
would invite, one might ask whether one possible interpretation is that the strong
and weak nuclear interactions represent limiting cases of the same underlying mech-
anism. In these regimes, certain components may become dominant while others are
suppressed.
For example, the strong interaction might correspond to a regime in which magnetic
and gravitational effects dominate while electric effects become negligible. Conversely,
the weak interaction might correspond to a regime in which electric and gravitational
effects dominate while magnetic effects are suppressed. Conventional electromagnetism
would then represent the complementary case in which gravitational effects become
negligible.
Under such an interpretation, the apparent diversity of interactions would arise not
because nature employs multiple fundamentally different force carriers, but because
different physical regimes selectively emphasize different components of the same
underlying mechanism.
Whether this possibility proves correct remains entirely speculative. Nevertheless,
the mechanistic framework developed in the present work raises a question that could
not previously be formulated: might all known interactions ultimately be different
expressions of the same mechanism responsible for electromagnetism and gravity?
If so, the search for a theory of everything may shift away from identifying addi-
tional fundamental interactions and toward understanding how this single underlying
mechanism gives rise to the diverse physical phenomena observed in nature.
5.4 Toward a Constitutional Theory of Everything
The preceding discussion considered the possibility that gravitation, electromagnetism,
and perhaps even all known interactions arise from a common mechanism. A deeper
question naturally follows.
If variations in the speed of causality govern both the propagation of light and the
motion of material bodies, why should this be so? Why should changes in the local
propagation of light influence the behavior of matter at all?
One possible answer is that light and matter are not fundamentally different cat-
egories of physical existence. Instead, they may represent different forms of the same
constituent entity.
This possibility is motivated by the pervasive role of light throughout physics. Elec-
tromagnetic radiation participates in the formation, deconstruction, transformation,
and interaction of matter across a wide range of physical phenomena. The behavior
of charged particles is inseparable from electromagnetic interactions, particle creation
and annihilation involve electromagnetic processes, and light participates in both the
interactions and transformations of physical systems.
If light and matter share a common constitution, then the distinction between
them may arise not from what they are, but from how the same constituent entity is
organized.
Within such a picture, matter may correspond to stable, self-sustaining wave con-
figurations, including standing waves, solitons, or other localized nonlinear excitations.
45

===== PAGE 46 =====
Particles would then be understood not as fundamental point-like objects, but as per-
sistent configurations whose properties emerge from the same physical processes that
govern the propagation of light.
This possibility has been explored in soliton-based models of elementary particles,
where particles are treated as localized oscillatory solutions and their properties emerge
from their internal dynamics [60]. More generally, nonlinear wave systems are known
to support stable, particle-like solutions [61]. These results suggest that the distinction
between particles and waves may reflect different organizations of the same physical
substance rather than fundamentally different physical entities.
If this interpretation proves correct, then variations in the speed of causality would
simultaneously influence both light and matter because both would arise from the
same physical constitution.
Whether such a constitutional theory ultimately proves viable remains entirely
speculative. Nevertheless, the mechanistic framework developed in the present work
raises a question that could not previously be formulated: might light and matter be
constituted from the same physical substance, differing only in form?

I.1 The Clock-Rate Gradient of Born-Rigid Acceleration

J Equivalence of Time-Dilation Gradients:
Weak-Field Gravity and Born-Rigid Acceleration
This appendix establishes the local equivalence between the time-dilation gradients
produced by two distinct physical situations:
1. a static weak gravitational field evaluated across a small radial interval; and
2. a Born-rigid accelerating system in flat spacetime.
The comparison is made at the level of the measurable clock-rate gradient: the
spatial variation in the ratio of clock intervals measured at neighboring locations. The
mathematical equivalence can therefore be established independently of any particular
interpretation of the physical mechanism responsible for that gradient.
Within Universal Specificity, the shared clock-rate gradient is interpreted as a
shared spatial variation in the rate of causal processes and, consequently, in causal
potential. This appendix establishes the mathematical equivalence of those gradients;
their causal interpretation is developed in the main text.
Throughout this appendix, primed quantities denote uncalibrated operational mea-
surements made by instruments comoving with the system under consideration. These
clocks, rulers, and accelerometers are themselves physically affected by time dilation,
length contraction, and acceleration. Their readings are therefore directly observable
proper quantities, but they have not been corrected back to preferred-frame calibrated
coordinates.

spatially perpendicular.
Within the mechanistic interpretation explored in this work, gravitation and
electromagnetism may act differently upon the propagation and motion of physical
systems. The complex representation developed here provides one possible mathemati-
cal language for representing such distinct contributions while retaining their common
field-equation form. The existence of this representation alone, however, does not
establish the physical mechanism connecting the two interactions.
The result should also be distinguished from the statement that the full non-
linear gravitational field equations have been unified with electromagnetism. The
construction applies to the gravitoelectromagnetic field equations adopted above and
demonstrates a formal correspondence with Maxwell’s equations after the stated
rescaling.
