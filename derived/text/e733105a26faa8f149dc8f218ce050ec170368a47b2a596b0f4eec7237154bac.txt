Gauge Freedom, Quartic Indeterminacy, and Truncation Artifacts

Abstract
Sulfuryl fluoride, SO2F2, is a quasi-spherical asymmetric top with rotational constantsA≈B≈C
and an exceptionally small sphericity parameter,γ = 2(C−B)/(C +B)≈− 0.003. Within Wat-
son’s theory, only five independent combinations of the six quartic centrifugal-distortion coeffi-
cients are spectroscopically determinable. However, the conventionalA- andS-reductions, defined
by unitary transformation ˆU = exp(i ˆS3(s111)), become poorly conditioned. Sarka et al. (2000)
argued that, near the spherical-top limit, the small rotational denominators invalidate the conven-
tionalA- andS-reduction constraints and leave the six quartic coefficients only marginally affected
by an admissibly small contact transformation; they therefore concluded that all six quartic (and
nine sextic) centrifugal-distortion constants are spectroscopically determinable. We re-evaluate
this statement and show that the contact transformation generates a one-dimensional gauge orbit
along which the rotational constants and quartic coefficients change, while the spectrum remains
invariant through the first order. We further show that the proposed conditionP′
006≈ 0 to deter-
mines111 is not justified because the omitted contributions are required for the exact cancellation.
The observed narrow uncertainties are conditional on the sextic constrainth1 = 0. Furthermore,
we derive a first-order invariantR5 +2σR6 =CR5R6, whereσ = (2Bz−Bx−By)/(Bx−By). Theoret-
ical conclusions are supported by numerical tests, demonstrating consistent change in six quartic
constants with alteration of the asymmetry parameterκ. Finally, the ratioσ provides a first-order
conditioning criterion:|σ|≪ 1 favors theA-reduction, whereas|σ|≫ 1 favors theS-reduction. For
SO2F2 in theIr representation,σ = 8.7 selects theS-reduction.

The present article is aiming at explaining the controversy between Sarkaet al.’s propositions98
and the conventional theory of rotational reduction, developed by Watson. Watson’s approach99
is based on formal application of the rotational perturbation theory and the hypervirial theorem100
[1]; he never considered the underlying fundamental physics of a rotating flexible molecule and101
principal indeterminacy, that appears due to the so-called ‘gauge freedom’ of choosing a molecule-102
fixed frame when a molecule is distorted by centrifugal forces. It is gauge freedom that permits103
five quartic, seven sextic, nine octic, etc. constants being rigorously determinable at correspond-104
ing orders of perturbation theory, while a practical application of the perturbation theory may105
be subject to various numerical situations, that may create illusions of a failure of underlying106
physics. Here, we will briefly mention principal objections against the system of Sarkaet al.’s107
argumentation and in the main part of this work we will present a more systematic proofs of108
physical considerations and mathematical facts that refute certain statements of Ref. [13].109
First, Watson’s unitary transformationˆH′ = ˆH +i[ ˆS3, ˆH0], where ˆS3 =s111( ˆJx ˆJy ˆJz + ˆJz ˆJy ˆJx)110
can be considered as a generator of an infinitesimal ‘wobbling’ (tilting) of the coordinate frame. It111
reflects the fact that some manifestations of centrifugal distortion are physically indistinguishable112
from a smallJ-dependent rotation of the coordinate frame used to build the theoretical model.113
This indeterminacy of the coordinate frame, calledgauge freedom, ensures that among six quartic114
centrifugal distortion parameters there is always one strict linear dependence and by choosing115
a specific value ofs111 it is possible to isolate one constant and fit five other truly physically116
meaningful constants.117
Second, the uncertaintiesσ are derived from the diagonal elements of the Covariance Matrix118
C = (J⊤W J)−1, which is the inverse of the approximated Hessian matrix. Introducing an artificial119
constraint to the sextic constanth1 artificially breaks the singularity, while the least squares fitting120
procedure (LSQ) still compensates the truncation error with all six quartic and the remaining121
sextic constants.122
Lastly, an ab initio calculation of the harmonic and anharmonic force fields in a specific123
molecule-fixed geometric frame (usually the PAS) evaluates unreduced centrifugal distortion con-124
stants without applying any rotational contact transformations. Sarkaet al. used an ab initio125
force field calculation as an independent test of the six quartic constants obtained from their126
6-reduction fit [13] and the reported absolute relative discrepancies were within15%. Together127
with the finite formal uncertainties of all six fitted constants, the normal quartic magnitude of128
R5, and its substantial contribution to the measured transition frequencies, this agreement was129
interpreted as an independent confirmation that all six quartic constants were physically valid and130
spectroscopically determinable.131

2. Theory163
The theory of reducing effective rotational Hamiltonians was developed in 1960s’-1970s’ by164
Watson in a fundamental series of works [1–4, 23, 27], likely inspired by an earlier work of Drei-165
zler and Dendl (1965) [25], which provided an evidence of the existing linear dependence among166
centrifugal distortion parametersA1...A 6, appearing in the Kivelson and Wilson (1952) [26] ex-167
pression Wr(W0,A 1...A 6) for a rigid rotor energy, perturbed in the first order by six quartic168
terms. It should be noted thatWr was suspected to have linear dependencies even prior to work169
[25]: Hill and Edwards (1964) [28] admitted an existence of a linear dependence between a related170
quartic representation via Nielsen’s constantsDJ,DJK,DK,δJ,R 5,R 6 [29]. Soon afterwards Wat-171
son explained [1, 3] this effect and proposed the solution by unitary transforming (A-reduction)172
the rotational Hamiltonian [2]. Since then, using of rotational Hamiltonians became a cornerstone173
of high-resolution rotational and vibration-rotation spectroscopy.174

5. Conclusions860
The theory of reduction of effective rotational Hamiltonians, developed by Watson [1–4, 23],861
stands as one of the foundational cornerstones of high-resolution molecular spectroscopy, because862
it connects basic molecular properties, such as geometry, masses and PES, to compact oper-863
ator expressions, suitable for their precise fitting to experimental MW and high-resolution IR864
spectroscopic data. This theory relies on the well-converged, power-ordered sequence of unitary865
transformationsUn = exp(i ˆS2n+1), that gradually transforms a full theoretical rotational Hamilto-866
nian of an asymmetric top, parameterized by the complete model sets of parameters in Cartesian867
or cylindrical representation, to a reduced form with lower, experimentally determinable number868
of constants (five quartic, seven sextic, etc.). Although Watson’s straightforward theory (A-869
reduction) is normally well conditioned for a typical semi-rigid molecule, cases of nearly-prolate or870
oblate limits require an alteration of the scheme (S-reduction). However, for a rare case of quasi-871
spherical tops, the denominators, defining the conventional reduction parameters may become872
abnormally small, producing large transformation parameters, which results in poor convergence873
of the perturbative expansion.874
