Abstract
This dissertation focuses on the Ollivier Ricci curvature ion a graph. This is a discrete notion
of edge-based curvature that can be formulated in terms of an optimal transport problem.
The first part of the report introduces the definition and discusses some of its main properties.
The second part of the report focuses on the Ollivier Ricci Flow, an iterative process which
uses the values of the curvature to try to find a set of edge weights that make the curvature
of the graph constant. We present a graph for which this algorithm doesn’t converge and
explain experimentally and theoretically the reason why. Finally, we study the robustness of
the Ollivier Ricci curvature and the Ollivier Ricci Flow to noisy data inputs using simulations
in Python.

1 Introduction
Complex networks are now fundamental objects in many scientific fields. They can be found in
everything from financial markets, roads and layout of cities, drug discovery, social networks and
more. These types of datasets can be mathematically phrased as a set of nodes, items, connected
together by edges. Significant effort has been put to develop novel and powerful techniques to
analyse these objects. A prominent approach has been to use geometrical information of the
network, such as curvature, to understand its underlying structure.
Curvature is usually a notion found on the context of differential manifolds. There are several
notions of curvature that can be defined at a point, some of them being more complex and
sophisticated than others, but they all aim to quantify the deviation of an object from being
flat. Typically the values are defined to be large when the object deviates strongly from its flat
counterpart, and to be close to zero when it is nearly flat. In the image below there is an example
of surfaces with positive, zero and negative Ricci curvature.
Discrete spaces don’t have a differential structure, but recent research has shown that the concept
of curvature can be transferred to the discrete setting by using analogies with the continuous
case. There are multiple notions of discrete curvature that have been defined in the last few
years, each of them revealing different properties of the network. This dissertation focuses on
the Ollivier Ricci Curvature. This is an edge based curvature that can be formulated in terms
of an optimal transportation problem. This curvature has received special attention due to
its success in identifying community structures within a graph. Broadly speaking, edges with
positive curvature tend to represent edges within a cluster, while negatively curved edge tend
to be the bridges within communities. This property potentially allows to identify communities
within a graph using intrinsic properties of the network only, without the need of any embedding
techniques.
The first part of this report focuses on the Ollivier Ricci Curvature. We provide its definition
and explain its connection to the Ricci Curvature defined in manifolds. We then expose some
experimental results obtained regarding regular graphs with constant curvature. On the second
part we introduce the Ollivier Ricci Flow, an iterative process that aims to flatten the curvature
of a graph. We explain the methodology behind the algorithm and show an example of the
Ollivier Ricci Flow implemented in a real-world dataset. We then focus on the study of the
convergence of this algorithm. We prove experimentally and theoretically that the algorithm
applied to a graph consisting of two triangles glued together by and edge doesn’t converge. To
conclude the dissertation, we provide some potential future lines of research.

2.2 Connection to Ricci Curvature in a manifold
In this subsection, we provide an intuition on the connection between the Ollivier Ricci Curvature
and the Ricci Curvature defined in Riemannian manifolds. We will not provide the formal
definition of Ricci Curvature in a manifold, but we will introduce one of its main characterisations:
Let x and y denote two close points in a manifoldM of dimension n, and let d(x,y) be the distance
along a geodesic γ joining these two points. We can then take a vector v∈ TxM and parallel
transport it along γ to a vector v’∈ TyM. Informally, to parallel transport a vector along
a curve means to transport it without changing its magnitude and direction (We would need
to introduce the concept of Levi-Civita connection for a formal definition). We then project
x along v back to the manifold again to obtain x’ and project y along v’ to obtain y’, and
calculate the distance between these two points along a geodesic. We repeat this procedure
for every possible direction of v and v’, constructing an infinitesimal balls Bϵ(x) and Bϵ(y)
centered at x and y respectively. It is well known that the average distance between x and y’ is

3 Ollivier Ricci Flow
The Ollivier Ricci Flow is an iterative process that modifies the edge weights to try to flatten
the curvature of the graph. Broadly speaking, edges with positive curvature will shrink (the
edge weight will decrease) and the edges with negative curvature will expand (the edge weight
will increase). This property has found important applications in community detection, as nodes
within the same community will get closer to each other, while moving away from nodes belonging
to other communities. The Ollivier Ricci Flow has also been used in other contexts, such as
to study the fragility of financial markets through the analysis of the correlation between the
robustness of a graph and its curvature [6], to study information propagation on a graph and
its applications to the over-squashing phenomenon of Graph Neural Networks [7], to study the
internet topology [8], and many more.
To give an intuitive example on the idea behind the algorithm, we have implemented the Ollivier
Ricci Flow to the Zachary’s Karate Club dataset [9]. This is a famous social network used broadly
in network analysis. It is well-known that this dataset contains two communities. In the image
below the weights and curvature are analyzed before and after implementing the Ricci Flow. We
are considering the unweighted version of the data, so before implementing the Ricci Flow, all
the weights are 1 (lower left diagram) and there is a mixture of curvatures ranging from -2 to 1
(upper left diagram). After the Ricci Flow, all the edge curvatures have converged to 0 (upper
right diagram), while the weights now range from 0 to 7 (lower right diagram). Notice that the
weight of the edges that initially had a negative curvature has increased after the Ricci Flow
(expanded), while the others have decreased (shrinked). The two communities can be clearly
identified after the Ricci Flow from the lower right diagram, and correspond to the true values
of the data. This is just an intuitive example on some properties of the algorithm, a more in
depth analysis will follow in the next sections.

3.2 Convergence of Ricci Flow
The convergence of the Ricci Flow is of great importance when applying the algorithm to real-
world datasets. Even though a number of experimental analyses have been carried out, no
theoretical guarantees about its convergence has been obtained yet. This is a particularly difficult
task due to the following factors:
1. In general, there is no explicit formula for the Wasserstein distance between masses distri-
butions around adjacent edges and, hence, for the curvature of an edge.
2. The normalization factor implemented at the end of every iteration of the Ricci Flow avoids
expansion of the weights to infinity, but makes the analysis significantly more complicated.
This is because even though the Wasserstein distance between masses distribution around
adjacent nodes may decrease from one iteration to another, the weight of the edge can still
increase if the sum of the total Wasserstein distance from all the edges of the graph has
also decreased. Therefore, because of the normalization factor, the weight of an edge at
the end of one iteration doesn’t depend only on the local neighborhood around the edge,
but on the global structure of the graph.
3. The shortest path between two nodes can change as the weight of the edges changes, making
difficult (or impossible) to track what is the optimal path between two nodes at a given
time.

3.2.1 Non-Convergence of the Ollivier Ricci Flow
So far, there are no examples in literature of non-convergence of the Ollivier Ricci Flow. We

a bouncing can occur between different optimal paths joining two nodes. In this section, we

3.2.4 Interesting points and future work on the convergence of the Ricci Flow
Along the experimental and theoretical analysis we have come across some points that would be
interesting to investigate in a future work.
1. Throughout out the theoretical analysis we have noticed that the weights only have a
direction towards eigenvectors associated with real and positive eigenvalues. Negative and
complex eigenvalues could create another type of bouncing on the edge weights that could
prevent convergence. We believe that the there is no feasible optimal transport plan that
will give rise to this situation, but it would be interesting to understand theoretically the
reason why.
2. In this dissertation, we have only proved the non-convergence of the Ricci Flow for the
specific graph G shown in Figure 8. Nevertheless, this is a behaviour that we have seen in
other graphs containing a high number of triangles. In Figure 15 there are some examples
of non-convergence of the Ricci Flow that we came across during the experimental analysis.
An interesting future work will be to study if it is possible to construct an arbitrarily large
graph exhibiting this phenomena.
Figure 15: Other examples of non-convergence of Ollivier Ricci Flow
3. We have only proved non-convergence when using the mass distribution defined in 7, which
is not the one used for practical purposes as we discussed earlier. Experimentally, we were
able to check that using the continuous mass distribution from Algorithm 1, the Ricci
Flow applied to G doesn’t converge for p >5. This is because for large values of p, the
continuous mass distribution defined in Algorithm 1 is similar enough to the step function
we have been working on (7). It could be interesting to extend our proof for the continuous
version of the mass distribution. Our proof could be adapted by adding a small portion of
mass δ along the edges with negative curvature and analyzing the convergence using the
same techniques. This approach will only work as long as we can express the problem as a
system of picewise linear maps, which as we already discussed, we were not able to prove
for the general case.

4 Robustness of Ollivier Ricci Curvature and Ricci Flow
In order to be able to use the Ollivier Ricci Curvature and Ollivier Ricci Flow in real-world
applications it is essential for the algorithms to be robust to noise in the input data. To study the
robustness of these algorithms we will apply Gaussian noise to the weights of an unweighted graph
and study how sensible the algorithms are to those changes. We decided to use Gaussian noise
as this represents small random fluctuations in the data that may arise in real-world datasets. In
this case, we will use a truncated version of the normal distribution as we don’t want the weights
to be negative, nor to be larger than the number of edges in the graph. In particular, the noise
will follow the below normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation σ.

In order to evaluate the robustness of the algorithms, we will express the problem in terms of a
classification problem and look at the accuracy. That is, when working with the Ollivier Ricci
Curvature, we will first compute the curvature of the edges without the noise. The edges with
positive curvature will belong to one group (call it 0) and the edges with negative curvature will
belong to the other group (call it 1). We will then apply the noise to the input weights of the
graph and look how many edges have been classified correctly. We will repeat this procedure for
different values of standard deviationσ. Our intuition is that as the standard deviation increases,
the accuracy will decrease. For the Ollivier Ricci Flow, the methodology is the same, but we
define one group as being the set of edges with weight smaller than 1 after the implementation
of the Ricci Flow (call it 0) and the other group as the set of edges with weight larger or equal
than 1 after the implementation of Ricci Flow (call it 1). Finally, the accuracy of the algorithm
is defined as follows:
Accuracy = Number of correct classifications
Total number of classifications
Notice that this is a simplification of the problem, as we are not considering how much has the
weight of a noisy edge changed with the Ricci Flow compared to its non noisy counterpart. We
are only analyzing if the general behaviour of the algorithms (positive/negative curvature and
shrinked/expanded edges) is the same.
The dataset that we have used to run our experiments is a random generated network from
Python following a Watts–Strogatz model. This model generates graphs with small-world prop-
erties. These are types of graphs with small clustering coefficients and that have been found to
represent well the dynamics of social networks [12]. The graph we have used contains 200 nodes
and 1000 edges. We are not working with larger graphs due to the computational costs of the
Ricci Flow.

Flow (left) when a Gaussian noise with standard deviation 4 is applied to the initial weights.
The algorithm classified most of the edges with initial positive curvature in the wrong group,
indicating that these could be the more unstable edges. In the other hand, the Ricci Flow
managed to classify most of the edges that initially shrinked (weight smaller than 1) correctly.
Overall, the confusion matrices indicate that the Ricci Flow has managed to classify most of the
edges in the correct group, while the Ricci Curvature seems to be more sensible to noisy data.

5 Conclusion
In this report we studied the Ollivier Ricci Curvature and the Ollivier Ricci Flow.
We first studied the definition and properties of the Ollivier Ricci Curvature, and its connection
to the Ricci Curvature defined in a manifold. We were able to understand how this notion give
us information about the structure of a graph and how can this be used in network analysis
applications. We were able to experimentally find regular graphs with constant curvature up to
and including 11 vertices using random generator models in Python.
We then studied the Ollivier Ricci Flow. We were able to understand the algorithm used in
the research paper Community detection on networks with Ricci flow [10] and explore potential
applications to community detection. We then proved experimentally and theoretically that
the Ollivier Ricci Flow doesn’t converge by giving a counterexample. Finally, we implemented
Python simulations to understand how robust are those notions to noisy dataset, a fundamental
property to be understood for real-world applications. Our results showed that the Ricci Flow
appears to be a robust algorithm, even for large value of noise, while the curvature seems to me
more sensible to changes in the input data. A more in depth analysis should be carried out with
different types of network and different parameters of the Ricci Flow to understand better this
behaviour.
This project also gave rise to numerous future lines of research that we described in Section 3.2.4.
