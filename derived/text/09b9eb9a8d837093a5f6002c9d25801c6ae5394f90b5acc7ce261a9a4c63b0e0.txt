A Unified Framework for Quantum Mechanics and Relativity 
The Interface Between Quantum States and Spacetime Geometry 
 
 
Juliet Zhong 
Independent Researcher, London, United Kingdom, July 2026 
ORCID: 0009-0006-5099-3671 
 
 
Abstract 
Quantum mechanics and general relativity are the two most successful physical theories of the 
twentieth century, yet their conceptual frameworks appear deeply incompatible. The standard 
formulation of this incompatibility holds that quantum superposition of matter  states generates an 
indeterminate energy-momentum tensor, which cannot serve as a unique source for the Einstein field 
equations, requiring either the quantisation of spacetime geometry or some other fundamental 
modification of existing theory. This paper  argues that the apparent incompatibility arises, at least in 
part, from a category error: the simultaneous application of two descriptions —quantum and 
geometric—that properly apply at different levels of physical description. Quantum mechanics 
describes the probability structure of possible configurations of a physical system; general relativity 
describes the geometric structure of spacetime associated with definite configurations of matter. These 
are not competing descriptions of the same physical moment; they are descriptions of different aspects 
of the same physical situation. On this account, quantum superposition does not entail a corresponding 
multiplicity of gravitational sources, because the quantum state describes possible configurations of a 
single physical system rather than multiple simultaneously realised classical matter distributions. The 
paper develops this conceptual interface, situates it relative to existing approaches to quantum gravity, 
and argues that the apparent conflict is better und erstood as a problem of descriptive level -mismatch 
than of fundamental contradiction. To this end, it proposes a staged mapping, |ψ⟩ → Ci → Tμν(Ci) → 
Gμν, as the formal interface that any future unified theory must formalise. 
 
Keywords: quantum gravity , general relativity , quantum mechanics , energy-momentum tensor ,

measurement problem, conceptual unification

Chapter 1: The Nature of the Conflict 
 
 
1.1 Two Theories, One Problem 
Quantum mechanics and general relativity are the two most successful physical theories produced by 
the twentieth century. Each has been confirmed by experiment to a degree that places them beyond 
serious empirical challenge within their respective domains.  Quantum mechanics accounts for the 
behaviour of matter at atomic and subatomic scales with a precision that has no parallel in the history 
of science: the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron, predicted by quantum electrodynamics, 
agrees with experiment to better than one part in a trillion [1]. General relativity, for its part, describes 
the large-scale structure of spacetime and the dynamics of gravitational fields with comparable accuracy: 
from the precession of Mercury's perihelion to the detection of gravitational waves from merging black 
holes, its predictions have been confirmed wherever they have been tested [2]. No experiment has found 
either theory to be wrong on its own terms. 
Yet the two theories are not compatible with each other. This is not a matter of one contradicting the 
experimental record of the other. It is a matter of their conceptual structures —the basic objects they 
define, the questions they are designed to answer, and the mathematical formalisms through which they 
answer those questions—being mutually inconsistent in ways that become acute precisely where both 
should apply simultaneously: at the Planck scale, at gravitational singularities, in the early universe, 
and at the horizon of black holes. In these regimes, both quantum effects and strong gravitational 
curvature are relevant, and no consistent theoretical description currently exists that incorporates both. 
The standard account of this incompatibility identifies several specific sources of tension. General 
relativity is a classical field theory: it describes spacetime as a smooth, continuous four -dimensional 
Lorentzian manifold, and the gravitational field as the curvature of that manifold, determined at every 
point by the distribution of matter and energy through the Einstein field equations. The theory is 
deterministic in the sense that, given the state of the universe on a spacelike hypersurface, the future  
evolution of the gravitational field is uniquely determined [3]. There is no place in this picture for 
probability, superposition, or the kind of fundamental indeterminacy that quantum mechanics assigns 
to physical systems before measurement. 
Quantum mechanics, by contrast, describes physical systems through state vectors in a Hilbert 
space—mathematical objects that encode not the definite values of physical quantities but the 
probability distributions over possible values that would result from measurement. The wave function

does not necessarily imply that a quantum system possesses a definite position, momentum, energy, or 
spin as a simultaneously realised classical property prior to measurement. It exists in a superposition of 
possible states, each with an associated probability amplitude, and only upon interaction with a 
measuring apparatus does the system acquire a definite value for the measured quantity. This is not 
merely an expression of i gnorance about an underlying classical reality: within the standard textbook 
formulation of quantum mechanics, the superposition is treated as the complete mathematical 
description of the system prior to measurement [4]. 
The deepest point of conflict between these two pictures concerns the gravitational field and its 
relationship to the quantum state of matter. In general relativity, the curvature of spacetime —the 
gravitational field —is determined by the energy -momentum tensor of matter. The Einstein field 
equations state this relationship explicitly: the geometry of spacetime on the left -hand side of the 
equations is equated to the energy -momentum content of matter on the right -hand side [5]. The 
geometry is therefore as definite and classically determinate as the matter distribution that sources it. 
But if matter can exist in quantum superposition —if its energy and momentum are genuinely 
indeterminate prior to measurement—then the energy-momentum tensor that sources the gravitational 
field is itself indeterminate. And if the source of the gravitational field is indeterminate, the question 
arises immediately and unavoidably: what is the state of the gravitational field? 
This is the precise formulation of the problem that the present paper addresses. It is not the vague 
intuition that one theory involves probability and the other does not. It is the specific observation that 
quantum mechanics permits—indeed, requires—that the source term of the Einstein field equations be 
in a state of superposition, while the field equations themselves have no provision for a superposed or 
probabilistic spacetime geometry. The two formalisms make mutually inconsistent demands on the 
same p hysical quantity: the energy -momentum distribution of matter. This inconsistency cannot be 
resolved by applying one theory first and then the other; it is a structural incompatibility that arises at 
the level of their fundamental concepts. 
The conventional response to this problem has been to seek a new theory —quantum gravity—that 
supersedes both general relativity and quantum mechanics in the regime where both are relevant, 
recovering each as a limiting case. This programme has produced several major research directions: 
string theory, loop quantum gravity, causal dynamical triangulations, and others [6]. Each attempts, in 
different ways, to construct a theory in which spacetime itself has quantum properties, so that the 
inconsistency between a quantum matter source and a classical spacetime geometry is resolved by 
eliminating the classical spacetime altog ether and replacing it with something more fundamental [7]. 
These approaches are intellectually serious and mathematically sophisticated. None has yet produced a 
theory that is empirically confirmed, and each faces deep conceptual and technical difficulties that 
remain unresolved [8]. 
The present paper does not propose a new quantum gravity theory. Its aim is different and, in a sense, 
prior: to examine whether the apparent incompatibility between quantum mechanics and general

relativity is as fundamental as it appears, or whether it arises, at least in part, from a conceptual

and that recognising this distinction allows their conceptual frameworks to coexist without

1.2 Two Levels of Description 
The claim that quantum mechanics and general relativity are incompatible rests on the assumption 
that both theories are attempting to describe the same physical object in the same way —that both are, 
so to speak, answering the same question about the same thing. This assumption is rarely made explicit, 
and it is worth examining it directly, because it is not obviously correct. 
Every physical theory is, among other things, a description of something. It selects certain aspects of 
the physical world as its object of description, defines the concepts through which that object is 
characterised, and specifies the relationships between those concepts that constitute its laws. Different 
theories may describe different aspects of the same underlying reality, or they may describe the same 
aspect at different levels of resolution, or they may describe the same aspect using different but 
equivalent conceptual vocabularies. In none of these cases does the existence of two different theories 
imply that they are in conflict; conflict arises only when two theories purport to give different answers 
to the same question about the same object. 
The question, then, is what question each theory is answering. 
General relativity answers a specific set of questions about the structure of spacetime and the 
relationships between events. Given a distribution of matter and energy, it specifies the curvature of 
spacetime in which that matter moves. Given the curvature of spacetime, it specifies how matter moves 
through it. It describes the causal structure of spacetime —which events can influence which other 
events, and through what paths. It describes the propagation of light, the behaviour of clocks and 
measuring rods in gravitational fields, and the large -scale dynamics of the universe. The objects that 
general relativity takes as fundamental are events, spacetime intervals, geodesics, and the metric tensor. 
Its laws are equations relating these objects [9] . The question it answers, at the deepest level, is: given 
the matter content of the universe, what is the structure of spacetime, and how do objects move through 
it? 
Quantum mechanics answers a different set of questions. Given a physical system and an initial 
preparation, it specifies the probabilities of obtaining various outcomes when measurements of physical 
quantities are performed on that system. It describes how the quantum state of a system evolves in time 
between measurements, and how it changes upon measurement. It specifies the correlations between

===== PAGE 5 =====
5 
 
the outcomes of measurements performed on different parts of a composite system. the mathematical 
objects through which quantum mechanics represents physical systems are state vectors, operators 
representing observables, and probability amplitudes [4]. Its laws are equations governing the evolution 
of these objects. The question it answers, at the deepest level, is: given the preparation of a physical 
system, what outcomes will measurement produce, and with what probabilities? 
These are not the same question. General relativity asks about the structure of spacetime and the 
motion of matter through it. Quantum mechanics asks about the outcomes of measurements on physical 
systems and the probabilities associated with those outcomes. The two questions are related—both are 
questions about the physical world —but they are not identical. They concern different aspects of 
physical reality, described through different conceptual frameworks, answering different kinds of 
inquiry. 
This observation does not, by itself, resolve the problem of quantum gravity. It does not explain how 
to describe a quantum superposition of spacetime geometries, or how to make general relativity 
compatible with the uncertainty principle. But it does suggest that the incompatibility between the two 
theories may not be as fundamental as it is often taken to be. If the two theories are not attempting to 
answer the same question, then the fact that they give different kinds of answers is not, in itself, evidence 
of contradiction. A contradiction arises when two statements assert incompatible things about the same 
object. If the two theories are describing different aspects of reality, their apparent incompatibility may 
reflect a category error in the way the conflict has been framed, rather than a genuine inconsistency in 
the structure of nature. 
An analogy may be helpful here, though it should not be pressed too far. Consider the relationship 
between thermodynamics and statistical mechanics. Thermodynamics describes the macroscopic 
behaviour of physical systems in terms of temperature, pressure, entropy, and the laws governing their 
relationships. Statistical mechanics describes the microscopic behaviour of the same systems in terms 
of the positions, momenta, and interactions of their constituent particles, and derives the macroscopic 
properties of  thermodynamics from the statistical behaviour of large numbers of microscopic 
constituents. The two theories describe the same physical systems, but at different levels of resolution. 
They are not in conflict; they are complementary descriptions of differ ent aspects of the same reality. 
The entropy that appears in thermodynamics as a macroscopic state function is related to, but not 
identical with, the microstate distribution that appears in statistical mechanics. Understanding the 
relationship between the two requires understanding what each theory is describing and at what level 
of description it operates. 
The relationship between quantum mechanics and general relativity is not identical to the relationship 
between thermodynamics and statistical mechanics—the analogy should not be over-extended [10]. But 
the structural point is the same: before concluding that two theories are in fundamental conflict, it is 
necessary to establish that they are attempting to describe the same object at the same level of 
description. The argument of this paper is that, in the case of quantum mechanics and general relativity,

this has not been adequately established, and that examining it more carefully opens the possibility of 
a different understanding of their relationship. 
The distinction between levels of description is not merely a philosophical nicety. It has direct 
implications for how the incompatibility between the two theories should be characterised and for what 
kind of resolution should be sought. If the two theories are not describing the same aspect of physical 
reality at the same descriptive level, then the task is not necessarily to find a single theory that replaces 
both, but to understand how their descriptions articulate with each other —what the interface between 
their respective domains of description looks like, and what constraints consistency places on that 
interface. This is a different problem from finding a quantum theory of gravity in the sense of quantising 
the gravitational field, and it may be a more tractable one [11] . 
 
1.3 A Different Question 
The dominant framing of the relationship between quantum mechanics and general relativity treats 
their incompatibility as evidence that one or both theories are incomplete, and that a more fundamental 
theory is required to supersede them. This is a natural  response to the situation, and it has motivated 
some of the most ambitious theoretical projects in modern physics. But it is not the only possible 
response, and it is worth pausing to ask whether the incompatibility, properly understood, requires this 
response, or whether it admits of a different kind of resolution [12]. 
The conventional framing assumes that the conflict between quantum mechanics and general 
relativity is a conflict between two theories that are both attempting to describe the fundamental 
structure of physical reality, and that the conflict arises because their descriptions are mutually 
inconsistent at that fundamental level. On this assumption, the resolution must be a new, more 
fundamental theory that describes reality correctly where the existing theories fail. The project of 
quantum gravity is the attempt to construct such a theory. 
But there is another possibility. The conflict between quantum mechanics and general relativity may 
arise not from an inconsistency in the structure of nature, but from an inconsistency in the conceptual 
framework through which the two theories have been interpreted and brought into contact. That is: the 
two theories may each be giving a correct and complete description of the aspect of physical reality 
they are designed to describe, and the appearance of conflict may arise from a failure to recognise that 
they are describing different aspects, or from imposing on one theory demands that properly belong to 
the other. 
This is not a deflationary claim. It does not assert that quantum gravity is a pseudo-problem, or that 
the difficulties at the Planck scale are illusory. Gravitational singularities are real. The breakdown of 
classical general relativity at extreme curvatures is real [13]. The difficulties of defining quantum field 
theory in curved spacetime are real  [14]. These are genuine physical problems that require genuine

solutions. What the present paper questions is not the existence of these problems but the diagnosis of 
their source—specifically, the assumption that they arise from a fundamental incompatibility between 
two correct theories, rather than from a conceptual confusion about what those theories are describing 
and how their descriptions relate. 
The different question that this paper proposes to ask is this: rather than asking how to construct a 
new theory that supersedes both quantum mechanics and general relativity, ask whether the apparent 
incompatibility between them is an artefact of the way their relationship has been conceptualised. Is the 
conflict intrinsic to the theories themselves, or is it introduced by the way those theories have been 
interpreted, combined, and brought into contact? If the latter, then resolving the conflict may not require 
a new theory at all—it may require a clearer understanding of what the existing theories are saying. 
This reframing has a specific target. The sharpest version of the incompatibility between quantum 
mechanics and general relativity is the problem of the quantum state of the gravitational field when 
matter is in superposition. If a particle is in a superposition of two different position states—say, located 
at point A with amplitude α and at point B with amplitude β —then its energy-momentum distribution 
is, correspondingly, in a superposition of two different configurations. The Einstein field equations 
require that the spacetime geometry be determined by the energy -momentum distribution. But a 
superposed energy-momentum distribution cannot, within the classical framework of general relativity, 
determine a unique spacetime geometry. The two descriptions —quantum state of matter, classical 
geometry of spacetime—are mutually inconsistent. 
The standard response is to conclude that spacetime geometry must itself be quantised. The present 
paper proposes a different response: to examine whether the inconsistency arises from the physics of 
the situation or from a conceptual error in the way the two descriptions have been juxtaposed. 
Specifically, it proposes to examine whether the demand that a superposed quantum state of matter 
immediately and directly determine a classical spacetime geometry is a demand that follows from the 
physics, or a demand that follows from an unexamined assumption about how the two frameworks are 
supposed to relate. If the latter, then the apparent inconsistency may be resolved not by quantising 
gravity but by clarifying the relationship between quantum descriptions of ma tter and classical 
descriptions of spacetime—understanding them as descriptions that apply at different stages or levels 
of the physical situation, rather than as simultaneous and competing descriptions of the same thing. 
The chapters that follow develop this argument in detail. Chapter 2 examines the nature of quantum 
states, probability, and physical reality, arguing that the quantum description of matter does not have 
the ontological implications that are standardly assu med—in particular, that quantum superposition 
does not entail the simultaneous existence of multiple classical realities. Chapter 3 examines the specific 
point of contact between the quantum description of matter and the general relativistic description of  
spacetime, and proposes a way of understanding that contact that dissolves the apparent inconsistency 
without requiring the quantisation of the gravitational field. Chapter 4 draws out the implications of this

reconceptualisation, compares it with existing approaches to quantum gravity, and identifies the 
questions it leaves open. 
The aim throughout is not to dismiss the problem of quantum gravity but to ask whether the right 
conceptual tools are being brought to bear on it. A problem correctly formulated is already half solved. 
If the apparent incompatibility between quantum mechanics and general relativity is partly a product of 
how the problem has been framed, then reformulating the problem—asking a different question—may 
open paths that the standard framing forecloses. 
 
 
Chapter 2: Quantum States, Probability, and Physical Reality 
 
 
2.1 What the Wave Function Describes 
The wave function is the central mathematical object of quantum mechanics. It encodes everything 
that quantum mechanics permits us to say about the state of a physical system, and its evolution 
according to the Schrödinger equation constitutes the complete dynamical law of the theory. Yet what 
the wave function describes—what it refers to in the physical world—remains one of the most contested 
questions in the foundations of physics  [15,16]. The answer given to this question has direct 
consequences for how the relationship between quantum mechanics and general relativity is understood, 
and for whether the apparent incompatibility between them is as deep as it is standardly taken to be. 
The most common popular account of the wave function presents it as describing a physical system 
that simultaneously exists in multiple states at once. On this account, a particle in a superposition of 
two position states is literally in both positions at the same time; the wave function is a catalogue of all 
the realities that the particle simultaneously inhabits. This account has the advantage of being vivid and 
easily communicated, and it underlies the intuition that quantum mechanics is deeply paradoxical—that 
it describes a world radically unlike the one we perceive. But it is not the only account available, and 
there are strong reasons to question whether it is the most accurate one. 
The alternative account, which this paper adopts and defends, is that the wave function describes not 
the simultaneous existence of multiple classical realities but the probability structure of a single physical 
system—the full set of possible outcomes that could result from measuring the system under specified 
conditions, together with the probabilities associated with each outcome. On this account, a particle in 
a superposition of two position states does not exist simultaneously at two locations; rather, the system 
has a definite physical character—a quantum state—that is such that, were its position to be measured, 
either location could be the result, with probabilities determined by the amplitudes of the respective

===== PAGE 9 =====
9 
 
components of the superposition. The superposition is not a description of multiple coexisting realities; 
it is a description of the probability structure of a single reality prior to the determination of a specific 
measurement outcome. 
This distinction is not merely semantic. It has substantive implications for what quantum mechanics 
is claiming about the physical world, and for how its claims relate to those of general relativity. If 
superposition means simultaneous existence in multipl e classical states, then a superposed quantum 
system is, in a well -defined sense, in multiple places at once, with multiple momenta, multiple 
energies—and, by implication, with multiple energy -momentum distributions, each of which would, 
through the Einste in field equations, determine a different spacetime geometry. The problem of 
quantum gravity then appears as the problem of what to do with these multiple, simultaneously existing, 
mutually inconsistent spacetime geometries. This is a genuine and very hard problem. 
But if superposition means not multiple simultaneous realities but a probability distribution over 
possible outcomes—if the quantum state describes what could result from measurement rather than 
what simultaneously exists—then the situation is different. A superposed matter system does not, on 
this account, have multiple simultaneously existing energy-momentum distributions. It has a quantum 
state that encodes the probabilities of various energy -momentum outcomes being real ised under 
measurement. The question of what spacetime geometry corresponds to this quantum state is then not 
the question of how to superpose multiple geometries, but the question of how the probabilistic 
description of matter at the quantum level relates to the geometric description of s pacetime at the 
classical level. This is still a hard question, but it is a different question—and, as Chapter 3 will argue, 
a more tractable one. 
To make this account precise, it is necessary to be clear about what probability means in quantum 
mechanics. Quantum probabilities are not expressions of ignorance about an underlying classical reality. 
It is not that the particle really is at a definite location, and we simply do not know which one; quantum 
mechanics, as understood through Bell's theorem and its experimental confirmations, rules out this 
interpretation for most observables  [17]. Bell-type experiments rule out local hidden -variable 
explanations for quantum correlations [18], establishing that quantum probabilities are not reducible to 
classical probabilities over pre-existing definite values [19]. But irreducible probability is not the same 
as simultaneous multiple existence.  A die, before it is thrown, does not simultaneously occupy six 
different faces; it has a probability distribution over possible outcomes. The analogy is imperfect —
quantum probabilities are not classical probabilities, and the physical situation is not classical—but the 
logical point holds: probability, even irreducible probability, describes the structure of possible 
outcomes, not the simultaneous realisation of all of them. 
A conceptual model may be useful here. Consider a leaf falling from a tree. Before it lands, its final 
resting position is undetermined —not merely unknown, but, in the relevant sense, not yet fixed. The 
leaf is a single object with a single physical trajectory, subject to forces —gravity, air resistance, 
turbulence—that are in principle deterministic but in practice sensitive to initial conditions in ways that

===== PAGE 10 =====
10 
 
make the outcome unpredictable. We can, however, assign a probability distribution over possible 
landing positions, based on the known conditions of the fall. This probability distribution is not a 
description of many leaves falling simultaneously to many different positions; it is a description of the 
range of possible outcomes for a single leaf. When the leaf lands, one outcome is realised, and the 
probability distribution is replaced by a definite fact. 
This model is not a physical model of quantum systems. Quantum mechanics is not classical 
mechanics with hidden variables, and a leaf falling in air is a classical system subject to deterministic 
laws, not a quantum system subject to irreducible indetermin acy. The leaf model is a conceptual 
model—a device for distinguishing between two ways of interpreting a probability distribution. The 
first interpretation takes the probability distribution as a description of multiple simultaneous realities: 
many leaves,  many positions, all equally real. The second interpretation takes the probability 
distribution as a description of the range of possible outcomes for a single system: one leaf, one future 
position, not yet determined. The leaf model illustrates the second interpretation, not because quantum 
systems are like falling leaves, but because the logical structure of the second interpretation —
probability as the description of possible outcomes for a single system, not as a catalogue of 
simultaneously existing realities—is the same in both cases. 
The argument of this paper is that the second interpretation is the correct one for quantum mechanics 
as well. The wave function describes the probability structure of a single physical system —the range 
of possible outcomes for that system under specified measurement conditions —not the simultaneous 
existence of multiple classical realities. This interpretation is consistent with the mathematical 
formalism of quantum mechanics, consistent with the experimental record, and, as the following 
sections argue, co nsistent with the most defensible accounts of quantum measurement and physical 
reality. 
 
2.2 The Observer as Physical Interaction 
The role of the observer in quantum mechanics has been the source of more philosophical confusion 
than almost any other aspect of the theory. The standard formulation of quantum mechanics assigns a 
special role to measurement: the wave function of a system evolves continuously and deterministically 
according to the Schrödinger equation until a measurement is performed, at which point it undergoes a 
discontinuous, probabilistic change —the collapse of the wave function —to an eigenstate of the 
measured observable. This measurement postulate is essential to the predictive success of quantum 
mechanics, but it raises an immediate question: what counts as a measurement, and what is special about 
it? 
The history of quantum mechanics includes a range of answers to this question, some of which have 
introduced unnecessary complexity and confusion by treating the observer —the entity that performs

===== PAGE 11 =====
11 
 
the measurement—as something distinct from the physical systems that quantum mechanics describes. 
On the most extreme versions of this view, the observer is identified with a conscious mind, and the 
collapse of the wave function is taken to require the intervention of con sciousness. This view is not 
widely held among physicists today, but traces of the associated confusion persist in the way quantum 
measurement is often discussed, and in the intuition that quantum mechanics is somehow about the role 
of the observer as a perceiving subject. 
The position defended here is different and more straightforward: the observer, in quantum 
mechanics, is not a conscious mind, a subject of experience, or anything that stands outside the physical 
description. The observer is a physical system—any physical system—that interacts with the quantum 
system under study [20]. A measurement is a physical interaction between the quantum system and the 
measuring apparatus; the measuring apparatus is itself a physical system, subject to the same physical 
laws as the system being measured. The outcome of the measurement —the result registered by the 
apparatus—is the result of this physical interaction, and it is determined by the physical conditions of 
the interaction, not by the presence of a conscious observer. 
This account of the observer as physical interaction has several important consequences. First, it 
removes the appearance of subjectivity from quantum mechanics. The outcomes of measurements are 
not created by consciousness; they are produced by physical processes. The role of the observer is not 
to collapse the wave function by looking at it but to interact with the quantum system in a way that 
produces a definite outcome. The definiteness of the outcome is a consequence of the physical 
interaction, not of the observer's awareness of it. 
Second, it connects the account of measurement to the theory of decoherence. Decoherence is the 
process by which a quantum system loses its quantum coherence —the ability to exhibit interference 
effects—through interaction with its environment. When a quantum system interacts with a large 
number of environmental degrees of freedom, the different components of its superposition become 
entangled with different states of the environment, and the interference  terms between them become 
unmeasurable in practice, e ven if they remain present in the full quantum state of the system -plus-
environment. The result is that the quantum system behaves, for all practical purposes, as if it were in 
a definite classical state, even though the full quantum state of the combined system remains a 
superposition. Decoherence does not solve the measurement problem —it does not explain why a 
particular outcome occurs rather than another—but it does explain why quantum superpositions are not 
directly observable at macroscopic scales, and why the classical world of definite objects and definite 
properties emerges from the quantum world of superpositions and probabilities [21]. 
The account of the observer as physical interaction fits naturally with the decoherence picture. The 
measuring apparatus is a macroscopic physical system with enormously many degrees of freedom. 
When it interacts with the quantum system being measured, it decoheres the quantum system very 
rapidly—on timescales many orders of magnitude shorter than any that are experimentally accessible. 
The result is that the quantum system, from the perspective of any subsequent interaction with the

===== PAGE 12 =====
12 
 
environment or with a human observer, behaves as if it were in a definite eigenstate of the measured 
observable [22]. The apparent collapse of the wave function is, on this account, not a fundamental 
physical process distinct from the Schrödinger evolution; it is the practical consequence of rapid 
decoherence caused by the physical interaction between the quantum system and the measuring 
apparatus [23]. 
Third, and most importantly for the argument of this paper, the account of the observer as physical 
interaction clarifies the relationship between quantum descriptions and classical descriptions. The 
quantum state of a system is a description of its probability structure—the range of possible outcomes 
for interactions with other physical systems. When a physical interaction occurs, one outcome from that 
range is realised. The interaction is itself a physical process, described by physical laws. The outcome 
of the interaction—the definite result—is then the object that enters the classical description: the definite 
position, the definite momentum, the definite energy. The transition from quantum description to 
classical description is not a transition from one world to another; it is a transition from a description of 
possible outcomes to a description of an actual outcome, mediated by a physical interaction. 
This account of measurement and observation has direct relevance to the problem of quantum gravity. 
If measurement is a physical interaction, and if the classical properties of matter—its definite position, 
momentum, and energy -momentum distribution —are the outcomes of such interactions, then the 
relationship between the quantum description of matter and the classical description of spacetime can 
be understood in terms of the relationship between possible outcomes and actual outcomes. The 
quantum stat e of m atter describes the probability structure of possible energy -momentum 
configurations. The relationship between quantum descriptions of matter and classical descriptions of 
spacetime may therefore be understood through the relationship between possible outcomes and 
physically realised configurations —a point developed in Chapter 3 . The two descriptions do not 
compete; they apply at different stages of the physical situation.  
 
2.3 Quantum Reality Beyond Ontological Assumptions 
The question of what quantum mechanics tells us about physical reality—about what really exists, 
and in what form—is one that the theory does not answer unambiguously. The mathematical formalism 
of quantum mechanics is well -defined and predictively successful; the interpretation of that 
formalism—what it says about the nature of physical reality —is not. This interpretational 
underdetermination has generated a large philosophical literature and several competing interpretations 
of quantum mechanics, each of which takes a different position on the ontological status of the wave 
function, the nature of quantum probability, and the relationship between the quantum description of 
the world and the classical world of experience.

===== PAGE 13 =====
13 
 
The present paper does not adjudica te between these interpretations  [24]. Its argument does not 
depend on any particular interpretation being correct  [15], and the conceptual bridge it proposes 
between quantum mechanics and general relativity is intended to be compatible with the range of 
interpretations that take quantum probability seriously as a feature of physical reality. What this section 
does is examine the ontological assumptions that are most commonly brought to the interpretation of 
quantum mechanics —specifically, the assumption that quantum superposition requires the 
simultaneous existence of multiple classical realities—and argue that this assumption is not forced by 
the formalism of quantum mechanics, and that rejecting it opens a more coherent account of the 
relationship between quantum and classical descriptions. 
The most prominent interpretation that does embrace the ontology of multiple simultaneous realities 
is the Everett, or many-worlds, interpretation. Everett proposed that the wave function is the complete 
description of physical reality, that it never colla pses, and that all components of a superposition are 
equally real [25]. On this account, when a quantum measurement is performed, the universe branches: 
every possible outcome of the measurement is realised, in a separate branch of the universal wave 
function. There is no collapse, no selection of a single outcome; all outcome s occur, in different 
branches, and the branches evolve independently thereafter. The apparent definiteness of measurement 
outcomes in our experience is explained by the fact that observers are themselves part of the wave 
function and therefore branch alon g with the system they are measuring; each branch -observer 
experiences a definite outcome, because within that branch, only one outcome has occurred. 
The many-worlds interpretation is internally consistent, and it has the significant virtue of taking the 
Schrödinger equation seriously as a complete and universal law, without introducing any additional 
collapse postulate. But it comes at a significant on tological cost: the postulation of an enormously 
large—indeed, continuously proliferating—number of equally real but mutually inaccessible branches 
of the universe. This ontological extravagance is not forced by the mathematics; it is a choice made in 
the interpretation of the mathematics. The wave function formalism, in itself, does not require that all 
components of a superposition correspond to simultaneously existing classical realities [26]. It requires 
only that the probabilities of measurement outcomes be given by the squared amplitudes of the wave 
function [27]. The many-worlds interpretation is one way of accounting for those probabilities; it is not 
the only way. 
Niels Bohr's Copenhagen interpretation, by contrast, declines to assign any ontological status to the 
wave function beyond its role as a predictive tool [28]. On the Copenhagen view, the wave function is 
not a description of physical reality; it is a representation of the knowledge available to an observer, 
and its collapse upon measurement is not a physical process but an update of that knowledge in response 
to new information. The quantum world, on this view, does not have definite properties independently 
of measurement; physical reality, as quantum mechanics describes it, is defined only relative to a 
measurement context [29]. What exists between measurements is not a question that quantum 
mechanics, or indeed physics, can or should answer.

===== PAGE 14 =====
14 
 
Bohr's position has been criticised for introducing a problematic distinction between the quantum 
system and the classical measuring apparatus, and for its apparent dependence on a classical description 
of the measurement context that is not itself grounde d in quantum mechanics. Werner  Heisenberg 
offered a related but somewhat different account [30]: the wave function describes objective tendencies 
or potentialities—what Heisenberg called the potentia of Aristotelian philosophy [30]—that are real 
features of the physical world, not merely of our knowledge, but that are not actualities until they are 
realised through interaction. On Heisenberg's account, quantum reality is a realm of potentiality, distinct 
from the classical realm of actuality, and measurement is the process through which poten tiality is 
converted into actuality. 
The account developed in this paper is closest to Heisenberg's, though it does not endorse any specific 
metaphysical framework. The wave function describes the probability structure of a physical system—
the range of possible outcomes for interactions with other systems, and the probabilities associated with 
each outcome. This probability structure is a real feature of the physical world: it is not merely a 
representation of our ignorance, as a classical h idden-variable account would have it, but a genuine 
property of the system, one that determines the statistical pattern of outcomes over many measurements. 
At the same time, it is not a description of multiple simultaneously existing classical realities: the 
probabilities describe possible outcomes, not simultaneous actualities. 
Wojciech Zurek's programme of quantum Darwinism offers a further perspective on how classical 
reality emerges from quantum reality [31]. Zurek argues that the classical properties of objects —the 
properties that are robust, reproducible, and accessible to many observers —are those that are 
preferentially recorded in the environment through decoherence. The environment acts as a witness to 
the quantum system, copying information about certain of its properties into many environmental 
degrees of freedom. The properties that are thus multiply recorded are the ones that acquire an objective, 
classical character; they are the ones that multiple observers can agree on, and that therefore constitute 
the shared classical world. Quantum Darwinism does not resolve the measurement problem in the sense 
of explaining why a particular outcome occurs, but it does explain how certain quantum properties 
acquire the robustness and objectivity that characterise classical reality. 
The common thread in these accounts—Heisenberg's potentia, decoherence, quantum Darwinism—
is that the transition from quantum description to classical description is not a transition between two 
ontologically separate realms, but a transition between two levels of description of the same physical 
reality: the level of possible outcomes and the level of actual, classically robust outcomes. The quantum 
state describes the former; the classical state describes the latter. Neither description is more 
fundamental in an absolute sense; each is appropriate to its domain of application [32, 33]. 
A principle that cuts across these interpretational differences can be stated as follows: the multiplicity 
of possible outcomes encoded in a quantum state does not imply a corresponding multiplicity of 
physical sources. A quantum state may assign non-zero probability amplitude to two different energy-
momentum configurations, but the physical system it describes remains one system—not two systems,

===== PAGE 15 =====
15 
 
not a superposition of independently existing matter distributions. The probability space expands; the 
number of physical entities does not. This principle —that quantum state multiplicity does not entail 
physical source multiplicity—provides the conceptual basis for the argument developed in Chapter 3, 
where the relationship between quantum descriptions of matter and gravitational descriptions of 
spacetime is examined directly. 
For the purposes of this paper, the critical point is that this understanding of the relationship between 
quantum and classical descriptions does not require the many -worlds ontology of simultaneously 
existing branches. The probability structure described by the wave function is real; the multiple 
simultaneous classical realities are not required. A physical system in a superposition of two energy -
momentum states has a real quantum state —a real probability structure over possible energy -
momentum outcomes—but it does not necessarily require two simultaneously existing classical energy-
momentum distributions as its physical correlate.  It has one quantum state, from which one classical 
energy-momentum distribution will emerge through physical interaction. The relationship between the 
quantum description of matter and the classical description of spacetime is therefore not, on this account, 
a relationship between multiple simultaneous matter configurations and multiple simultaneous 
spacetime geometries. It is a r elationship between a quantum probability structure and the classical 
geometry that corresponds to the actual matter configuration that is realised through physical interaction. 
This is the conceptual basis for the argument developed in Chapter 3. 
 
 
Chapter 3: Gravity and the Quantum State 
 
 
3.1 Where the Descriptions Diverge 
The precise point at which quantum mechanics and general relativity come into conceptual conflict 
can be identified with some care. It is not, as is sometimes suggested, simply the fact that one theory is 
probabilistic and the other is deterministic. Deter ministic theories can be approximations to 
probabilistic ones, or probabilistic theories can emerge from deterministic ones, without either being in 
fundamental conflict with the other. The conflict is more specific, and it concerns the relationship 
between the quantum state of matter and the spacetime geometry that general relativity associates with 
that matter. 
In general relativity, the gravitational field —the curvature of spacetime —is determined by the 
energy-momentum tensor of matter. This relationship is encoded in the Einstein field equations, which 
equate the Einstein tensor, a measure of spacetime curvature, to the energy-momentum tensor of matter,

===== PAGE 16 =====
16 
 
scaled by appropriate constants. The energy -momentum tensor is a classical object: it assigns to each 
point in spacetime a definite value, encoding the density and flux of energy and momentum at that point. 
Given a definite matter distribution—a definite energy-momentum tensor—the Einstein field equations 
determine a definite spacetime geometry. The relationship is local, deterministic, and unique: one matter 
distribution, one geometry. 
Quantum mechanics, as argued in the preceding chapter, describes matter through a wave function 
that encodes not a definite energy -momentum distribution but a probability structure over possible 
energy-momentum outcomes. A quantum particle does not, in gen eral, have a definite position or 
momentum prior to measurement; correspondingly, it does not have a definite contribution to the 
energy-momentum tensor. If the particle is in a superposition of two different position states, its 
contribution to the energy -momentum tensor is, in the quantum description, correspondingly 
indeterminate—not a definite value but a probability distribution over possible values. 
The conflict, stated precisely, is this: quantum mechanics assigns to matter a state from which no 
definite energy-momentum tensor can be extracted, while general relativity requires a definite energy-
momentum tensor in order to determine the spacetime geo metry. The two frameworks make 
inconsistent demands on the same physical quantity. This inconsistency is what drives the project of 
quantum gravity: the attempt to construct a theory in which the spacetime geometry itself acquires 
quantum properties, so th at a superposed matter state corresponds not to a definite geometry but to a 
superposition of geometries. 
But the standard framing of this inconsistency contains an assumption that is worth examining. It 
assumes that the quantum state of matter —the superposition—must be directly and simultaneously 
translated into a gravitational description. It treats the quantum description and the gravitational 
description as if they must be applied at the same moment, to the same physical situation, and reconciled 
with each oth er in that moment. This assumption is what generates the appearance of a hard 
contradiction: if you  demand that a superposed quantum state immediately determine a classical 
spacetime geometry, you will find that it cannot, because the quantum state does not provide the definite 
energy-momentum tensor that the Einstein field equations require. 
Consider the following example, which makes the logical structure of the difficulty vivid. 
Schrödinger's cat is a physical system —a cat in a sealed box —that is placed, by the setup of the 
experiment, into a quantum superposition of two states: alive and dead [34]. Before the box is opened 
and the cat's state is observed, quantum mechanics assigns to the cat a superposition of these two 
outcomes, each with some probability amplitude. The question that the standard framing of the quantum 
gravity problem would then as k is: what is the gravitational field of this cat? If the cat is in a 
superposition of alive and dead, and if these two states correspond to different energy -momentum 
configurations—a living cat has a different metabolic activity, different internal energ y distribution, 
than a dead one—then what spacetime geometry does the Einstein field equations assign to the cat?

===== PAGE 17 =====
17 
 
The standard framing treats this as a genuine and hard problem: the cat is in a superposition, its 
energy-momentum tensor is therefore indeterminate, and the spacetime geometry is therefore undefined. 
But notice what this framing obscures. Whatever the quantum state of the cat —alive, dead, or in 
superposition—there is exactly one cat in the box. Not two cats, not a probability-weighted average of 
cats, not an infinite ensemble of cats corresponding to every possible quantum outcome simultaneously: 
one cat. The gravitational field outside the box is the gravitat ional field of one cat, of a specific mass, 
occupying a specific volume. The quantum superposition does not double the cat, or split it into multiple 
simultaneously existing versions of itself. It describes the probability structure of the cat's internal 
state—whether, upon observation, it will be found alive or dead —without in any way multiplying the 
number of cats or the total mass-energy that generates the gravitational field. 
This observation cuts directly against the standard framing of the quantum gravity problem. The 
problem, as standardly posed, implicitly assumes that the quantum superposition of the cat's internal 
state requires a corresponding superposition of spacetime geometries—that because the cat's energy -
momentum tensor is, in the quantum description, indeterminate, the spacetime geometry must be 
correspondingly indeterminate or superposed. But this assumption conflates two distinct things: the 
quantum indeterminacy of the cat's internal state, and the gravitational field generated by the cat as a 
whole. At the coarse-grained level relevant to the gravitational field, not all quantum degrees of freedom 
necessarily correspond to independent degrees of freedom in the spacetime geometry. The relevant 
gravitational source may depend only on conserved or coarse -grained properties of the system —
properties that are not necessarily placed in superposition by the quantum state under discussion. There 
is one cat, with one mass, generating one gravitational field, regardless of whether its internal quantum 
state is alive, dead, or in superposition. 
This is not to say that there is no quantum gravitational effect at all, or that the quantum state of 
matter never has implications for the gravitational field. At sufficiently fine -grained scales —at the 
Planck scale, where quantum effects and gravitational effects are both relevant —the quantum state of 
matter does have implications for the structure of spacetime, and a complete theory must account for 
these implications [35]. But the standard framing of the quantum gravity problem at the conceptual 
level—the claim that quantum superposition immediately and necessarily generates an inconsistency 
with the classical spacetime geometry —rests on an assumption that the quantum description and the 
gravitational description must be applied simultaneously and at the same level of resolution. The 
Schrödinger's cat example illustrates why this assumption is questionable: the quantum indeterminacy 
of the cat's internal state does not render  the gravitational field of the cat indeterminate, because the 
gravitational field is determined by the cat's total mass-energy, which is not in superposition. 
The more general point is this. Quantum superposition is a feature of the quantum description of a 
physical system—a description of the probability structure of possible measurement outcomes. It is not 
a feature of the system's total mass -energy, or of any other conserved quantity that is the same across 
all branches of the superposition. When a physical system is in a superposition of two states, quantum

===== PAGE 18 =====
18 
 
superposition does not necessarily imply a corresponding multiplicity of gravitational sources [36], 
because the quantum state describes possible configurations of a single physical system rather than 
multiple simultaneously realised classical energy -momentum distributions . The apparent conflict 
between quantum mechanics and general relativity is sharpest when it is assumed that every quantum 
degree of freedom directly and independently determines a corresponding gravitational degree of 
freedom—that the quantum state must be translated, point by point and moment by moment, into a 
classical gravitational field. But this assumption is not forced by either theory. It is a choice about how 
to relate the two descriptions, and it is a choice that, as the cat example illustrates, is not obviously 
correct. 
 
3.2 A Conceptual Realignment 
The argument of the preceding section establishes that the standard framing of the quantum gravity 
problem rests on a contestable assumption: that the quantum description of matter and the gravitational 
description of spacetime must be applied simultaneous ly, at the same level of description, and 
reconciled with each other in that moment. This section proposes a different way of understanding the 
relationship between the two descriptions—one that does not require this assumption, and that dissolves 
the apparent inconsistency without either quantising gravity or abandoning the quantum description of 
matter. 
The key conceptual move is to recognise that the quantum description of matter and the classical 
description of spacetime are descriptions that apply at different stages of a physical situation, rather 
than simultaneously competing descriptions of the same  situation. The quantum state of a physical 
system is a description of its probability structure—the range of possible outcomes for interactions with 
other systems, and the probabilities associated with each outcome. This description is appropriate prior 
to, and independently of, any specific physical interaction that realises one of those possible outcomes. 
The classical description of spacetime, by contrast, is a description of the geometric structure associated 
with a definite matter distribution—an actual configuration of matter, not a probability distribution over 
possible configurations. This description is appropriate to the situation after a specific matter 
configuration has been physically realised. 
To make this concrete: consider a quantum particle that is in a superposition of two position states—
located near point A with probability amplitude α, and near point B with probability amplitude β. The 
quantum description of this particle is the wave function encoding this superposition. What is the 
gravitational field of this particle? The question, as standardly posed, appears to require an answer in 
terms of the quantum state: since the particle is in superposition, its gravitational field must also be, in 
some sense, in superposition —it must be both the gravitational field of a particle at A and the 
gravitational field of a particle at B simultaneously.

===== PAGE 19 =====
19 
 
But this framing assumes that the gravitational description must be applied to the quantum state of 
the particle—to the probability distribution over possible positions —rather than to the actual position 
that the particle will be found to have when it interacts with another system. The gravitational 
description applies to physically realised configurations rather than to the abstract probability 
amplitudes of possible configurations. What spacetime geometry corresponds to is not the probability 
distribution over possible positions, but the configuration that the system presents at the level of 
description where definite spacetime structure is defined. And where the particle actually is—its actual 
position—is determined through physical interaction. Prior to such interaction, the quantum description 
is the appropriate one: the particle's position is a probability distribution, not a definite value. But the 
gravitational field that the particle generates in s pacetime is a property of the particle's actual 
configuration, not of its probability distribution over possible configurations. 
The conceptual realignment proposed here is therefore the following: the gravitational description of 
a physical system—its contribution to the spacetime geometry through the energy-momentum tensor—
should be understood as applying to the actual configuration of that system, as realised through physical 
interaction, rather than to its quantum state prior to interaction. This is not a claim about the physical 
process through which the actual configurat ion is determined; it is neutral between different 
interpretations of quantum mechanics, including those that reject the notion of wave function collapse. 
It does not assert that the gravitational field is produced only after a measurement is performed, or that 
there is a moment of collapse at which the gravitati onal field switches from superposed to definite. It 
asserts only that the gravitational description and the quantum description apply at different levels —
the quantum description to the probability structure of the system, the gravitational description to t he 
actual configuration that the system's probability structure is a description of. 
This distinction can be expressed without reference to collapse or to any specific interpretation of 
quantum mechanics. The gravitational field description requires, as its input, a definite energy -
momentum tensor. A definite energy-momentum tensor corresponds to a definite classical configuration 
of matter. The quantum state of matter is not a definite classical configuration; it is a probability 
structure over possible classical configurations. These two descriptions —quantum state and classical 
configuration—are not simultaneous and competing descriptions of the same physical situation; they 
are descriptions that apply to different aspects of the same physical situation: the quantum state to the 
probability structure of possible outcomes, the classical configu ration to the actual outcome that is 
realised. The gravitational description requires the latter, not the former. 
A further way of expressing this point draws on the account of decoherence developed in the 
preceding chapter. Decoherence is the process by which a quantum system's superposition becomes 
effectively classical through interaction with its environment. As a  quantum system interacts with the 
many degrees of freedom of its environment, the different components of its superposition become 
entangled with different environmental states, and the interference terms between the components 
become unmeasurable. The sy stem then behaves, for all practical purposes, as if it were in a definite

===== PAGE 20 =====
20 
 
classical state. The relevant point here is that the timescale of decoherence for macroscopic objects —
objects large enough to generate a gravitational field that would be, even in principle, measurable —is 
extraordinarily short, many orders of magnitude shorter than any experimentally accessible timescale 
[37]. Macroscopic objects are, in practice, always already decohered; their quantum superpositions, if 
any exist, are instantly suppressed by environmental interaction [38]. 
This means that the regime in which the quantum gravity problem appears most acute —the regime 
in which a macroscopic object generates a measurable gravitational field while simultaneously being in 
a quantum superposition—is precisely the regime in which decoherence ensures that the superposition 
is, for all practical purposes, eliminated. The quantum state of a macroscopic object, in practice, is 
always effectively classical by the time it generates a gravitational field t hat can be measured. The 
apparent conflict between the quantum description of matter and the classical description of spacetime 
therefore does not arise, in practice, at macroscopic scales; it arises only at the Planck scale, where both 
quantum effects and gravitational effects are simultan eously relevant, and where the decoherence 
argument cannot be straightforwardly applied. 
At the Planck scale, the conceptual realignment proposed in this section does not resolve all 
difficulties. A complete theory of quantum gravity at the Planck scale will require mathematical tools 
that go beyond the conceptual argument presented here [39]. But the conceptual argument does establish 
something important: the apparent inconsistency between quantum mechanics and general relativity at 
the conceptual level —the apparent impossibility of relating a quantum description of matter to a 
classical description of spacetime —is not a fundamental logical contradiction. It is a consequence of 
applying the two descriptions simultaneously and at the same level, rather than recognising that they 
apply to different aspects of the physical situation. Recognising this distinction does not solve quantum 
gravity, but it does reformulate the problem in a way that is less paradoxical and more tractable. 
The core principle that this section establishes is the following: a conceptual interface between the 
quantum description of matter and the geometric description of spacetime requires that each description 
be applied within its proper domain —the quantum description to probability structures of possible 
configurations, the geometric description to physically realised configurations at the level where 
definite spacetime structure exists [11]. Neither description need be extended beyond that domain in 
order for the two to be mutually consistent . When the physical description is quantum —when the 
appropriate description of the system is a probability structure over possible configurations —the 
gravitational description should not be required to provide a definite classical geometry corresponding 
to the quantum superposition. The two descriptions are consistent when they are understood as applying 
at different levels; they appear inconsistent only when they are applied simultaneously at the same level. 
This is the conceptual realignment that this paper proposes.

3.3 Comparison with Existing Approaches 
The conceptual argument developed in the preceding two sections is not a quantum gravity theory. 
It does not propose a new set of field equations, a new mathematical formalism, or a new physical 
mechanism. Before drawing out its implications in Chapter 4, it is useful to situate it relative to the 
existing approaches to quantum gravity, in order to clarify what this paper is and is not claiming, and 
to show how the conceptual realignment it proposes differs from, and potentially complements, the 
established research programmes. 
The dominant approaches to quantum gravity fall into several broad categories. Canonical quantum 
gravity, the earliest and in some respects most direct approach, attempts to quantise general relativity 
by applying the methods of quantum field theory directly to the gravitational field. The Wheeler-DeWitt 
equation—the result of applying the canonical quantisation procedure to general relativity —is a wave 
equation for the quantum state of the universe [40], including the geometry of spacetime. Canonical 
quantum gravity treats spacetime geometry as a quantum degree of freedom, subject to the same kind 
of probability-amplitude description that quantum mechanics applies to matter [41]. The approach faces 
deep technical difficulties —in particular, the problem of time, which arises because the Wheeler -
DeWitt equation has no explicit time variable, making it unclear how to recover the time -dependent 
evolution that characterises ordinary quantum mechanics [42]—and has not produced a complete, 
empirically confirmed theory [43]. 
Loop quantum gravity is a more recent approach that builds on the canonical programme. It 
reformulates general relativity in terms of connection variables and their conjugate momenta —loop 
variables—and applies quantisation to these [44]. The result is a theory in which the geometry of 
spacetime is quantised: area, volume, and other geometric quantities acquire discrete spectra, and the 
smooth continuum of classical spacetime is replaced, at the Planck scale [45], by a discrete quantum 
structure. Loop quantum gravity has produced significant results in the context of black hole 
thermodynamics and cosmology, but remains incomplete as a theory of quantum gravity [39], and has 
not yet made contact with the predictions of the Standard Model of particle physics. 
String theory approaches the problem from a different direction. Rather than quantising general 
relativity directly, string theory posits that the fundamental constituents of matter are not point particles 
but one -dimensional extended objects —strings—and that the different vibrational modes of these 
strings correspond to the different elementary particles, including the graviton, the hypothetical 
quantum of the gravitational field. String theory is, in principle, a unified theory of all forces, including 
gravity, and it is mathematically consistent in a way that point-particle quantum field theory with gravity 
is not. But string theory requires extra spatial dimensions beyond the three of ordinary experience, and 
has not yet made contact with experiment in a  way that would confirm or disconfirm its distinctive 
predictions [46].

===== PAGE 22 =====
22 
 
Semiclassical gravity is a more conservative approach that does not attempt to quantise the 
gravitational field at all. In semiclassical gravity, the spacetime geometry remains classical, described 
by the Einstein field equations, but the right -hand side o f those equations —the energy -momentum 
tensor—is replaced by its quantum expectation value: the average value of the energy -momentum 
tensor in the quantum state of matter, rather than a definite classical value. This approach has proven 
fruitful in the context of quantum field theory in curved spacetime, including the derivation of Hawking 
radiation [47], but it is understood to be an approximation that must break down at the Planck scale 
[48], where the quantum fluctuations of the gravitational field cannot be neglected. 
Each of these approaches accepts, at least implicitly, the standard framing of the quantum gravity 
problem: the framing in which quantum mechanics and general relativity are in fundamental conflict 
because the former requires a quantum treatment of matter—including the gravitational source—while 
the latter requires a classical treatment of spacetime. Each approach attempts to resolve this conflict by 
modifying one or both theories: canonical quantum gravity and loop quantum gravity modify general 
relativity by quantising it; string theory replaces both theories with something more fundamental; 
semiclassical gravity modifies the source term of general relativity to accommodate quantum matter 
while keeping spacetime classical. 
The present paper does not propose a fifth approach of this kind. It does not modify either theory, 
quantise the gravitational field, or replace either framework with something more fundamental. What it 
proposes is different in character: a re -examination of the framing within which the conflict has been 
understood, and a reconceptualisation of the relationship between the two theories that does not require 
either to be modified or replaced. The question it asks is not how to build a new theory that supersedes 
both, but whether the apparent conflict between them arises from the theories themselves or from the 
way their relationship has been conceptualised. 
The answer it proposes —that the quantum description of matter and the classical description of 
spacetime are descriptions that apply at different levels of the physical situation, and that the apparent 
conflict arises from applying them simultaneously at the same level —is not a solution to quantum 
gravity in the technical sense. It does not produce the Planck -scale physics that a complete quantum 
gravity theory must provide. But it does establish something that is, in a certain sense, prior to the 
technical problem: a conceptual interface through which the quantum description of matter and the 
geometric description of spacetime can be related without requiring either description to be extended 
beyond its proper domain —and from which the technical problem of quantum gravity can be more 
precisely formulated. 
In this respect, the present argument has a different relationship to the existing approaches than they 
have to each other. The existing approaches are competitors: each offers a different technical solution 
to the same problem. The present argument is not  a competitor in this sense; it is a conceptual 
clarification of the problem itself. If the argument is correct—if the apparent conflict between quantum 
mechanics and general relativity is, at the conceptual level, a consequence of applying the two

===== PAGE 23 =====
23 
 
descriptions simultaneously at the same level rather than a fundamental logical inconsistency—then the 
task of quantum gravity is not to resolve a contradiction but to construct a mathematical framework 
adequate to describe the physical situation at the level where both quantum and gravitational effects are 
simultaneously relevant. This is still a hard problem, but it is a different problem from resolving a 
fundamental contradiction between two correct theories [11]. 
The closest analogy in the history of physics may be the relationship between thermodynamics and 
statistical mechanics before Boltzmann. The apparent conflict between the time -reversible laws of 
classical mechanics and the time-irreversible second law of thermodynamics was, for a period, treated 
as a fundamental contradiction. Boltzmann's resolution —that the second law is statistical rather than 
absolute, and that thermodynamic irreversibility is a consequence of the overwhelming probability of 
certain macroscopic transitions, not a violation of microscopic reversibility [49]—did not modify either 
classical mechanics or thermodynamics. It reframed the relationship between them, showing that the 
apparent contradiction was a consequence of misunderstanding the level at which each description 
applied. The present paper proposes an  analogous reframing for quantum mechanics and general 
relativity: not a new theory, but a clearer understanding of the relationship between two existing theories, 
which dissolves an apparent contradiction by showing it to be a consequence of applying de scriptions 
at the wrong level. 
 
 
Chapter 4: Toward Compatibility 
 
 
4.1 Resolving the Apparent Conflict 
The preceding chapters have developed three interconnected arguments. The first is that quantum 
mechanics and general relativity are not attempting to describe the same aspect of physical reality at the 
same level of description: quantum mechanics describes the probability structure of possible outcomes 
for physical systems, while general relativity describes the geometric structure of spacetime associated 
with definite configurations of matter. The second is that quantum superposition does not entail the 
simultaneous existence of multiple classical realities; it entails a probability distribution over possible 
outcomes for a single physical system, and the transition from quantum description to classical 
description is mediated by physical interaction rather than by any mysterious or subjective process. The 
third is that the apparent inconsistency between the two theories arises from applying their respective 
descriptions simultaneously and at the same level, rather than recognising that they are appropriate  to 
different aspects and different stages of the physical situation.

===== PAGE 24 =====
24 
 
These three arguments, taken together, support a resolution of the apparent conflict between quantum 
mechanics and general relativity that does not require either theory to be modified, quantised, or 
replaced. The resolution is conceptual rather than techn ical: it consists in recognising that the two 
theories are complementary descriptions of different aspects of the same physical reality, and that their 
apparent incompatibility is a consequence of a category error in the way their relationship has been 
understood. 
To make this resolution explicit, it is useful to state the conflict as precisely as possible, and then to 
show how the conceptual framework developed in the preceding chapters dissolves it. The conflict, in 
its sharpest form, is this: quantum mechanics requires that a physical system can be in a superposition 
of states that correspond to different energy-momentum configurations, while general relativity requires 
that the spacetime geometry be uniquely determined by a definite energy-momentum configuration. If 
matter is in a quantum superposition of two energy -momentum configurations, then the Einstein field 
equations cannot determine a unique spacetime geometry, because their source term —the energy -
momentum tensor—is not definite. The two theories therefore make mutually inconsistent demands on 
the same physical quantity. 
The resolution proposed here identifies two misunderstandings embedded in this formulation of the 
conflict. The first misunderstanding is that the quantum superposition of a matter system corresponds 
to the simultaneous existence of multiple energy -momentum configurations. As argued in Chapter 2, 
this is not correct: a quantum superposition is a probability distribution over possible outcomes, not a 
simultaneous realisation of all of them. A matter system in a superposition of two energy -momentum 
states has  one quantum state —one probability structure —not two simultaneously existing energy -
momentum distributions. The energy-momentum tensor that enters the Einstein field equations is not a 
quantum superposition of two classical tensors; it corresponds to the matter system at the level of 
description where a definite classical configuration exists—the level at which the geometric description 
of spacetime is defined. 
The second misunderstanding is that the quantum description and the gravitational description must 
be applied simultaneously to the same physical situation. As argued in Chapter 3, this is also not correct: 
the two descriptions apply at different levels. T he quantum description —the wave function, the 
superposition, the probability amplitudes—applies to the probability structure of the matter system prior 
to, and independently of, any specific physical interaction that realises one of the possible outcomes. 
The gravitational description —the energy -momentum tensor, the Einstein field equations, the 
spacetime geometry—applies to the actual configuration of matter that is realised through physical 
interaction. These are not competing descriptions of the same moment; they are descriptions of different 
aspects of the physical situation. 
When these two misunderstandings are corrected, the apparent conflict dissolves. There is no moment 
at which a quantum superposition of matter configurations must simultaneously determine a classical 
spacetime geometry, because the gravitational description does not apply to the quantum superposition;

===== PAGE 25 =====
25 
 
it applies to the actual matter configuration that the quantum superposition is a probability description 
of. The quantum description and the gravitational description are consistent because they apply at 
different levels: the quantum description to the pr obability structure of possible configurations, the 
gravitational description to the actual configuration that is realised. They are not in conflict, because 
they are not making competing claims about the same thing. 
This resolution can be stated in terms of a simple logical structure. The apparent conflict takes the 
form: quantum mechanics says that the energy -momentum tensor is indefinite; general relativity says 
that the energy -momentum tensor is definite; therefore  the two theories are inconsistent. But this 
argument contains an equivocation on the phrase "the energy -momentum tensor." In quantum 
mechanics, the energy -momentum tensor refers to the energy -momentum observable of a quantum 
system—a quantity that, prior to measurement, has a probability distribution over possible values. In 
general relativity, the energy-momentum tensor refers to the actual energy -momentum distribution of 
matter—the definite configuration that sources the gravitational field. These are not the same object. 
Quantum mechanics says that the energy -momentum observable, prior to measurement, has a 
probability distribution  [17]; general relativity says that the actual energy -momentum distribution 
sources the gravitational field. There is no contradiction, because the two theories are making claims 
about different objects—one about the probability structure of possible configurations, the other about 
the actual configuration that is realised. 
The resolution is not a denial that quantum gravity is a genuine problem. At the Planck scale, where 
quantum fluctuations of the gravitational field itself cannot be neglected, a complete physical 
description will require a theory that goes beyond both qua ntum mechanics and general relativity as 
currently formulated [7]. The conceptual framework proposed here does not provide such a theory, and 
it does not claim to. What it claims is more modest but, in a certain sense, more fundamental: that the 
apparent conflict between quantum mechanics and general relativity at the conceptual level—the level 
at which their basic categories and their relationship are understood —is not a genuine logical 
inconsistency, but a consequence of applying two complementary descriptions simultaneously at the 
same level [8]. Recognising this does not solve quantum gravity, but it does clarify what quantum 
gravity needs to solve: not a contradiction between two correct theories, but the challenge of 
constructing a description adequate to the physical regime where both quantum and gravitational effects 
are simultaneously relevant at the same scale. 
 
4.2 Implications and Limits 
The conceptual resolution proposed in this paper has several implications for how the relationship 
between quantum mechanics and general relativity should be understood, and for how the problem of 
quantum gravity should be approached. It also has clear lim its, which are important to state explicitly

===== PAGE 26 =====
26 
 
in order to prevent the argument from being either over -extended or dismissed as irrelevant to the 
genuine physical challenges. 
The first implication concerns the status of the measurement problem. The standard framing of the 
quantum gravity problem is entangled with the measurement problem of quantum mechanics —the 
problem of explaining why measurements have definite outcomes, given that the Schrödinger equation 
predicts that a quantum system and a measuring apparatus should, after interaction, be in an entangled 
superposition rather than in a definite outcome state [50]. The two problems are connected because the 
apparent need to relate a quantum superposition of matter to a classical spacetime geometry is sharpened 
by the unresolved status of quantum measurement: if it is unclear what it means for a quantum system 
to have a definite state, it is correspondingly unclear what it means for the gravitational field to be 
determined by that state. 
The conceptual framework of this paper does not solve the measurement problem. It does not explain 
why measurements have definite outcomes, or select among the available interpretations of quantum 
mechanics. What it does is show that the connection between  the measurement problem and the 
quantum gravity problem is less tight than it appears. The argument that the gravitational description 
applies to the actual configuration of matter, not to the quantum probability structure, does not depend 
on a resolution  of the measurement problem; it is compatible with any interpretation of quantum 
mechanics that takes quantum probability seriously as a feature of physical reality, including many -
worlds, Copenhagen, and decoherence-based accounts [25]. The conceptual resolution of the quantum-
gravity conflict is therefore not hostage to the resolution of the measurement problem, even though the 
two problems are related. 
The second implication concerns the semiclassical approximation. Semiclassical gravity, as 
described in Chapter 3, treats the spacetime geometry as classical while replacing the classical energy-
momentum tensor with its quantum expectation value. This is k nown to be an approximation, valid in 
regimes where quantum fluctuations of the gravitational field are negligible. The conceptual framework 
of this paper offers a natural account of why this approximation works as well as it does: the expectation 
value of  the energy -momentum tensor represents the effective classical source obtained by coarse -
graining the underlying quantum description [14]—the gravitational source term appropriate to the level 
of description at which the classical spacetime geometry is defined. Using the expectation value as the 
source of the gravitational field is, on this account, not an arbitrary choice but a physically motivated 
one: it represents the actual energy-momentum distribution as closely as possible within the constraints 
of the quantum description. 
This also clarifies the sense in which the semiclassical approximation breaks down. It breaks down 
when the quantum fluctuations of the energy -momentum tensor are large —when the actual energy -
momentum configuration realised in a given interaction differs significantly from the expectation value. 
In this regime, the expectation value is no longer a good estimate of the actual configuration, and the 
semiclassical approximation accordingly fails. A complete quantum gravity theory must account for the

===== PAGE 27 =====
27 
 
regime where fluctuations are large, and the conceptual framework of this paper suggests that what such 
a theory needs to provide is a description of the relationship between the quantum probability structure 
of matter and the spacetime geometry at the level where both are simultaneously relevant—a description 
that does not simply replace the quantum state with its expectation value, but engages with the full 
probability structure of the quantum state and its implications for spacetime geometry. 
The third implication concerns the black hole information paradox. The information paradox arises 
from the apparent conflict between the unitarity of quantum evolution —the principle that quantum 
information is conserved—and the prediction of general relativity that black holes evaporate through 
Hawking radiation, apparently destroying the information about the matter that formed the black hole. 
The paradox is one of the deepest unsolved problems at the intersection of qua ntum mechanics and 
general relativity, and its resolution is widely regarded as one of the key tests that any complete quantum 
gravity theory must pass [51]. 
The conceptual framework of this paper does not resolve the information paradox. It does not provide 
a mechanism by which information is preserved during black hole evaporation, or a description of the 
microscopic degrees of freedom responsible for black h ole entropy. But it does suggest a perspective 
on the paradox that is consistent with the broader argument: the paradox arises, at least in part, from 
applying the quantum description and the gravitational description simultaneously and at the same 
level—specifically, from asking what happens to quantum information in a classical spacetime 
geometry, when the classical geometry itself is the result of applying the gravitational description to a 
regime where the quantum description should be primary. A comple te resolution of the information 
paradox will likely require the kind of theory that the conceptual framework of this paper points toward: 
a description that does not treat the gravitational geometry as a fixed classical background but as 
something that must be consistently related to the quantum state of matter at every level. 
The limits of the argument are equally important to state. The conceptual resolution proposed here is 
not a physical theory. It does not produce equations, predictions, or calculational tools. It does not 
describe the Planck -scale physics that a complete q uantum gravity theory must provide. It does not 
explain how spacetime emerges from a more fundamental quantum description, or what the 
fundamental degrees of freedom of quantum gravity are. These are genuine physical questions that 
require genuine physical answers, and the conceptual argument of this paper does not provide them. 
The argument is also not a claim that quantum gravity research is misguided. The existing 
approaches—loop quantum gravity, string theory, canonical quantum gravity, and others—are serious 
and sophisticated attempts to construct a physical theory adequate to the Planck-scale regime [52]. The 
conceptual framework of this paper does not compete with these approaches; it is prior to them, in the 
sense that it concerns the conceptual framing of the problem rather than its technical solution. A clearer 
conceptual framing does not make the technical problem easier in any direct sense, but it may prevent 
certain kinds of confusion and suggest certain kinds of approaches over others.

===== PAGE 28 =====
28 
 
Finally, the argument does not claim that the two theories are fully compatible in all respects, or that 
no new physics is required at the Planck scale. The claim is more limited: that the apparent conceptual 
incompatibility between quantum mechanics and general relativity —the apparent impossibility of 
relating their basic categories to each other without contradiction —is not a genuine logical 
inconsistency. The two theories can coexist within a coherent conceptual framework, as complementary 
descriptions of different aspects of physical reality, without either being i n logical conflict with the 
other. This is not the same as saying that no new physics is needed; it is saying that the motivation for 
new physics should not be the apparent logical contradiction between two correct theories, but the 
genuine physical challenges that arise at the Planck scale, where both quantum and gravitational effects 
are simultaneously relevant. 
 
4.3 Relationship to Future Quantum Gravity 
The conceptual framework proposed in this paper has a specific relationship to the project of quantum 
gravity that is worth making explicit. It is not a proposal to stop doing quantum gravity research, or a 
claim that the existing approaches are unnecessary. It is a proposal about the conceptual preconditions 
that any satisfactory quantum gravity theory must meet—the conceptual problems that must be solved 
before, or alongside, the technical ones. 
The central precondition that this paper identifies is the following: any complete quantum gravity 
theory must provide a consistent account of the relationship between the quantum description of matter 
and the geometric description of spacetime, at every l evel of physical description from the classical 
macroscopic regime to the Planck scale. This requires, in particular, a clear account of how the quantum 
probability structure of matter relates to the spacetime geometry—not by requiring a definite classical 
geometry to correspond to every quantum state, which is the assumption that generates the apparent 
conflict, but by providing a description adequate to the full range of physical situations, including those 
in which the quantum probability structure of ma tter has direct implications for the structure of 
spacetime. 
This precondition is, in a sense, more fundamental than the technical challenges of quantum gravity. 
It is a requirement of conceptual consistency: any theory that describes the gravitational implications 
of a quantum matter state must do so in a way that is consistent with what quantum mechanics says 
about that state. The existing approaches to quantum gravity all meet this requirement in their own 
ways—loop quantum gravity by quantising the geometry itself, string theory by replacing both matter 
and geometry with a more fundamental description, semiclassical gravity by using the expectation value 
of the energy-momentum tensor. But none of them has fully resolved the conceptual problem identified 
in this paper: the problem of understanding what it means for  the gravitational description and the

===== PAGE 29 =====
29 
 
quantum description to be consistent at the level of their basic categories, rather than at the level of their 
technical formalisms. 
The conceptual framework of this paper suggests that the consistency requirement is best understood 
as a requirement of level-appropriate description. The quantum description of matter is appropriate to 
the level of possible outcomes and probability struct ures; the gravitational description of spacetime is 
appropriate to the level of actual configurations and definite geometries. A complete quantum gravity 
theory must bridge these two levels —it must provide a description that is adequate to the physical 
situation where both levels are simultaneously relevant, and that recovers the quantum description and 
the gravitational description as appropriate limiting cases in the regimes where each is separately 
applicable. 
This is a more precise formulation of the quantum gravity problem than the standard one. The 
standard formulation asks: how do we construct a theory in which the gravitational field has quantum 
properties? The formulation suggested by the present argument asks: how do we construct a theory that 
describes the relationship between quantum probability structures of matter and spacetime geometry, 
in a way that is consistent at every level of description and recovers both quantum mechanics and 
general relativity in their respective domains? These are related questions, but they are not identical, 
and the difference between them may be significant for how the problem is approached. 
One implication of this reformulation concerns the role of observers and measurement in quantum 
gravity. If the quantum description of matter applies to probability structures and the gravitational 
description applies to actual configurations, then the pro cess by which probability structures are 
converted into actual configurations —the process of physical interaction and decoherence —becomes 
relevant to the question of how quantum matter and classical spacetime are related. In a complete 
quantum gravity theory, this process will presumably be described by the theory itself, rather than being 
assumed as a given. The question of how measurement and decoherence work in a theory where 
spacetime itself has quantum properties is one of the deepest open questions in  the foundations of 
quantum gravity [53], and the conceptual framework of this paper points toward it as a central rather 
than peripheral issue. 
A further implication, noted here speculatively, concerns the arrow of ti me [54]. Both the Einstein 
field equations and the Schrödinger equation are time-symmetric; yet the physical world is manifestly 
time-asymmetric. The conceptual framework of this paper —in which the transition from quantum 
probability structures to physically realised configurations plays a central role —suggests a possible 
connection: the asymmetry between possible and actual may bear on the asymmetry between past and 
future. Developing this suggestion into a precise argument would require resources beyond what this  
paper provides, but it indicates a direction in which the present framework may connect to broader 
foundational questions. 
The broader point is that the conceptual realignment proposed in this paper, if correct, does not 
merely dissolve an apparent contradiction; it reframes the quantum gravity problem in a way that

===== PAGE 30 =====
30 
 
suggests new questions and new connections. The standard framing of quantum gravity as the problem 
of resolving a fundamental conflict between two correct theories leads naturally to the search for a new 
theory that supersedes both. The reframing proposed here—quantum gravity as the problem of 
constructing a description adequate to the physical regime where quantum probability structures and 
spacetime geometry are both simultaneously relevant —leads naturally to a different set of questions: 
about the relationship between quantum description and classical description, about the role of physical 
interaction and decoherence in the emergence of classical geometry, and about the conceptual 
preconditions that any satisfactory quantum gravity theory must meet. 
 
4.4 The Interface Between Quantum States and Spacetime Geometry 
The conceptual argument of the preceding sections can be given a preliminary formal expression. 
This is not a new physical law, and it is not a claim that the unification of quantum mechanics and 
general relativity has been achieved. It is a mapping struct ure—a formal articulation of the interface 
between the quantum description of matter and the geometric description of spacetime—that identifies 
what a complete unified theory must provide, and what the correct mathematical relationship between 
the two frameworks must look like. 
General relativity requires a definite energy -momentum tensor as the gravitational source. This 
requirement is expressed in the Einstein field equations: 
 
Gμν + Λgμν = (8πG / c⁴) Tμν 
 
The left-hand side, Gμν, is the Einstein tensor encoding the curvature of spacetime. The right -hand 
side, Tμν, is the energy -momentum tensor of matter, which must be a definite classical object for the 
equations to determine a unique spacetime geometry. Th is is the structural requirement that quantum 
mechanics, in its standard form, does not straightforwardly satisfy. 
Quantum mechanics describes matter through a state vector in a Hilbert space. For a system in 
superposition of two configurations A and B: 
 
|ψ⟩ = α|A⟩ + β|B⟩ 
 
The amplitudes α and β encode the probability structure of the system: |α|² and |β|² are the probabilities 
that physical interaction will realise configuration A or B respectively. The quantum state is not two 
simultaneous classical configurations existing in parallel; it is the complete probability structure of one 
physical system. The multiplicity is in the space of possible outcomes, not in the number of physical 
entities. 
The standard framing of the quantum gravity problem implicitly assumes a direct mapping from 
quantum state to spacetime geometry:

===== PAGE 31 =====
31 
 
 
|ψ⟩ ⟶ gμν 
 
This direct identification is what generates the apparent inconsistency: a quantum state in 
superposition cannot determine a unique classical metric. A superposed |ψ⟩ fed directly into the Einstein 
field equations produces no well -defined gμν. Many approaches to quantum gravity respond to this 
difficulty by treating spacetime geometry itself as a quantum degree of freedom.  The direct mapping 
may therefore represent the source of the conceptual difficulty, rather than the classical character of the 
metric itself. 
The conceptual interface proposed in this paper replaces the direct mapping with a staged one: 
 
|ψ⟩  —[ᴵ]—⟶  Cᵢ  ⟶  Tμν(Cᵢ)  ⟶  Gμν 
 
Here ᴵ denotes the physical process by which a quantum state gives rise to a gravitationally relevant 
configuration. ᴵ does not denote conscious measurement or observation; it denotes the process—whether 
decoherence, physical interaction, or classical emergence —by which the quantum description gives 
way to a configuration at the level where the gravitational description is properly defined. The energy-
momentum tensor T μν(Cᵢ) corresponds to that configuration, and the spacetime geometry G μν is 
defined at the level of Cᵢ, not at the level of |ψ⟩ directly. The quantum description and the gravitational 
description are not applied simultaneously to the same object; they are applied at successive levels of 
the physical description, connected by the interface ᴵ. 
The architecture of a complete unified theory, on this account, must provide a mathematically well-
defined version of the map ᴵ. This means specifying, in general, the relationship between the Hilbert 
space of matter states and the space of spacetime geometries: 
 
𝒢 : ℋₘₐₜₜₑᵣ  ⟶  ℳₛₚₐ⁣ₑₜᵢₘₑ 
 
where ℋₘₐₜₜₑᵣ is the Hilbert space of quantum matter states and ℳₛₚₐ⁣ₑₜᵢₘₑ is the space of physically 
admissible spacetime geometries. The central claim of this paper is that 𝒢 cannot be constructed as a 
direct point-to-point identification of |ψ ⟩ with gμν. It must be constructed as a staged mapping that 
passes through the intermediate level of classically realisable configurations —the level at which 
definite energy-momentum distributions exist and at which the Einstein field equations are properly 
defined. 
Any candidate unified theory must satisfy three conditions that follow from this framework. First, it 
must recover standard quantum mechanics in the limit where gravitational effects are negligible: the 
probability structure of |ψ⟩ must be preserved at scales where spacetime curvature plays no role. Second, 
it must recover general relativity in the classical limit: when the matter system is in a state that 
corresponds to a well -defined classical configuration, T μν must reduce to the  standard energy -
momentum tensor and the Einstein field equations must hold without modification. Third, it must

===== PAGE 32 =====
32 
 
specify the conditions under which the interface ᴵ is well-defined—that is, the physical conditions under 
which a quantum state gives rise to a classically realisable configuration that can serve as a gravitational 
source. It is precisely in specifying these conditions at the Planck scale that the technical work of 
quantum gravity must be done. 
The logical structure of this proposal has a precedent in the history of physics. The unification of 
electricity and magnetism did not begin with Maxwell's equations; it began with Faraday's conceptual 
insight that electric and magnetic phenomena were aspects of a single field. Maxwell's equations—the 
mathematical completion of that unification —came later, once the correct conceptual relationship 
between the two domains had been established [55]. The present paper proposes an analogous first step 
for quantum mechanics and general relativity: not the mathematical completion of their unification, but 
the conceptual framework within which such a completion becomes possible. The identification of the 
staged interface 𝒢, and the replacement of the direct mapping | ψ⟩ ⟶ gμν with the correct staged 
mapping |ψ⟩ → Cᵢ → Tμν(Cᵢ) → Gμν, is the conceptual step that this paper offers as a foundation for 
the formal unification that remains to be built. 
The formal development of 𝒢—specifying its mathematical structure, its domain conditions, and its 
behaviour at the Planck scale—constitutes the programme of work that this framework makes possible 
[56]. Existing approaches to quantum gravity can be understood, on this account, as different attempts 
to specify 𝒢 under different assumptions: loop quantum gravity quantises the geometric side of the 
mapping [45]; string theory replaces both sides with a more fundamental structure from which both 
emerge; semiclassical gravity approximates 𝒢 by replacing Tμν(Cᵢ) with its quantum expectation value. 
Each approach captures part of the structure that a complete 𝒢 must have. What the present framework 
contributes is the identification of the conceptual error that has made the construction of 𝒢 appear 
impossible: the assumption that it must be a direct mapping rather than a staged one. Once that 
assumption is released, the construction of a unified theory connecting quantum mechanics and general 
relativity becomes not a logical impossibility but an open and well-defined formal problem [35]. 
4.5 Conclusion 
This paper has developed a conceptual argument with a specific and limited aim: to show that the 
apparent incompatibility between quantum mechanics and general relativity, at the level of their basic 
categories and their relationship, is not a genuine logical inconsistency, but a consequence of a category 
error in the way their relationship has been understood. 
The argument proceeds in three steps. The first, developed in Chapter 1, is that the conflict between 
the two theories has been framed in a way that assumes they are attempting to describe the same aspect 
of physical reality at the same level of descriptio n. This assumption is not obviously correct, and 
examining it is a necessary precondition for understanding whether the conflict is genuine.

===== PAGE 33 =====
33 
 
The second step, developed in Chapter 2, is that quantum superposition does not entail the 
simultaneous existence of multiple classical realities. The wave function describes the probability 
structure of a single physical system —the range of possible outcomes for interactions with other 
systems, and the probabilities associated with each outcome. This probability structure is a real feature 
of the physical world, but it is not a description of multiple simultaneously existing configurations. The 
transition from quantum description to classical description is mediated by physical interaction and 
decoherence, not by any mysterious or subjective process. 
The third step, developed in Chapter 3, is that the gravitational description of spacetime applies to 
the actual configuration of matter—the definite energy-momentum distribution that is realised through 
physical interaction—rather than to the quantum probability structure of matter prior to interaction. The 
two descriptions are therefore not competing descriptions of the same physical moment; they are 
descriptions of different aspects of the same physical situation, and their  apparent inconsistency 
dissolves when this distinction is recognised. 
The resolution proposed here is complementary, not competing, with the existing approaches to 
quantum gravity. It does not provide a technical solution to the Planck -scale physics that a complete 
quantum gravity theory must describe. What it provides is a conceptual framework within which 
quantum mechanics and general relativity can coexist as complementary descriptions of different 
aspects of physical reality, without either being in logical conflict with the other. This framework 
reframes the quantum grav ity problem: not as the problem of resolving a fundamental contradiction 
between two correct theories, but as the problem of constructing a description adequate to the physical 
regime where both quantum probability structures and spacetime geometry are simultaneously relevant 
at the same scale. 
Quantum mechanics and general relativity are not two theories in irreconcilable conflict, awaiting 
the discovery of a third theory that will supersede them both. They are two descriptions of different 
aspects of the same physical world —one describing the probability structure of possible physical 
outcomes, the other describing the geometric structure associated with actual physical configurations. 
The apparent conflict between them is real, in the sense that it arises from a genuine difficulty in relating 
their respective descriptions. But it is not a logical impossibility: it is a conceptual challenge, and one 
that becomes more tractable when the two theories are understood as complementary rather than 
competing. The bridge between them is not a new equation or a new physical mechanism; it is a clearer 
understanding of what each theory is describing, and of the relationship between the levels of 
description at which each operates. 
The relationship between quantum mechanics and general relativity is one of the deepest open 
questions in the foundations of physics. This paper has argued that part of the difficulty in addressing 
it comes from a misunderstanding of the relationship betwe en the two theories —a misunderstanding 
that, once corrected, opens a more coherent picture of how the quantum and gravitational descriptions 
of nature fit together. That picture is incomplete: many questions remain open, and a full account of

===== PAGE 34 =====

quantum gravity will go far beyond anything argued here. What this paper has aimed to provide is not 
a complete unified theory, but a conceptual and formal framework that clarifies the nature of the 
problem, identifies the interface that any future unified theory must explain, and establishes a coherent 
direction for its mathematical development.
