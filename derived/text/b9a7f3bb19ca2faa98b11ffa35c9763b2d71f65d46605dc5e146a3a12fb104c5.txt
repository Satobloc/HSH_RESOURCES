Coupling Electronic Topology to Phonons and Magnetism in
Semimetals
Xiaohan Yao
Advisor: Fazel Tafti, Ph.D.
ABSTRACT
Semimetallic quantum materials provide a platform in which electronic structure, lat-
tice dynamics, and magnetic order can become strongly intertwined. While many of
these materials are introduced through symmetry-constrained band crossings, Berry
curvature, or related band-structure features, their experimentally observed proper-
ties are shaped by phonons, spin textures, and the scattering processes that connect
them to itinerant electrons. This dissertation investigates how phonons and mag-
netism couple to semimetallic electronic states and how these couplings appear in
transport and scattering measurements.
The first part of the dissertation focuses on the nonmagnetic MX 2 semimetal
family, where M = Nb or Ta and X = Ge or Si. By combining electrical transport,
quantum oscillations, Raman scattering, and first-principles calculations, this work
examines the origin of anomalously large transport mobility. The results show that
phonon-electron dominated scattering can enhance conductivity by returning momen-
tum to the electronic system, establishing lattice dynamics as an active ingredient in
charge transport.
The second part turns to magnetic Weyl semimetals, where electronic topology can
couple directly to local-moment magnetism. In SmAlSi, neutron diffraction, magne-
tization, heat-capacity, transport, and electronic-structure analysis reveal spiral mag-
netic order, a field-induced A-phase, and a large topological Hall effect, suggesting

a mechanism in which topological Fermi pockets participate in stabilizing complex
magnetic order. Across the CeAlSi1−xGex series, Ge-rich compositions develop incom-
mensurate magnetism and singular angular magnetoresistance, providing an electrical
fingerprint of the underlying magnetic texture.
Together, these studies show that topology in semimetals should not be viewed
as an isolated band-structure property. Instead, electronic topology, phonons, and
magnetism form coupled systems whose interactions produce measurable transport,
magnetic, and spectroscopic responses.

CHAPTER I
Introduction
Topological and topologically related semimetals have emerged as a central plat-
form for studying electronic states whose properties are constrained by symmetry,
spin-orbit coupling, and band geometry. Their band structures can host Dirac or
Weyl crossings, Fermi-arc surface states, large Berry curvature, or other symmetry-
constrained electronic features, leading to unusual responses to electric, magnetic, and
optical fields. These features have motivated intense interest in semimetals both as
fundamental quantum materials and as possible platforms for electronic, spintronic,
and optoelectronic applications.
Much of the language used to describe topological semimetals begins from a single-
particle band picture. In this view, the most important ingredients are the crystal
symmetry, spin-orbit coupling, band inversion, and the location of topological band
crossings relative to the Fermi level. This picture is essential, but it is not complete.
Real materials contain phonons, defects, electronic correlations, local moments, mag-
netic domains, and structural imperfections. Electrons scatter from these degrees of
freedom, exchange energy and momentum with them, and in some cases help stabilize
them. As a result, the experimentally measured properties of topological semimetals
can reflect not only the topology of the electronic structure, but also the way that
topology is coupled to lattice vibrations, magnetic textures, and ordered states.
1

The central theme of this dissertation is that electronic structure should be stud-
ied together with the lattice and magnetic degrees of freedom to which it couples.
In the materials discussed here, phonons and spin textures are not passive back-
grounds. Phonons can control whether electronic momentum is relaxed or returned
to the electron system. Magnetic order can be shaped by the topology and geometry
of the electronic structure and can, in turn, generate unusual Hall and magnetore-
sistance responses. Transport measurements provide one of the most direct ways to
observe these effects, but transport is treated here as a window into a broader phys-
ical question: how do semimetallic electrons, phonons, and magnetism form coupled
quantum-material systems?
This dissertation addresses that question through three material platforms. The
first is the nonmagneticMX 2 semimetal family, whereM = Nb or Ta andX = Ge or
Si. In this family, chemical substitution tunes the electronic and phonon structures
while preserving the essential crystal structure, allowing transport to be compared
with phonon and band-structure calculations across a controlled chemical series (Yao*
and Plisson* et al., 2024). The second platform is SmAlSi, a magnetic Weyl semimetal
in which spiral magnetic order and a large topological Hall effect point to a close rela-
tionship between Weyl electronic states and local-moment magnetism (Yao, Gaudet,
et al., 2023). The third platform is the CeAlSi 1−xGex series, where Ge substitution
drives the system toward incommensurate magnetism and singular angular magne-
toresistance as an electrical fingerprint of complex magnetic order (Yao, Chen, et al.,
2026).
1.1 Topological Semimetals
Topological semimetals are characterized by band crossings near the Fermi level
that are protected or constrained by crystal symmetry and topology. In Dirac
semimetals, fourfold-degenerate Dirac points can occur when both time-reversal
2

===== PAGE 21 =====
and inversion symmetries, together with additional crystal symmetries, protect the
crossing. In Weyl semimetals, either time-reversal or inversion symmetry is broken,
and a Dirac point can split into pairs of Weyl nodes with opposite chirality. Near a
Weyl node, the electronic dispersion is approximately linear, and the node acts as a
source or sink of Berry curvature in momentum space (Hu et al., 2019).
The presence of Berry curvature gives Weyl semimetals a natural connection to
anomalous transport phenomena. Depending on symmetry, magnetic order, and
Fermi-level position, these materials can show unusual magnetoresistance, anoma-
lous Hall responses, chiral-anomaly-related transport, and surface Fermi arcs. These
effects make topological semimetals powerful systems for exploring the relationship
between electronic structure and measurable response functions.
At the same time, topology alone rarely determines the full experimental behav-
ior. Two materials with similar topological classifications can show very different
mobilities, scattering rates, magnetic ground states, and field responses. This sensi-
tivity reflects the importance of material-specific ingredients: carrier density, disorder,
phonon spectra, magnetic exchange interactions, anisotropy, and Fermi-surface geom-
etry. A major goal of this dissertation is to understand how these ingredients interact
with topology rather than treating them as secondary complications.
1.2 Transport as a Probe of Coupled Degrees of Freedom
Electrical transport is one of the most widely used probes of topological semimet-
als because it is sensitive to carrier density, mobility, scattering, magnetic order, and
Berry curvature. Longitudinal resistivity reveals how efficiently charge carriers move
through a material, while Hall measurements provide information about carrier type,
density, mobility, and anomalous contributions. Magnetoresistance and angular mag-
netoresistance further reveal how electronic motion depends on field strength and
field direction.

In this dissertation, transport plays two related roles. In the MX 2 compounds,
transport is the central phenomenon to be explained: the measured transport mobil-
ity can be much larger than the single-particle mobility, suggesting that the processes
limiting individual quasiparticle lifetimes do not necessarily relax the total electronic
momentum. In SmAlSi and the CeAlSi 1−xGex series, transport is not the only story.
Instead, Hall and magnetoresistance measurements act as signatures of deeper cou-
pling between electronic topology and magnetic order. This distinction is important
for the overall logic of the thesis: transport is both a phenomenon in its own right
and a diagnostic tool for coupled electronic, lattice, and magnetic behavior.
1.3 Electron-Phonon Coupling and Phonon Drag
In ordinary metals, electron-phonon scattering is often treated as a process that
limits conductivity. An electron accelerated by an electric field scatters from a
phonon, transfers momentum to the lattice, and thereby contributes to resistive dis-
sipation. This picture is appropriate when phonons efficiently lose their momentum
through phonon-phonon scattering, defects, or boundaries.
However, a different regime can occur when phonon-electron scattering is stronger
than phonon-phonon scattering. In that case, a phonon that receives momentum from
one electron can return that momentum to another electron before it decays into the
broader phonon bath. The scattering of an individual electron may still be strong,
but the total momentum of the coupled electron-phonon system can be partially
preserved. This produces a phonon-drag-like enhancement of charge transport.
The MX 2 chapter examines this physics directly. By comparing transport mo-
bility, quantum mobility, Raman linewidths, and first-principles calculations across
NbGe2, TaGe2, NbSi2, and TaSi2, the study tests whether phonon-electron dominated
scattering can account for anomalously large transport mobility (Yao* and Plisson*
et al., 2024). The key idea is that the phonon system can enhance, rather than

momentum to the electron system.
1.4 Magnetic Weyl Semimetals
Magnetic Weyl semimetals introduce magnetic order as an additional active degree
of freedom. In these systems, local moments and itinerant electronic states coexist,
and their coupling can produce phenomena that are absent in nonmagnetic semimet-
als. Magnetic order can break time-reversal symmetry, reconstruct electronic bands,
generate Berry curvature, and create anomalous Hall responses. Conversely, the elec-
tronic structure can influence the magnetic interactions between local moments.
The rare-earth aluminum silicides and germanides studied in this dissertation are
especially useful for exploring this interplay. Their noncentrosymmetric crystal struc-
tures support Weyl electronic states, while the rare-earth ions provide local magnetic
moments. This combination allows the topology of the electronic bands and the mag-
netism of the local moments to become intertwined. In SmAlSi, the spiral magnetic
order is connected to the topology and nesting of the electronic structure (Gaudet
et al., 2021; Yao, Gaudet, et al., 2023). In the CeAlSi 1−xGex series, incommensurate
magnetic order produces a singular angular magnetoresistance response, showing how
transport can detect complex magnetic textures (Puphal et al., 2020; Yao, Chen, et
al., 2026).
These materials therefore motivate a broader view of magnetic Weyl semimetals.
Rather than treating magnetism only as a way to create Weyl nodes, one can ask
how Weyl electrons participate in magnetic exchange, how magnetic textures reshape
transport, and how transport measurements reveal the underlying spin structure.
This viewpoint provides the basis for the SmAlSi and CeAlSi 1−xGex chapters.

1.5 Dissertation Overview
Chapter II summarizes the experimental and computational methods used
throughout the dissertation, including crystal growth, structural characterization,
magnetotransport, Raman scattering, neutron diffraction, thermodynamic measure-
ments, and first-principles calculations.
Chapter III focuses on electron-phonon coupling and enhanced transport in MX 2
semimetals. This chapter asks how phonons can mediate momentum flow in a
semimetal and shows that phonon-electron dominated scattering provides a mech-
anism for anomalously large transport mobility.
Chapter IV discusses topology-magnetism coupling in SmAlSi. The chapter cen-
ters on spiral magnetic order, its connection to topological electronic structure, and
the large topological Hall effect that emerges as a transport signature of the magnetic
texture.
Chapter V examines incommensurate magnetism and singular angular magnetore-
sistance across the CeAlSi 1−xGex series. This chapter shows how angular magneto-
transport can reveal complex magnetic order in a magnetic Weyl semimetal.
Chapter VI summarizes the unified physical picture developed across the disser-
tation. Together, the three projects show that semimetallic quantum materials are
best understood as coupled systems in which electronic topology, lattice dynamics,
and magnetism jointly shape the observed material response.
