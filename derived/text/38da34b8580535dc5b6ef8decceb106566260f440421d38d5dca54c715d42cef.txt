Recently, a host of exciting magnetic textures such as topologically protected skyrmion lattices has been
discovered in several bulk metallic lanthanide compounds. In addition to hosting skyrmion phases, a
hallmark of this class of materials is the appearance of numerous spin textures characterized by
superposition of multiple magnetic modulations: spin moir´ e superlattices. In order to understand the
multitude of complex phases often present in these materials, we require a general-purpose experimental
and theoretical framework. Here, we demonstrate such an approach in EuAg 4Sb2 by comprehensively
characterizing and modeling its three complex zero-field magnetic textures. Systematic symmetry-breaking
experiments using uniaxial strain determine that the ground-state incommensurate magnetic phase (ICM1)
is single q, meaning the magnetic moments modulate along one magnetic propagation vector. In contrast,
ICM2 and ICM3 are both double q, meaning they are formed from the superposition of two sinusoidal spin
modulations, i.e., spin moir´e superlattices. Further, through application of polarized small-angle neutron
scattering and spherical neutron polarimetry, we demonstrate that ICM1 is a single-q cycloid and ICM2 and
ICM3 are double- q vortex lattices. Despite the quasi-two-dimensional nature of EuAg 4Sb2, the
modulations propagate out of the ab plane, leading to a shift of the spin texture between triangular
lattice planes. Further, the ICM3 to ICM2 transition includes an unusual 45° rotation of the magnetic vortex
lattice. Motivated by the coexistence of such drastically different phases in this compound, we conclude by
developing a phenomenological model that sheds light on the energetic origins of these varied phases. Our
experimental probes and theoretical modeling definitively characterize three different and tunable phases in
one material and provide insight for the design of topological spin-texture materials.

I. INTRODUCTION
Density wave instabilities are ubiquitous in quantum
materials and are often intertwined with other emergent
phenomena of interest [1,2]. Generally, the presence of
such modulations represents a connection between the
electronic structure and other degrees of freedom of the
system. In particular, multi- q magnetism formed from the
interference of multiple incommensurate magnetic modu-
lations, which are known as spin moir´ e superlattices
(SMSs), are of great interest due to their potential spin-
tronics applications [3]. As these SMSs may, in general, be
formed from any combination of potentially low symmetry
modulations of varying character, experimental determi-
nation of their precise nature can be challenging. Here,
using EuAg4Sb2 as a model material, we outline a general
approach that may be utilized in any material with complex
single-q or multi- q incommensurate magnetic (ICM)
phases, even in the presence of many coexisting domains.
EuAg4Sb2 falls into a family of quasi-two-dimensional
(quasi-2D) lanthanide materials that have attracted

A. Determination of domains and multi- q nature
A concise demonstration of the magnetic domain struc-
ture is possible by cooling the sample with applied uniaxial
strain, with additional evidence presented in Supplemental
Material, Sec. I [20]. The unstrained crystal has a uniform
population of domains [see Figs. 2(d)–2(f)]. Upon applica-
tion of tension (elongating the crystal along the ½−1; 2; 0/C138
direction, see Methods), one pair of peaks becomes dom-
inant in ICM1, while two sets of peaks become dominant in
ICM2 and ICM3 [see Figs. 2(a)–2(c)]. The application of
compression favors the peaks that become weaker under
tension [see Figs. 2(g)–2(i)]. The small residual intensity in
the minority peaks under tension or compression are likely
either due to insufficient or inhomogeneous application of
strain or from scattering of the unstrained portion of the
crystal held by glue to the jaws. This experiment confirms
ICM1 is singleq, while ICM2 and ICM3 are doubleq. ICM2
has nearly perpendicular propagation vectors forming a
rhombic lattice, while ICM3 has orthogonal (and symmetry-
distinct) propagation vectors forming a rectangular lattice.
This observation identifies the appropriate propagation
vectors qi to use for each phase in Eq. (1). One domain
of peaks in ICM1 –3 is depicted in red in Figs. 1(c)–1(e),
visualized in 3D. Here, we focus on the magnetic domains of
one crystallographic twin. Further details of the connection
between crystallographic and magnetic domains are found
in Supplemental Material, Sec. I Supplemental Material
and Ref. [21].
With the modest applied strain (no more than approx-
imately 0.25%), the domains could be controlled by strain
cooling, though with more strain it may be possible to switch
domains directly within the ordered state. This strain control
of magnetic domains suggests that there must be some
magnetoelastic coupling that changes the relative energies of
the different magnetic domains upon the application of
strain. Applying strain along the b axis breaks the threefold
rotation symmetry about the c axis. Therefore, the red and
white domains depicted in Fig. 2 will still have the same
energy, but the black domain may now have a different
energy. Which domains are favored will depend in detail on
how the magnetic exchange parameters are affected by
distortions of the unit cell and interatomic distances.
Strain has been shown to control magnetic domain
populations in a variety of compounds[23–26]. In the related
EuAl4, the skyrmion lattice transitions are accompanied by
small (approximately 0.1%) tetragonal-to-orthorhombic
structural distortions [27]. If a similar lattice distortion is

condition, jMðrÞj ¼ M0, however, the (anti)vortex cores of
ICM2 and ICM3 do not. To reconcile this, we propose that,
at high temperature, the vortex cores may be either
disordered or fluctuating (this higher entropy compared
to ICM1 may also explain the stability of these phases at
higher temperature in zero field), and at low temperature, as
field is applied out-of plane, the cores of the vortices begin
to align with the applied field. This effect would produce a
small 2q1-type peak intensity in field, which is also
observed experimentally (see Supplemental Material,
Sec. I and Fig. S3 [20]). The equal-moment condition is
strictly valid for fully local, classical spins at zero temper-
ature, but is often a good approximation for Eu 2þ moments
due to the large moment size and strongly localized small
orbitals. Indeed, previous work found that the Eu 4f
orbitals are well below (approximately 1.5 eV) the Fermi
energy [8] and show no signs of Eu 3þ character [31].
Additionally, the Rhodes-Wohlfarth ratio [32] is approx-
imately 1.1, consistent with a local-moment picture. This
consideration of the equal-moment condition would
explain why ICM1 is favored at lower fields at T ¼ 0,
while ICM2 and ICM3 are allowed only with applied field
at T ¼ 0. This picture is also consistent with previous
descriptions of this effect in terms of a Landau free-energy
picture, where the equal-moment condition leads to a
preference for different Mi to be perpendicular, as in
ICM2 and ICM3, or for the components of one Mi to
be perpendicular as in ICM2 [33].

While the isotropic nature of the europium L ¼ 0 half

that classical dipole-dipole and spin-orbit coupling with

anisotropy due to different spin-orbit coupling and band

of the anisotropy (perhaps by chemical doping or uniaxial
strain) to be easy axis may prefer the formation of
skyrmions. Indeed, the skyrmion hosting EuAl 4 is only
weakly anisotropic, which may, in part, explain its ten-
dency toward skyrmion and helical order [41,42].
IV. CONCLUSION
EuAg4Sb2 is an exemplary case of how conduction
electron-mediated spin interactions can lead to a rich
coexistence of spin moir´e superlattices that intertwine with
electronic degrees of freedom. While previous work in
Ref. [8] established the existence of multiple incommen-
surate phases in EuAg 4Sb2, here we fully resolve their
domain structure and microscopic spin texture via polarized
SANS, SNP , and application of in situ strain, enabling the
first complete phase-by-phase mapping and modeling for
this material class. Combined with phenomenological
modeling, we are able to reveal both the nature and origins
of these complex spin textures — an approach with

In particular, we have demonstrated a domain-selective,

uniaxial strain to select symmetry-related magnetic
domains and comprehensive polarimetry analysis to deter-
mine vector chirality, we (i) distinguish single- q cycloidal

multi-q sequence and its anisotropic saturation behavior.
Beyond EuAg4Sb2, this combined domain-selective, polar-

where standard neutron diffraction measurements of peak
positions and intensities alone are clearly insufficient to
uniquely determine the real-space textures.

netic domains and symmetry-breaking uniaxial strain
represents a potentially fruitful route to the control of
multi-q magnetic domains. The vortex lattice phases
present in EuAg 4Sb2 therefore offer an ideal platform to
study strain-tunable spin moir´ e superlattices.
We also note that the system displays an easy-plane
anisotropy, where all three phases prefer modulated tex-
tures with spin components predominantly within the ab
plane. This anisotropy is consistent with the lower in-plane
saturation field observed in magnetization measurements.
If the easy-plane anisotropy is lifted, the system may
instead favor a skyrmion or meron-antimeron lattice state.
This insight suggests that probing bulk magnetic anisotropy
can provide insight into what phases may be present in a
frustrated system before the application of more sophisti-
cated techniques to determine the precise phases. This
observation also motivates targeted experiments such as in-
plane field experiments [43] and chemical or physical strain
tuning of interlayer thickness that may tune the system into
a phase with nonzero skyrmion number. Exploring the
tunability of the phase diagram would further test our
model parameters, providing a predictive loop between
experiment and theory.
Consideration of the equal-moment condition also jus-
tifies why the ground state at zero field is a single-q cycloid,
while SMS of vortex lattice type become favorable at
elevated temperatures or with applied field at T ¼ 0. The
ground-state ICM1 completely conserves the saturated
moment, and ICM2 and ICM3 only have the spin go to
zero at pointlike (anti)vortex core centers, whereas a
transverse spin modulation contains lines where the spin
goes to zero. At higher temperatures in ICM2 and ICM3,
the vortex and antivortex cores may be thermally fluctuat-
ing (stabilizing the phases via their greater entropy than
ICM1), while at low temperature and high field they may
become field polarized to satisfy the equal-moment con-
dition, explaining the small 2q1-type peaks observed (see
Supplemental Material, Sec. I [20]). These observations
provide insight into the design principles of multi- q
materials: The magnetic moment of Eu 2þ is relatively
isotropic (being an L ¼ 0 state) and local in character,
so the moment is largely Heisenberg-like and favors
any spin modulation that respects the equal-moment con-
dition. The addition of frustration and conduction electron-
mediated interactions further leads to a plethora of tunable
spin moir´e superlattice states. These insights extend to the
engineering of SMS phases in any lanthanide metal that
exhibits signs of frustration.
V. METHODS
A. Sample synthesis
Single crystals of EuAg 4Sb2 were synthesized via a self-
flux method described previously in Ref. [8]. The crystals
exhibit clear hexagonal or triangular platelet morphology
enabling straightforward alignment, with characteristic
crystal sizes of order 4 × 4 × 0.1 mm3.
B. Small-angle neutron scattering
SANS was performed at the Institut Laue-Langevin on
the D33 beamline, at the High Flux Isotope Reactor on the
GP-SANS beamline at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, and
the Swiss Spallation Neutron Source on the SANS-I
beamline at the Paul Scherrer Institut. Neutrons of wave-
length 3.1 –5 Å were used. The sample was rocked about
the vertical and/or horizontal axes to map a 3D volume of
reciprocal space. SANS measurements were analyzed in
GRASP [44] and in the GRIP module [45]. Every measure-
ment has a high-temperature background subtracted.
C. Strain SANS
For the uniaxial strain SANS experiment, measurements
were performed on SANS-I at PSI using a Razorbill
UC220T piezoelectrically driven strain cell with clearance
for a transmission scattering measurement on a bespoke
sample stick. The strain cell is equipped with calibrated
capacitive sensors that measure the displacement and force
applied to the crystal. This cell can apply up to 50 N or
25 μm of strain (depending on the Y oung ’s modulus and
length of the sample), with a standard sample size of
∼0.1 × 1 × 3 mm3. To reduce beam absorption by the
europium, 3.1 Å neutrons were used. A small portion of
sample in the titanium strain cell jaws was relatively
unstrained; small spot-size measurements confirmed that
less domain imbalance was present in this region.

D. Polarized SANS
Polarization analysis SANS [44,46,47] was performed
on the D33 beamline at the Institut Laue-Langevin in
Grenoble, France. The incident beam is spin polarized
along the vertical axis by an FeSi supermirror polarizer and
can be flipped with an rf spin flipper. The outgoing neutron
spin is measured with a cell of gaseous 3He, where the
nuclear spins are polarized to approximately 78%. The
neutrons are selectively absorbed by the 3He when their
spin is antialigned with the 3He nuclear spin [48,49]. The
polarization of the 3He is maintained with a constant field
chamber and can be flipped through the application of an rf
pulse in a so-called “magic box. ” In this experiment,
one quadrant of the SANS pattern was fully covered by
the 3He, allowing access to all symmetry-distinct peaks.
Additionally, the polarization efficiency was monitored by
regularly measuring the intensity of the direct beam in both
the spin-flip and non-spin-flip channels throughout the
course of the experiment to characterize the depolarization
of the 3He cell and allow for correction of the imperfect
polarization. The scattering intensity was then corrected for
nonmagnetic background and an imperfect flipping ratio to
the true polarization-dependent scattering cross sections

E. Spherical neutron polarimetry
Because of the relatively small wave vector of the ICM
phases, a cold neutron instrument is ideal to observe the
magnetic peaks in EuAg 4Sb2. Therefore, we employed the
cold triple-axis spectrometer ThALES at the Institut Laue-
Langevin in Grenoble, France, which can be equipped with
the cryogenic polarization analysis device (Cryopad)
spherical neutron polarimeter [52]. Analysis and visuali-
zation of SNP data were performed with Mag2Pol [53].
Both statistical and estimated systematic uncertainties
(following Ref. [54]) were included. The sample was
manually reloaded multiple times with different ψ (the
angle between the scattering plane and the a/C3 axis) to place
the different magnetic peaks of interest in the horizontal
scattering plane of the instrument: ψ ¼ 0° for the ðH0LÞ
peaks of ICM1 and ICM3, ψ ≈ 13.5° for the ICM2 peaks,
and ψ ¼ 30° for the ðHHLÞ ICM3 peaks. A beam
collimator and 5 Å neutrons were employed to provide
sufficient resolution to observe the magnetic peaks of
interest. The principle peaks in the zeroth zone appear at
a 2θ of 7.3° from the direct beam, which is a large enough
angle to measure reliably despite the elevated background
from the direct beam. In this configuration, the magnetic
satellites can be observed about the (000), (003), and
(006) peaks for all ψ, and the (101), (102), (104), and
(105) peaks can be accessed for the ψ ¼ 0 measurement in
the ðH0LÞ scattering plane. The satellite peaks observed
about the (003) and (006) sum together the propagation
vector from both crystallographic twins due to the broader
instrument resolution along Q. Additional details of the
polarization analysis may be found in Supplemental
Material [20] and Ref. [22].
