Quantum Coherence Theory
A Constraint-Based Framework for Classical Emergence
Matthew Ainsworth
Abstract
Contemporary approaches to physical unification typically emphasize dynamical laws, in-
terpretive frameworks, or information-theoretic reconstructions. Less attention is given to the
structural constraints that determine which physical descriptions can remain coherent and sta-
ble under interaction, scale transition, and aggregation. This paper develops a constraint-first
analysis of coherence, arguing that persistence and unification require restrictions on admissible
physical continuations that are not reducible to dynamics alone.
Within the framework referred to here as Quantum Coherence Theory (QCT), coherence is
treated not as a fragile quantum resource but as a structural property governed by interaction
geometry and constraint density. On this view, classical behavior emerges when accumulated
constraints sufficiently narrow admissible evolution, while quantum behavior re-emerges when
constraints are relaxed. The account does not introduce new dynamics, collapse mechanisms, or
modifications of quantum formalism, but instead clarifies the conditions under which existing
formalisms yield stable, unified descriptions.
The resulting constraints distinguish QCT from observer-centric, intrinsic-collapse, and
purely descriptive decoherence accounts, while remaining compatible with globally unitary dy-
namics. Formal analysis in later sections develops operational redundancy conditions, admissi-
bility constraints on joint states, and rigidity-style arguments linking redundant record structure
to the stability of classical descriptions. These results do not constitute a complete theory of
unification, but they narrow the space of physically admissible descriptions and clarify the
structural role of coherence in bridging quantum and classical regimes.

Scope, Commitments, and Structure
Quantum Coherence Theory (QCT) is presented as an explanatory framework concerned with
the structural conditions under which coherence, collapse, and classical behavior emerge in physical
systems. The analysis proceeds in two complementary stages.
Sections 1–10 develop the interpretive and conceptual foundation of the framework. These
sections introduce the constraint-first perspective adopted throughout the work and clarify how
coherence, admissibility, and persistence are to be understood within that explanatory posture.
Sections 12–16 then begin the mathematical formalization of these ideas. In these sections, opera-
tional redundancy conditions, admissibility constraints on joint states, and structural restrictions
on simultaneous record structures are developed in order to make the interpretive framework math-
ematically precise.
The formal development introduced here builds upon several established strands of research
in quantum information theory and decoherence studies. The treatment of environmental frag-
ments and redundant records follows the decoherence and quantum Darwinism program [1, 2, 3, 4].
Information-theoretic quantities such as entropy, mutual information, and accessible information
follow the standard formalism of Shannon information and its quantum extensions[7, 8, 9].
The contribution of the present work is not to rederive these foundations, but to reformulate
redundancy conditions as operational certification statements that act as structural constraints
on admissible joint states. The present work therefore establishes the structural program and its
initial formal development rather than a completed mathematical theory. Future work will focus on
extending and refining the formalism introduced here and exploring its consequences across broader
physical regimes.

1 Introduction
This section establishes the central problem addressed by QCT: while quantum mechanics
is empirically successful, it lacks an explanatory account of how classical definiteness emerges
from quantum possibility. This section motivates a constraint-first framework that reframes coher-
ence, collapse, and observation without invoking observer privilege, new dynamics, or metaphysical
claims.
1.1 The Interpretive Gap in Quantum Mechanics
Quantum mechanics is among the most empirically successful frameworks in the history of sci-
ence. Its mathematical formalism yields predictions of extraordinary precision across an immense
range of physical phenomena, from atomic spectra to solid-state behavior to high-energy interac-
tions. No competing theory has matched its predictive reach or experimental resilience. Yet this
success coexists with a persistent and widely acknowledged interpretive gap: while the theory pre-
dicts outcomes with remarkable accuracy, it does not explain, in structural terms, why physical
systems transition from quantum admissibility to classical definiteness.
This gap is not a failure of calculation, nor a lack of experimental confirmation. It is not
resolved by improved measurement, larger datasets, or more refined instrumentation. It is a gap
of explanatory linkage. The formalism specifies how probability amplitudes evolve, interfere, and
decohere under interaction, but it remains largely silent on the conditions under which superposed
possibilities cease to remain jointly admissible and instead manifest as stable, classical identities.
Quantum theory describes what is allowed. Classical physics describes what persists. The
connection between these two descriptive regimes is operationally managed but not structurally
explained.
Existing interpretations address this gap in different ways, but often by displacing rather than
resolving it. Some elevate probabilistic postulates to foundational status, treating collapse or out-
come selection as irreducible features of reality. Others invoke observer-relative updates, epistemic
bookkeeping, or branching descriptions that preserve the formalism while shifting the burden of
explanation onto interpretation or ontology. In still other approaches, decoherence is treated as
sufficient explanation, despite its reliance on environmental assumptions that are themselves not
structurally grounded.
In each case, the mathematical machinery remains intact. What remains unresolved is the
underlying question: what constrains the universe such that only certain realizations persist, while
others remain admissible in principle but unrealized in practice? The interpretive gap, as treated
here, does not concern whether quantum mechanics is correct, complete, or empirically valid. Nor
does it concern the introduction of new dynamics, hidden variables, preferred observers, or spec-
ulative ontologies. It concerns the absence of a constraint-based explanation connecting quantum
permissiveness to classical persistence.
More precisely, the gap can be stated as follows. Quantum mechanics describes a space of
admissible possibilities. Classical physics describes a space of persistent identities. The formalism
alone does not specify why the former narrows into the latter, nor what structural pressures govern
that narrowing. Collapse, decoherence, and measurement are described operationally, but their
status as consequences of deeper limiting conditions is left implicit or undefined.
As a result, the transition from quantum coherence to classical behavior is often treated as
either primitive, observer-dependent, or interpretation-relative. Yet the persistence of classical

structure across scale, interaction, and disturbance suggests that this transition is neither arbitrary
nor incidental. Stable identities do not merely appear; they endure.
This work treats the interpretive gap as an explanatory deficiency rather than a metaphysical
mystery. It assumes that the transition from quantum admissibility to classical persistence reflects
the action of limiting conditions that govern stability itself. The task is therefore not to reinterpret
quantum mechanics, revise its mathematics, or privilege particular observers, but to identify the
structural constraints that must exist for the observed transition to occur.
Quantum Coherence Theory is introduced precisely to address this gap. It does so by shifting
explanatory focus away from outcomes, observers, or postulated selection rules, and toward the
constraints that determine which configurations remain admissible, stable, and persistent across
scale and interaction. The aim is not to replace quantum theory, but to supply an explanatory
layer that clarifies why its predictions resolve into the classical world that persists.
1.2 Limits of Probabilistic, Observer-Centric, and In-
strumentalist Accounts
Many existing interpretations of quantum mechanics address the interpretive gap identified in
the previous section by reframing the problem rather than resolving it. While these approaches
differ in emphasis and philosophical posture, they share a common structural feature: each treats
certain explanatory elements as primitive that, within a constraint-first framework, remain insuffi-
ciently grounded.
Probabilistic accounts typically elevate probability to foundational status. In such views, quan-
tum outcomes are taken to be irreducibly stochastic, with no deeper explanation offered for why
particular outcomes stabilize while others do not. Probability functions as an endpoint of explana-
tion rather than a derived consequence. While this move preserves predictive adequacy, it leaves
unanswered why probabilistic descriptions govern physical persistence at all, or why probabilistic
behavior gives way to effectively deterministic classical behavior as systems scale, interact, and
endure.
Put differently, probability describes distributions over admissible states, but it does not explain
why admissibility narrows in practice. Treating probability as fundamental accounts for uncertainty,
but not for durability.
Observer-centric accounts attempt to resolve the transition by assigning a privileged role to
measurement, observation, or informational update. In these frameworks, definiteness arises not
from structural properties of physical systems themselves, but from interactions involving observers
or observer-like processes. While such accounts can be internally coherent, they implicitly relocate
the explanatory burden. The question of why classical outcomes emerge is replaced by the question
of why observers experience definiteness.
This displacement leaves the underlying physical transition unaddressed. Stable macroscopic
structure persists regardless of whether it is observed, recorded, or interpreted. Any account that
makes observation explanatorily central must still explain why unobserved systems obey the same
stability constraints as observed ones. Without such an explanation, observer-centric approaches
risk confusing epistemic update with physical persistence.
Instrumentalist approaches, by contrast, explicitly decline to offer an explanatory account.
They treat the quantum formalism as a predictive tool whose role is exhausted by its success in
forecasting experimental outcomes. From this perspective, questions about collapse, emergence, or
persistence are regarded as either meaningless or unnecessary. While this stance avoids speculative

===== PAGE 8 =====
commitments, it also forgoes any attempt to explain why the formalism works as it does, or why
its predictions align so consistently with a stable macroscopic world. Instrumentalism succeeds by
narrowing the definition of explanation to prediction alone. In doing so, it renders the interpretive
gap invisible rather than resolved.
From the perspective adopted in this work, these approaches are not rejected as incorrect. Each
captures genuine aspects of quantum behavior and remains compatible with the formalism. Their
limitation is instead explanatory completeness. Probabilistic accounts stop at chance. Observer-
centric accounts stop at experience. Instrumentalist accounts stop at utility. None provide a
structural account of the conditions under which physical possibilities cease to be jointly admissible
and instead resolve into persistent configurations.
What these frameworks share is an under-articulation of constraint. In probabilistic and
observer-centric accounts, constraints are present but implicit, folded into postulates or measure-
ment rules. In instrumentalist accounts, constraints are bracketed entirely. As a result, the transi-
tion from quantum permissiveness to classical stability is managed operationally but not explained
structurally.
This absence is not accidental. Constraint-based explanations occupy a conceptual layer that
sits between formal dynamics and observed outcomes. Without explicitly naming this layer, inter-
pretive frameworks are forced either to treat narrowing as primitive or to relocate it to observers,
probability, or pragmatics.
Quantum Coherence Theory does not seek to replace these interpretations or to adjudicate
between them. Instead, it treats their shared limitation as diagnostic. The persistent absence
of an explicit account of constraint suggests not that such constraints are unnecessary, but that
they have remained untheorized. The task, therefore, is not to dispute probability, observation, or
prediction, but to articulate the limiting conditions under which these features emerge as secondary
consequences of deeper structural restrictions governing admissibility and persistence.
1.3 Motivation for a Constraint-First Explanatory
Framework
The limitations outlined in the preceding sections motivate a shift in explanatory posture rather
than the introduction of new physical postulates. Quantum mechanics remains empirically unri-
valed in its predictive accuracy, and nothing in this work challenges its formal validity. What
remains unresolved is not whether the theory works, but why its permissive descriptions reliably
give rise to stable, classical outcomes without invoking observer privilege, irreducible chance, or
explanatory abstention.
At the center of this unresolved question lies a simple but underexamined fact: persistence
is not automatic. Physical descriptions permit many possible configurations, but only a narrow
subset of those configurations remain identifiable over time. For a system to persist as the same
system, alternatives must be excluded in a structured and sustained way. Stability, in this sense,
is not merely the continuation of state evolution, but the result of limiting conditions that restrict
which possibilities remain admissible under interaction.
A constraint-first framework begins from this observation. Rather than asking why a particular
outcome is selected from among many, it asks under what conditions multiple possibilities remain
jointly admissible, and under what conditions they cease to be so. The focus shifts from outcomes
to admissibility, and from observers to structure. Collapse, decoherence, and classical definiteness

===== PAGE 9 =====
are treated not as special events requiring interpretive intervention, but as consequences of how
constraints accumulate, interact, and enforce exclusion across scale.
This reframing also alters the status of probability within explanation. Probability is not
treated as a primitive governing principle, but as a descriptive artifact that arises when multiple
admissible configurations coexist under insufficient constraint. Where admissibility remains broad,
probabilistic descriptions are appropriate. Where constraints aggregate and admissibility narrows,
probabilistic freedom diminishes and persistence emerges.
Probability, in this view, tracks permissiveness rather than causation. Similarly, measurement
is not granted ontological privilege. Measurement is treated as one class of interaction among
many that can introduce additional constraints by coupling a system to an environment with its
own persistence requirements. Observation does not create definiteness; it participates in con-
straint aggregation. Predictive success, while indispensable, is therefore not taken as an endpoint
of explanation, but as a surface manifestation of deeper regularities governing admissibility and
stability.
The motivation for this approach is not metaphysical. It arises from a practical explanatory
need. Without an explicit account of how constraints operate across scale and interaction, the
transition from quantum coherence to classical determinism remains partitioned across interpretive
boundaries. Decoherence explains suppression of interference, collapse postulates explain outcome
definiteness, and classical physics explains stability, but no single structural account explains why
these regimes connect as reliably as they do.
By contrast, a constraint-first framework aims to describe the quantum–classical transition as a
continuous process governed by the aggregation and interaction of limiting conditions, rather than
as a categorical jump requiring special intervention. It treats stability as an achievement rather
than a default, and collapse as an enforced reduction of admissibility rather than an unexplained
selection.
Importantly, this framework does not presume that constraints are externally imposed, intelli-
gently selected, or teleologically ordered. Constraints are treated descriptively, as conditions that
arise from interaction, coupling, boundary formation, symmetry restriction, and mutual compati-
bility requirements among physical systems. Their role is not to direct outcomes, but to delimit
persistence.
The goal of Quantum Coherence Theory is therefore not to reinterpret quantum mechanics from
first principles, nor to compete with existing interpretations on their own terms. Its aim is narrower
and more foundational: to articulate how constraint structure governs admissibility, stability, and
collapse across regimes, providing a unifying explanatory layer beneath probabilistic description,
observer-centric narratives, and instrumental success.
1.4 Methodological Closure and Scope Discipline
Before proceeding to the foundational assumptions of Quantum Coherence Theory, it is nec-
essary to state explicitly the methodological limits within which the framework operates. These
limits are not provisional hedges or rhetorical safeguards. They are structural constraints imposed
to preserve explanatory clarity, maintain internal consistency, and prevent category drift as the
theory develops.
Quantum Coherence Theory adopts methodological closure in the following sense: the observ-
able universe is treated as an informationally closed domain for purposes of explanation. This
assumption is methodological rather than ontological. It does not assert that the observable do-
main is complete, unique, or exclusive. Nor does it deny the possibility of structures, domains, or
9

===== PAGE 10 =====
realities beyond empirical access. It reflects a disciplined refusal to rely on unobservable external
inputs to explain persistence, stability, or collapse within the observable regime.
Methodological closure establishes an evidentiary boundary. All explanations advanced within
QCT must be grounded in interactions, constraints, and structural relations that arise within the
domain of empirical accessibility. No explanatory weight is assigned to hidden external agencies,
privileged reference frames, transcendent selectors, or metaphysical interventions. Where explana-
tion cannot proceed without such appeals, the limit is acknowledged rather than bypassed.
This posture is pragmatic rather than doctrinal. It does not claim that external influences do
not exist; it claims only that they cannot be invoked as explanatory resources without dissolving
the distinction between explanation and speculation. QCT therefore treats unobservables as epis-
temically out of scope unless their effects can be traced through constraint structure within the
closed description. Scope discipline follows directly from this commitment. Quantum Coherence
Theory does not attempt to:
•derive fundamental constants
•specify underlying microdynamical mechanisms
•adjudicate among competing ontologies beyond what constraint structure requires
•or provide prescriptive rules for intervention, engineering, or control
Instead, QCT positions itself as an explanatory layer concerned with how admissibility, coher-
ence, and collapse behave under varying constraint conditions. It does not address why the universe
exists, what ultimately grounds physical law, or how outcomes should be optimized or manipulated.
These questions are neither answered nor denied; they are simply not required to accomplish the
explanatory task at hand.
This discipline also governs the relationship between QCT and adjacent works. Foundations of

2 Orientating QCT
This section establishes the orientation language required to speak meaningfully about per-
sistence in QCT. It defines coherence as a structural condition rather than a metaphor, treats
constraint as primary, and frames identity and information as exclusionary processes. These as-
sumptions do not explain reality, but bound what kinds of explanations can remain viable under

2.1 The Meaning of Coherence in QCT
Quantum Coherence Theory employs the term coherence in a deliberately non-standard sense.
This choice is neither accidental nor rhetorical. It reflects the explanatory level at which QCT
operates and the specific problem it addresses: not the dynamics of particular physical variables,
but the conditions under which any physical description can support persistence, identity, and
stable structure under constraint.
In standard quantum mechanics, coherence refers to well-defined phase relations between com-
ponents of a quantum state, typically expressed through off-diagonal terms in a density matrix.
This usage is precise, domain-local, and indispensable within quantum formalism. Nothing in
Quantum Coherence Theory disputes or modifies that definition within its proper scope. QCT
does not attempt to reinterpret quantum-mechanical coherence, nor to replace its technical role in
wave mechanics, interference phenomena, or decoherence theory.
Instead, QCT introduces a structurally prior notion of coherence, inherited from Foundations
of Coherence Science (referenced in Related Works), that operates at a different explanatory level.
In this framework, coherence refers to the satisfaction of invariants under transformation: the
capacity of a system, description, or world-model to remain self-consistent, non-contradictory, and
persistently identifiable as constraints accumulate. Coherence, in this sense, is not a wave property,
a phase relation, or a superpositional feature. It is a structural condition for persistence.
This distinction in usage reflects a difference in explanatory task rather than a conceptual
conflict. Quantum mechanics presupposes a stable descriptive framework in which states, operators,
and dynamics are meaningfully defined. Quantum Coherence Theory asks a prior question: what
must be true of a description for such a framework to remain viable at all under interaction,
coupling, and constraint? The notion of coherence employed here is introduced to answer that
question.
Within QCT, coherence is therefore not synonymous with maximal superposition, maximal
symmetry, or unrestricted dynamical freedom. On the contrary, as explained in the framework, co-
herence increases as admissible possibilities are selectively restricted in ways that preserve identity
and stability. An example explored more thoroughly in the following sections is superposition. Su-
perposition, when viewed through this lens, represents a state of low structural commitment: many
possibilities are simultaneously admissible, but none are yet stabilized as records. As constraints ac-
cumulate—through interaction, decoherence, boundary conditions, or environmental coupling—the
space of admissible states narrows. Coherence, in the QCT sense, increases as contradiction is me-
tabolized rather than eliminated and as persistence becomes possible.
This usage is intentionally invariant-centered. A system is coherent to the extent that it satisfies
the invariants required for its continued existence across transformations. Loss of coherence, corre-
spondingly, is not primarily the loss of phase information but the failure of invariant satisfaction:
internal contradiction, uncontrolled proliferation of admissible states, or collapse of identity under

constraint. In this sense, coherence is not opposed to constraint; it is produced by the right kind
of constraint.
Readers familiar with quantum-mechanical terminology may experience an initial friction when
encountering this definition. That friction does not arise from ambiguity within the theory, but
from a shift in explanatory level. Quantum Coherence Theory explicitly declares semantic prece-
dence: coherence is defined structurally at the level of Coherence Science, and quantum coherence
appears as one domain-specific manifestation evaluated against that broader criterion. The theory
is internally consistent once this hierarchy is established.
This framing is essential for the arguments that follow. Claims about energy, vacuum structure,

confusion while preserving full compatibility with established physics.

a wave-mechanical property.
2.2 Constraint as a Prerequisite for Existence
Within this theory, constraint is treated as a prerequisite for existence in a minimal and strictly
descriptive sense. Without constraint, no distinction can persist long enough to be identified,
tracked, or meaningfully referred to. This claim is not metaphysical. It does not assert that
constraint creates existence, nor that existence is reducible to constraint. Rather, it specifies the
conditions under which existence becomes referable as persistent structure within a given explana-
tory boundary.
Constraint, as used here, refers to any condition that reduces, structures, or delimits the set
of states or transitions that remain accessible under the admissible dynamics of a chosen descrip-
tion. Such conditions may arise from boundary relations, conservation laws, interaction couplings,
symmetry restrictions, environmental entanglement, or coarse-graining choices.
They are not assumed to be forces, agents, goals, or causes by default. Constraint is a structural
condition on admissibility, not an intention, directive, or dynamical command. Operationally, a
constraint is present wherever the admissible evolution of a system leaves certain correlations,
distinctions, or patterns intact across interaction and time. In these cases, accessibility is limited in
a way that allows some structures to remain identifiable despite disturbance. Constraint functions
by restricting what can vary freely, thereby permitting invariance or metastability at the descriptive
level.
The claim advanced here is deliberately narrow. Constraint is necessary for persistence, identity,
and information to be defined. It is not sufficient to guarantee coherence, stability, objecthood, or
functional viability. Many constrained configurations fail to endure. They fragment, decohere, or
collapse under interaction. Constraint makes persistence possible, but it does not ensure it. It is
the entry condition for describability, not a guarantee of survival.

At any given descriptive boundary—specified by a choice of coarse-graining, admissible inter-
actions, tolerance, and timescale—the absence of effective constraint means that the description
provides no internal basis for preserving distinctions across time. The effective dynamics explore
the accessible set without leaving long-lived structures at that resolution. Distinctions may arise
momentarily, but they dissolve as interaction disperses correlations across the available state space.
From the standpoint of the reduced description, such a regime is indistinguishable from noise: not
because nothing occurs, but because nothing remains re-identifiable.
Existence in Quantum Coherence Theory is therefore defined operationally, in terms of persis-
tence. A structure exists within a given description only insofar as it remains re-identifiable under
the admissible transformations of that description, within an appropriate tolerance and over a rel-
evant timescale. This framing avoids two common errors: treating existence as an intrinsic essence
independent of conditions, and treating transient fluctuations as equivalent to stable structure.
Only what persists under effective constraint qualifies as existent within the theory’s descriptive
scope.
Importantly, constraint does not imply rigidity or determinism. A system may be highly con-
strained and brittle, or lightly constrained and flexible. What matters is not the presence of
constraint alone, but its configuration and its compatibility with persistence. Constraint that is
too weak fails to stabilize structure. Constraint that is not approximately preserved under the
induced dynamics or system–environment coupling suppresses variability or collapses the system
into triviality. Persistence occupies a constrained but non-degenerate regime in which structure is
both protected and responsive.
This perspective also clarifies why constraint cannot be equated with determinism. Constraints
delimit what is admissible, but they do not fix outcomes. Probabilistic behavior, indeterminacy,
and stochastic dynamics remain fully compatible with a constraint-first framework, provided the
constraints defining admissibility are respected. Constraint limits possibility; it does not exhaust
it.
Constraint in Quantum Coherence Theory is always relative to a descriptive boundary. What
appears weakly constrained at one scale may consolidate into robust invariants at another. A sys-
tem may exhibit few stabilizing distinctions at one resolution and many at another. This scale
dependence is not a defect or ambiguity. It is a structural feature that allows the same under-
lying system to display different persistence profiles across regimes without invoking categorical
ontological shifts.
By treating constraint as prerequisite rather than cause, Quantum Coherence Theory avoids
both reductionism and metaphysical inflation. Constraint does not explain why something ex-
ists rather than nothing. It explains why, given existence, stable structure is rare, fragile, and
conditional. It establishes the stage on which coherence, identity, and information can later be
meaningfully discussed.
In summary, constraint is introduced here not as an explanatory endpoint, but as the minimal
condition that makes explanation possible at all. Without effective constraint, there is no persis-
tence. Without persistence, there is no identity. Without identity, there is nothing for theory to
refer to.
2.3 Identity as Exclusion and Persistence
For this framework, identity is not treated as a primitive feature of reality, nor as an intrin-
sic essence attached to objects. Identity is not assumed at the outset of explanation. Instead,

it is defined relationally and conditionally, as a property that emerges when a system remains
distinguishable under constraint across time, interaction, and transformation.
At the most basic level, identity requires exclusion. A system is identifiable only insofar as
it occupies a restricted subset of admissible states within a larger space of possibility. To be one
configuration rather than another is necessarily to exclude alternative configurations that remain
admissible but unrealized. This exclusion is not metaphysical negation; it is structural differentia-
tion. Identity begins when “this” is not “that” within a defined descriptive frame.
However, exclusion alone is insufficient to establish identity. A momentary restriction that
collapses immediately under interaction does not constitute an identifiable system. In QCT, identity
requires persistence: the capacity of a configuration to remain distinguishable as constraints act
upon it. A configuration that appears briefly but cannot withstand interaction does not form an
identity in any meaningful sense. Identity is therefore not instantaneous. It is extended, conditional,
and evaluated over trajectories rather than snapshots.
Persistence is always relative to a constraint set. A system’s identity is defined by the transfor-
mations it can undergo without losing distinguishability. These transformations form an equivalence
class under the active constraints. Changes that remain within this class preserve identity; changes
that exceed it degrade identity. Importantly, identity loss is typically gradual rather than binary.
Systems do not usually “cease to exist” in a single step. They erode, fragment, or dissolve as
constraints overwhelm their capacity to maintain distinction.
This gradual degradation is a critical feature of QCT’s treatment of identity. Identity is not
a toggle. It is a tolerance band. As constraints intensify or change, a system may retain partial
identity, ambiguous identity, or scale-limited identity. Only when exclusion can no longer be main-
tained does identity fully dissolve. This allows QCT to describe transitions without invoking sharp
ontological breaks or arbitrary thresholds.
Identity is also inherently scale-dependent. A configuration that persists at one scale may
fail to remain distinguishable at another. Quantum states, classical objects, and macroscopic
structures differ not by categorical ontology, but by the degree to which their identities withstand
aggregated constraint across interactions. Classical stability reflects high tolerance to constraint,
not a fundamentally different mode of being. What appears “object-like” is simply what survives
constraint thickening without losing distinguishability.
Because identity arises from constraint structure, it is contextual without being observer-
dependent. The conditions that define persistence are imposed by interaction, coupling, and en-
vironmental embedding, not by observation or interpretation. Observers may describe identity,
classify it, or track its degradation, but they do not instantiate it. Identity does not depend on
measurement for its existence. It depends on whether distinguishability can be maintained under
the relevant constraints.
This distinction is essential for avoiding observer-centered interpretations of persistence. Iden-
tity does not emerge because something is noticed. It emerges because something remains. The
role of the observer is descriptive, not constitutive. QCT therefore treats identity as an emergent
structural property of constrained systems, not as a projection of epistemic access.
Within this framework, identity is neither absolute nor illusory. It is conditional, durable when
constraints permit, fragile when they do not, and always bounded by the structure of admissibility.
Identity is what remains distinguishable when alternatives are excluded and that exclusion can be
sustained across interaction.
In its most compact form, identity in Quantum Coherence Theory is defined as the persistence of
distinguishability under constraint. Where exclusion fails, identity cannot arise. Where persistence
fails, identity cannot endure. Everything else follows from that condition.

2.4 Information as Constrained Realization
In Quantum Coherence Theory, information refers to realized differentiation under constraint.
It does not denote semantic meaning, representation, reference, or observer-relative interpretation.
Information names a physical condition: that one admissible configuration has been realized rather
than others, producing distinguishability within a constrained description.
Information is not treated as a primitive of reality. It does not exist independently of constraint,
nor does it arise from unconstrained fluctuation alone. A system may exhibit transient patterning or
statistical variation, but without stabilizing conditions that preserve distinctions across interaction
and time, no realized differentiation can persist long enough to count as information. Where nothing
remains distinguishable, nothing informative exists.
This framing separates the use of information in Quantum Coherence Theory from both se-
mantic and purely probabilistic accounts. Semantic theories require interpretation or reference; no
such requirement appears here. Probabilistic theories describe likelihoods over admissible states,
but probability alone does not constitute information in this framework. Information appears
only when constraints restrict admissibility in a way that fixes realizations relative to excluded
alternatives. In this sense, information is an effect of constraint, not its cause.
Information is inseparable from exclusion. To realize one configuration is necessarily to exclude
others that remain admissible but unrealized within the same descriptive frame. This exclusion is
not absolute or global. It is always relative to a chosen partition, scale, and constraint set. Infor-
mation therefore exists only within a bounded description, not as a universal quantity independent
of modeling choices.
A distinction counts as information in Quantum Coherence Theory when it is realized, remains
distinguishable under a specified coarse-graining, and persists across a relevant class of interactions
and timescales due to stabilizing constraints. Transient differentiation that cannot be re-identified
under interaction does not qualify.
Not all constraints that stabilize information are reducible to internal statistical interactions
within a system. For realized differentiation to persist across disturbance and scale change, con-
straint relations must remain compatible under transformation. Such constraints may take the form
of boundary conditions, conservation relations, symmetry restrictions, inter-scale couplings, or his-
torical contingencies. They do not require external agents, purposes, or designs. They need only
be stabilizing conditions not implied by treating the same descriptive boundary as unconstrained
noise. This requirement distinguishes persistent information from momentary patterning.
Information in Quantum Coherence Theory is therefore relational. A realization becomes in-
formative only insofar as it remains mutually compatible with other constrained realizations across
a system. Information does not reside in isolated states but in the network of constraints that
preserves differentiation across interaction. When this compatibility erodes, information degrades
gradually rather than disappearing abruptly, reflecting loss of constraint alignment rather than
annihilation of states.
This account distinguishes information from entropy while allowing the two to remain related.
Entropy may characterize dispersion or uncertainty relative to a chosen coarse-graining. Informa-
tion refers instead to the existence of stabilized differentiation itself. Entropy may increase while
information persists, provided constraints continue to preserve distinguishability at the relevant
scale. Conversely, entropy reduction does not guarantee informational persistence if constraints fail
to maintain identity under interaction.
Information is also scale-dependent. A configuration may carry information at one scale while
failing to remain informative at another. Quantum states, classical objects, and macroscopic struc-
tures differ not by categorical informational status, but by the robustness with which their differ-

to interaction and disturbance, not a distinct informational ontology.
Quantum Coherence Theory makes no claim that information must arise, increase, or optimize

asserts only that wherever persistent differentiation is observed, constraint conditions sufficient to

3 Possibility, Constraint, and Ontology
This section establishes the minimal ontological language used by QCT. It reframes energy,
matter, and scale as expressions of constrained possibility rather than primitive entities. By treating
scale as a proxy for constraint density, this section provides a neutral descriptive bridge between
quantum admissibility and classical persistence without invoking hidden substances, teleology, or
observer privilege.
3.1 The Zero-Sum Field
Any discussion of structure presupposes a contrast against which structure can be recognized.
Before distinctions such as energy, matter, or identity can be meaningfully introduced, there must
exist a reference condition in which none of these distinctions yet operate decisively. The zero-
sum field is introduced here as that reference condition. The zero-sum field is not proposed as a
physical field, nor as an ontological claim about the ultimate nature or origin of reality. It is a
methodological construct used to represent a state of maximal admissibility: a condition in which
no configuration is privileged, no outcome is stabilized, and no asymmetry yet enforces persistence.
Its role is to provide a neutral baseline against which the effects of constraint can later be described.
“Zero-sum” in this context does not refer to competition, cancellation, or conservation in the
economic or game-theoretic sense. It denotes the absence of net structural preference. All configu-
rations are equally admissible; no pathway carries authority over another; no distinction yet carries
the force required to endure. The field is “flat” with respect to structure, not empty of content.
This construct is deliberately minimal. It does not assume dynamics, causality, or time-directed
evolution. Nor does it assume randomness as a productive principle. Possibilities may be enumer-
ated, but none are yet selected. Variation may be conceivable, but no variation is retained. In this
sense, the zero-sum field is not chaotic; it is non-committal.
The usefulness of this baseline lies in what it does not contain. By excluding implicit structure
at the outset, it allows later sections to identify precisely where and how structure is introduced.
Any appearance of persistence, localization, or durability must arise from additions to this baseline
rather than being silently inherited from it.
It is important to emphasize that the zero-sum field is not intended to describe an actual
historical or physical state of the universe. It is a limiting construct, analogous to idealized reference
conditions used throughout physics and mathematics. Its purpose is comparative, not descriptive:
to make the contribution of constraint legible by contrast.
In the sections that follow, departures from this baseline will be introduced gradually. Energy
will first appear as a means of restricting admissible transitions without yet enforcing localization.
Subsequent refinements will show how additional layers of constraint further narrow what can
persist. The zero-sum field thus marks the point at which no such narrowing has yet occurred.
3.2 Constraint as the Structuring of Potential
In Quantum Coherence Theory, “constraint” is not a physical entity, force, or governing prin-
ciple. It is a descriptive classifier: a way of characterizing how the space of admissible states is
structured so that some configurations remain meaningful, stable, and predictively usable under

interaction, while others do not. “Potential” denotes the full set of configurations that are kine-
matically and dynamically allowed within a given description. “Constraint” characterizes how that
space is shaped—by interaction, coupling, and modeling choice—into subsets that either sustain or
fail to sustain persistence. Constraint does not produce outcomes. It defines the conditions under
which a description continues to function without breakdown.
Admissibility in QCT is always evaluated relative to a descriptive partition. A partition specifies
which variables count as the effective state of a system (macroscopic observables, coarse-grained
degrees of freedom, pointer variables, or other operationally stable quantities). The partition
induces an equivalence relation on microstates: different microstates are treated as equivalent
when they realize the same effective state at the description’s resolution.
Persistence refers to predictive stability of those effective variables. A description persists when
microstates that are equivalent under the partition continue to evolve into states that remain
equivalent—within tolerance—over the timescale of interest. A microstate is admissible (relative
to a partition) when its evolution preserves that predictive stability.
Constraint therefore functions as a viability condition for descriptions, not as a causal mecha-
nism acting on physical states. Saying “constraint structures potential” does not mean constraint
causes events. No new dynamics are introduced; underlying evolution remains standard physics
(unitary dynamics on the combined system–environment, plus effective open-system dynamics de-
rived from that unitary description). Constraint names the fact that, under interaction and a
chosen partition, some configurations fail to remain jointly representable as stable descriptors.
These configurations are not eliminated by force or fundamental selection. They become in-
admissible because they do not support persistence of the effective variables defined by the de-
scription. Multiple physical structures can implement the same constraint pattern. Conservation
laws, symmetries, interaction Hamiltonians, boundary conditions, and environmental monitoring
all instantiate constraints within their respective formalisms. QCT abstracts across these imple-
mentations to describe their shared effect: restriction of the admissible state space relative to a
description.
Constraint accumulates through interaction. When systems couple, admissibility is no longer
evaluated independently for each subsystem but jointly under the coupled dynamics. States admissi-
ble for isolated systems may become incompatible with persistence once correlations, entanglement,
or environmental coupling are introduced. This narrowing does not require collapse, selection, or
privileged observation; it follows from compatibility requirements under continued interaction. As
interaction compounds, the admissible set may shrink gradually rather than discontinuously.
Constraint density refers to how strongly interaction reduces the measure of microstates that
remain admissible under a chosen partition, tolerance, and timescale. The measure is defined over
the microstates relevant to the description (e.g., induced by a preparation ensemble, a typicality as-
sumption, or another sampling over microstates consistent with the description). At low constraint
density, multiple alternatives may remain jointly admissible, allowing interference, superposition,
and indeterminacy in the effective description. At higher constraint density, compatibility require-
ments render interference terms inaccessible to the effective algebra defined by the partition. Those
terms become non-contributory to stable predictions, reducing the number of equivalence classes
that remain jointly admissible.
Constraint is always partition-relative, but partitions are not arbitrary. Legitimate partitions
are justified by operational accessibility, dynamical robustness, and predictive stability. Variables
are selected because they remain well-defined under interaction and are redundantly supported by
environmental correlations. Constraint density is therefore relational rather than absolute, but it is
not unconstrained by modeling choice. Different partitions can assign different constraint densities
to the same underlying system.

This is not a defect of the framework. It reflects the fact that persistence and admissibility are
properties of descriptions, not of bare microstates in isolation. In standard open-system models,
increasing constraint density often correlates with stronger environment-induced monitoring (faster
decoherence), greater redundancy of environmental records, and increased closure of the effective
observable algebra. QCT does not privilege any single formalism as uniquely definitive of these
effects; it treats them as different implementations of the same descriptive role.
Constraint does not imply rigidity. A highly constrained system may be robust or brittle de-
pending on how constraints are configured. Some constraint structures preserve compatibility under
disturbance and stabilize persistence. Others overdetermine behavior and precipitate breakdown of
the effective description. QCT therefore does not equate “more constraint” with “more stability.”
Stability arises when constraints are mutually compatible and proportionally corrective rather than
conflicting or excessive.
This framework clarifies decoherence and collapse without new postulates. Decoherence corre-
sponds to progressive restriction of admissible phase relations under environmental coupling: inter-
ference terms become dynamically and operationally inaccessible relative to the chosen partition,
and thus irrelevant to stable prediction within that description. Collapse, in QCT, refers only to ef-
fective collapse: the point at which alternatives are no longer jointly admissible within the effective
description because aggregated constraints render them incompatible with stable representation.
No fundamental non-unitary event is implied; underlying dynamics remain unitary.
Exclusivity emerges as exclusivity of joint representability and record-consistent prediction un-
der the partition, not as selection among ontic outcomes. Classical behavior emerges when con-
straint density becomes sufficiently high that only a narrow equivalence class of configurations
remains admissible under continued interaction. Determinism, in this sense, is emergent rather
than fundamental. Quantum dynamics do not cease to apply; their expressive capacity collapses
at the descriptive level because the admissible set has narrowed to configurations that behave
classically.
In QCT, constraint is the means by which potential becomes structured into persistence. It
neither creates reality nor selects outcomes. It delineates the conditions under which realizations
endure, interactions remain compatible, and identity is preserved across scale. It is not an onto-
logical addition to physics, but a unifying descriptive lens for how permissive quantum possibilities
give rise to persistent structure.
3.3 Energy as Less Constrained not Unconstrained
Energy is traditionally treated in physics as a conserved quantity, formally defined within math-
ematical frameworks such as Hamiltonian and Lagrangian mechanics and operationalized through
measurable exchanges between systems. Nothing in Quantum Coherence Theory disputes this
role. The conservation of energy, its calculational utility, and its empirical indispensability remain
fully intact. The distinction introduced here is not a rejection of energy’s formal function, but a
clarification of its explanatory status within a constraint-first account of persistence and structure.
Within Quantum Coherence Theory, energy is not treated as an ontic substance or indepen-
dently existing entity. Instead, it is understood as a representation of admissible potential for
change: a parameter that tracks which state transitions are permitted relative to a specified dynam-
ical description. Energy encodes which transitions are allowed given a Hamiltonian, its symmetries,
and its boundary conditions; it persists relationally, through the constraint structure that references
it, rather than as an independently localized form. It therefore does not itself constitute structure,

identity, or persistence in the sense required for record-bearing, locally referencable configurations.
This reframing preserves all standard physical uses of energy while avoiding unnecessary ontological
commitments.
This clarification matters because foundational confusion often arises when energy is implicitly
asked to perform incompatible roles at once: as a conserved scalar, a causal agent, and a material
substrate. In established physical practice, energy fulfills none of these roles in isolation. While
energy constrains which transitions are dynamically accessible, it does not by itself determine which
configurations persist, decohere, or stabilize as records. Those outcomes depend on constraint struc-
ture—interaction terms, coupling topology, spectra, symmetries, and boundary conditions—not on
the magnitude of energy alone.
Within a constraint-first framework, potential refers to the space of admissible configurations
available to a system relative to a chosen descriptive boundary. Energy is one powerful way of
parameterizing that space, particularly in dynamical models where evolution is represented as
motion through state space. However, potential is not exhausted by energetic description. Two
systems may possess identical total energy and yet differ radically in the number, accessibility, and
stability of the configurations they can sustain. What distinguishes such systems is not energy
content, but constraint structure.
This perspective also clarifies the meaning of energy conservation. In closed descriptions exhibit-
ing time-translation symmetry, a conserved energy functional serves as an invariant that restricts
admissible trajectories through state space. Conservation does not imply that a “thing” called
energy is preserved as a substance, nor that the overall space of possibilities is conserved in any
ontological sense. Rather, conservation reflects the invariance of a scalar functional under a chosen
description. Redistribution of energy corresponds to a redistribution of admissible transitions across
degrees of freedom, not to the creation of new ontological content. In explicitly time-dependent
drivings, non-stationary spacetimes, or certain general-relativistic settings, a globally conserved
energy may be non-unique or undefined; the present argument concerns cases where a conserved
energy functional is well-posed within the chosen model.
Energy should therefore not be described as unconstrained. It is constrained by global symme-
tries, conservation laws, and boundary conditions. What distinguishes energy from ontic structures
is not the absence of constraint, but the kind of constraint it bears. Energy is globally constrained
yet non-authoritative: it does not supply identity conditions, persistence criteria, or record author-
ity. Where stability, decoherence, or classicality arise, they do so through the full Hamiltonian
structure—its couplings, spectra, locality properties, and environmental interactions—not through
the scalar value of energy alone. In standard formalisms the Hamiltonian generates dynamics; the
claim here is that the scalar quantity “energy” is not ontologically generative. Explanatory work
is done by the Hamiltonian’s structural content, with energy serving as one invariant parameter
within that structure.
For this reason, energy is not itself a persisting form or configuration. Where energy appears
localized or embodied, what persists are constrained field or matter degrees of freedom whose
states admit energetic description relative to a chosen Hamiltonian. Energy is an attribute of
such states, not the bearer of form. Form requires exclusion and persistence: to have form is to
rule out alternatives and remain distinguishable under constraint. Energy, by contrast, indexes
configurability without enforcing exclusion on its own.
This explains why energy alone cannot ground ontology. An unconstrained or weakly con-
strained distribution of potential would admit many configurations without stabilizing any. Onto-
logical structure arises only when constraints channel potential into stable patterns that exclude
alternatives. Energy participates in this process descriptively, but it does not drive it. Greater
energy does not imply greater structure; absent appropriate constraints, it implies only a larger

space of admissible reconfiguration.
The representation-dependence of energy further reinforces this stance. Energy values depend
on reference frames, coordinate choices, coarse-graining, and modeling assumptions. Changes in
description can alter energetic representations without necessarily altering the underlying dynamics.
This confirms that energy functions as a coordinate for describing potential within a model, not as
the ground from which persistence arises.
Importantly, this reframing does not privilege any particular physical formalism. Whether
energy is expressed through classical fields, quantum operators, or thermodynamic variables, its role
remains consistent: to encode how constraints shape admissible change within a given description.
Quantum Coherence Theory remains agnostic about which formalism is ultimately fundamental,
focusing instead on the invariant feature they share—the tracking of potential as it is redistributed

By treating energy as globally conserved but non-authoritative potential, Quantum Coherence

persistence. Energy does not cause coherence, collapse, or classicality as an independent ontic
driver. It indexes how constraint pathways are implemented within existing formalisms, with
energy scales entering as parameters of interaction structure rather than as autonomous agencies.
Stabilization and collapse occur not because energy “acts,” but because admissible possibilities are
progressively restricted as systems interact and aggregate constraints.
This framing also provides a natural interpretation of mass–energy relations in bound systems.
In standard physics, the mass of a composite system is often less than the sum of the masses
of its constituents, with the difference accounted for as binding energy. Within a constraint-first
framework, this reflects a reduction in constraint redundancy. When systems bind, degrees of
freedom that previously required independent stabilization become jointly governed under shared
constraints. The resulting mass defect does not represent a “cost” paid to bind the system, but
the release of energetic potential rendered unnecessary by the consolidation of constraint structure.
Energy conservation remains intact; what changes is the density and redundancy of constraint
required to sustain distinct configurations.
This section therefore establishes a disciplined modeling posture. Energy is retained fully within
its empirical and mathematical roles, but it is not granted explanatory primacy. The explanation
of why particular structures persist, decohere, or stabilize must be sought in how potential is con-
strained, not in the mere presence or magnitude of energy. This shift is methodological rather than
metaphysical, conservative rather than speculative, and fully consistent with established physics.
3.4 Vacuum Energy as a Boundary Case
Vacuum energy provides a uniquely clear boundary case for any account that treats energetic
presence as inherently sufficient to generate persistent structure. In modern physics, vacuum states
are empirically real, mathematically indispensable, and in some contexts gravitationally consequen-
tial. Yet under fixed conditions they do not, by themselves, give rise to localized, record-bearing
forms. This makes vacuum energy an ideal test of the central claim of Quantum Coherence Theory:
energy can be real, conserved, and globally influential while still failing to determine persistence,
identity, or form. What performs the explanatory work is not energy alone, but the architecture of
constraints that restrict and stabilize admissible possibilities.
In quantum field theory, the vacuum is not “nothing,” but the ground state of interacting fields
relative to a specified Hamiltonian and boundary conditions. As a state rather than a substance, it

can possess rich internal structure: nontrivial correlations, entanglement across regions, and well-
defined symmetries. Observable effects associated with vacuum structure include forces that arise
when boundary conditions are altered and vacuum-like contributions to the stress–energy tensor
that, in general relativity, influence cosmic expansion. These facts establish that vacuum energy is
physically consequential rather than a mere accounting convention.
At the same time, vacuum energy is not a locally actionable reservoir. In nongravitational
quantum field theory, the absolute value of the vacuum energy can be shifted without changing the
dynamics; what is physically meaningful are correlations and differences between configurations,
especially when boundaries or interactions change. Even where vacuum-like energy has observable
gravitational effects, its influence is uniform rather than selective. The presence of energy does
not by itself specify where structure should appear, which configurations should persist, or how
identities should stabilize.
This helps clarify a frequent misunderstanding. Vacuum fluctuations are real, but they do
not consist of transient packets of freely extractable energy. They reflect nonzero variances and
correlations characteristic of a stationary state. Under fixed constraints, such a state does not
yield sustained work or stable structure. To obtain localized form, one must introduce additional
conditions that alter the constraint environment: boundaries, gradients, symmetry breaking, non-
equilibrium driving, or time-dependent backgrounds. Energy becomes actionable only when the
constraints governing it change.
A homogeneous, translation-invariant vacuum also makes clear why vacuum structure does not
yield persistent localized form. Such a state has no preferred location, orientation, or internal
mechanism for selecting one configuration over another. Time evolution under the same govern-
ing conditions preserves this homogeneity. While the vacuum contains structure in the sense of
correlations, this is not the same as stable, classically legible form. Persistent identities, records,
and macroscopic structures require mechanisms that both select and stabilize particular configu-
rations—mechanisms absent in a stationary vacuum. Gravity sharpens rather than weakens this
lesson. Vacuum-like energy components in general relativity can influence global dynamics, such
as cosmic expansion, through a uniform energy density with negative pressure. Yet gravitational
influence does not imply local structure formation. A Lorentz-invariant vacuum component re-
mains homogeneous and does not, by itself, clump into atoms, boundaries, or records. Structure
arises only when additional dynamical processes—instabilities, dissipation, clustering matter, or
symmetry breaking—restrict admissible outcomes and stabilize particular forms.
Apparent counterexamples reinforce this point rather than undermine it. Particle production
associated with horizons, time-dependent backgrounds, or rapidly changing boundaries occurs only
because the constraint environment has changed. Inflationary cosmology relies on accelerated
expansion and horizon dynamics; particle production near black holes depends on horizon structure
and observer-dependent conditions; boundary-driven effects require external driving. In every case,
vacuum energy becomes form-relevant only because new constraints are introduced that select and
stabilize specific outcomes.
Even situations involving degenerate or metastable vacua do not escape this logic. Where multi-
ple minima exist, the appearance of domains, defects, or ordered phases depends on how constraints
partition the space of possibilities and how dynamics selects among them. Cooling histories, bound-
ary conditions, interactions, and environmental coupling determine which configurations persist.
Energy alone does not explain the result; constraint architecture does.
Vacuum energy therefore illustrates a fundamental limitation of energetic explanation. Ener-
getic accounting can be complete and even globally decisive while still leaving the emergence of
form unexplained. Energy presence underdetermines which degrees of freedom become localized,
symmetry-broken, and stabilized as persistent structures. The decisive factor is how constraints re-

Within Quantum Coherence Theory, vacuum energy thus functions as a boundary case rather
than a foundational driver. It demonstrates that energy can be real, constrained, and consequential
without being form-generating. Persistence arises not from energetic abundance, but from the
organization of constraints that select, localize, and stabilize possibility.
3.5 Matter as Localized Identity Under Constraint
In Quantum Coherence Theory, matter is not treated as a primitive substance. It is under-
stood as a stable, localized configuration of constrained potential—a pattern that constitutes a
re-identifiable identity under interaction. What distinguishes matter from a transient fluctuation
is not composition, nor longevity, but the fact that an identity structure exists at all. Persistence
here does not mean indefinite duration; it means that identity is well-defined in the presence of
interaction.
Re-identifiability is understood structurally rather than procedurally. A configuration consti-
tutes an identity if its defining equivalence relation remains well-defined under interaction, rather
than collapsing into indistinguishability. Identity is thus treated as a robustness property of pat-
terns under interaction, not as an intrinsic label attached to a microstate. A configuration either
admits re-identification under interaction, or it does not.
Here, constraint refers generically to structural restrictions—dynamical, relational, or boundary-
based—that limit the admissible continuations of a configuration. Constraints are not limited
to external boundaries; they include dynamical invariants, symmetry restrictions, selection rules,
conservation-governed couplings, and interaction structures that restrict how a configuration may
evolve. Many patterns may arise within a system, but only those supported by sufficient con-
straint density—that is, by constraints that significantly restrict admissible evolutions relative to
an identity equivalence class—constitute coherent identities.
Equivalently, higher constraint density corresponds to a smaller set of admissible future trajec-
tories consistent with the configuration remaining the same kind of thing under interaction. Matter
is therefore not defined by stasis. It is defined by coherence through change, where transformation
is permitted but bounded in ways that preserve identity for as long as that identity exists.
Identity in this framework is not identity of microstate. A material configuration may undergo
continuous internal rearrangement while remaining the same entity in any operationally meaningful
sense. What is preserved, while the identity exists, is an equivalence class of admissible configu-
rations defined by stabilizing constraints. The equivalence relation is fixed by whatever features
are preserved by those constraints—such as conserved quantum numbers, robust response signa-
tures, topological invariants, or dynamically maintained boundary conditions. These constraints
determine which variations preserve identity and which transitions constitute transformation into
a different identity.
Crucially, identity is necessarily local and asymmetric. For a configuration to count as matter, it
must distinguish itself from its surroundings in a non-arbitrary way. This requires constraints that
close locally, meaning that the identity conditions primarily reference internal relations rather than
being wholly specified by external bookkeeping. Local closure does not require complete isolation:
identities may be stabilized either by internally closed constraints or by structured environmental
support, provided that re-identification remains well-defined under the relevant interactions.
Such closure yields a bounded region—spatial, relational, or functional—whose admissible in-
ternal configurations differ from those outside it. Without local asymmetry, there is no principled

basis for re-identification, and therefore no identity. Matter is thus defined by the presence of a
localized identity structure, not by separation from interaction.
This clarifies why isolation cannot serve as a criterion for material identity. Identity is not
supplied by suppressing interaction; it is defined relative to the interaction structures that couple
a configuration to its environment. A configuration whose identity is only meaningful in perfect
isolation does not constitute material identity in this operational sense. Interaction does not test
matter into existence; it is the context in which identity is defined at all.
Energy, as treated in Quantum Coherence Theory, is ontically constrained, but as a conserved
quantity it does not by itself supply an individuation rule for carving the world into bounded,
re-identifiable entities. Conserved quantities constrain transitions and enforce continuity across in-
teractions, but individuation requires additional constraints—such as binding structures, symmetry
breaking, gaps, or topological protections—that generate localized, stable sectors. Matter names
those regimes in which such constraints produce localized identity.
At macroscopic scales, this localized identity manifests phenomenologically as discreteness and
objecthood. High constraint density yields bounded, repeatable patterns that appear as distinct
objects. An atom constitutes a re-identifiable identity under generic coupling and exchange; an
interference pattern or thermal fluctuation typically does not, except under carefully maintained
boundary conditions that supply additional stabilizing constraints. Where constraint density weak-
ens, localization degrades: configurations spread, interfere, or lose distinguishability.
These regimes form a continuum, not a sharp dichotomy. Matter-likeness increases with the
breadth of interaction classes under which re-identification remains stable. Calling this a continuum
does not erase the usefulness of the term matter; it explains why objecthood can be sharp in some
regimes and progressively ill-defined in others as constraint organization changes.
Matter, then, is not assumed at the outset of Quantum Coherence Theory. It emerges wherever
constraint induces a localized, re-identifiable identity, regardless of how long that identity lasts.
The distinction is structural rather than metaphysical: what is ontologically present as an identity
versus what never constitutes one. Matter names the former—not as a fundamental substance, but
as a stabilized mode of being.
3.6 Locality and Gravity from Constraint Aggregation
In Quantum Coherence Theory, locality is not treated as a primitive geometric fact, nor as
a coordinate assignment imposed upon otherwise unconstrained entities. It is understood as a
structural consequence of identity persistence under dense mutual constraint. Once a configuration
constitutes a re-identifiable identity under interaction, the conditions required to preserve that
identity necessarily restrict how it may relate to other identities. Locality arises as the regime in
which admissible interactions are constrained by the requirement that identities remain mutually
recognizable within a shared constraint environment.
An identity, in this framework, is not a fixed object or microstate, but an equivalence class
of admissible configurations that remain re-identifiable across interaction. Re-identification fails
not when change occurs, but when the constraints defining that equivalence class are violated.
Constraint density refers to the number and strength of independent compatibility conditions that
must be simultaneously satisfied for re-identification to remain possible. As constraint density
increases, admissible continuations narrow, and identity persistence becomes increasingly sensitive
to relational compatibility.
Locality, therefore, is not merely spatial proximity. It is a relational compatibility regime defined
by how tightly the admissible evolutions of one identity depend upon the admissible evolutions of

compatibility with a bounded relational neighborhood of other identities, such that departures
from shared constraints rapidly undermine re-identifiability. Localization is reinforced collectively:
each localized identity participates in a network of mutual constraint that stabilizes the locality of
others.
This framing explains why locality strengthens rather than weakens as constraint density in-
creases. As constraints accumulate, transitions that would delocalize an identity do not merely
become improbable; they become structurally inadmissible. Such transitions violate the equiva-
lence relations required for re-identification and therefore cease to count as valid continuations of
that identity at all. Locality is thus enforced internally, as a consequence of identity preservation
under interaction, rather than imposed by an external background or limiting principle.
At sufficiently high aggregate constraint density, an additional stabilizing behavior emerges.
When large numbers of localized identities participate in a shared constraint environment, configu-
rations that preserve broad relational compatibility across the system become preferentially admis-
sible. This large-scale stabilization response is identified phenomenologically as gravity. Quantum
Coherence Theory does not introduce gravity as a fundamental interaction, nor does it seek to
replace its established mathematical descriptions. Instead, gravity is treated structurally as the
macroscopic expression of constraint aggregation: a global bias toward configurations that preserve
compatibility among many densely constrained identities.
From this perspective, gravity is not anomalously weak at small scales and dominant at large
ones. Its scaling behavior follows directly from its structural role. Where identities are weakly
constrained or sparsely coupled, the stabilizing effect associated with constraint aggregation is
negligible. Where identities are densely constrained and mutually reinforcing, that stabilizing
effect becomes increasingly significant. The macroscopic prominence of gravity is therefore not an
independent mystery, but an expected consequence of dense localization across large collections of
identities.
Locality and gravity, though often treated as distinct phenomena, arise from the same un-
derlying structural condition. Both reflect system behavior under high constraint density, where
admissible configurations are restricted not only internally but relationally. Locality governs the
near-scale restriction of admissible interactions by enforcing compatibility within bounded relational
neighborhoods. Gravity governs the large-scale restriction of admissible configurations by favoring
globally compatible arrangements of those neighborhoods. They are not separate mechanisms, but
different expressions of the same constraint regime operating across scale.
The result is a unified account of classical persistence that avoids interpretive discontinuities.
Localized matter remains localized because the constraints that define its identity are reinforced
through mutual participation in a dense constraint environment. Apparent delocalization decoheres
rapidly because it violates the equivalence relations required for re-identification under interaction.
Large-scale structure remains stable because configurations that preserve global constraint com-
patibility remain dynamically viable.
This account neither replaces nor competes with existing physical theories. It functions as
an organizing framework. It clarifies why classical behavior emerges continuously from quantum
regimes as constraint density increases, why localization is reinforced rather than destroyed by
interaction, and why gravity becomes dominant at scale without being fundamental in the same
sense as microscopic interaction rules.
Locality and gravity, then, are not additional assumptions within Quantum Coherence Theory.
They follow from the same structural requirement that defines matter itself: the persistence of re-
identifiable identity under interaction. Where constraint closes locally, identity stabilizes. Where
constraint aggregates globally, stabilization extends. The classical world is not imposed upon

3.7 Scale as a Proxy for Constraint Density
Just as material identity emerges when constraints close locally, classical behavior emerges when
constraints accumulate globally across interaction. For QCT, scale is consequently not treated as
a fundamental explanatory variable. Differences between quantum and classical behavior are not
attributed to size, complexity, or aggregation in themselves, but to how constraint density changes
as systems interact, compose, and persist. Scale functions only as a proxy: it correlates with the
number, strength, and interdependence of limiting conditions acting on admissible configurations
within a given description.
At small scales, systems are often weakly constrained in an operational sense. Degrees of
freedom may remain loosely coupled, environmental interaction may be limited, and admissible
configurations may remain broad relative to the chosen partition. Under these conditions, multiple
alternatives can remain jointly admissible without immediate exclusion. Superposition, interfer-
ence, and probabilistic manifestation arise not because small systems are ontologically special, but
because constraint density is low. Low constraint density does not imply disorder or randomness;
it implies permissiveness. Many configurations are allowed to coexist without forcing resolution.
As systems compose and endure, constraint density often increases. Composition introduces
additional interaction pathways, coupling becomes more pervasive, and compatibility requirements
multiply. Environmental entanglement, boundary formation, repeated interaction, and coarse-
grained stabilization impose overlapping restrictions on admissible configurations. This accumula-
tion does not require new principles, thresholds, or scale-dependent laws. It follows naturally from
the composition of constraint relations across interacting parts.
Classical behavior emerges when constraint density becomes sufficiently high that only a nar-
row subset of configurations remains mutually compatible under continued interaction. Under
such conditions, superposed alternatives are rapidly rendered non-contributory to stable predic-
tion, recoverability of interference collapses, and persistent identities emerge across interaction.

Determinism, in this view, is not fundamental but effective. It reflects the scarcity of admissible
alternatives relative to the prevailing constraint structure, not the absence of underlying possibility.
Scale, however, is an imperfect proxy. Large systems can remain weakly constrained with re-
spect to particular degrees of freedom when interaction is limited, coupling channels are restricted,
or constraint pathways are aligned to protect coherence rather than record alternatives. Empirical
cases such as superconductivity, Bose–Einstein condensation, and macroscopic quantum interfer-
ence demonstrate that coherence can persist at large scales when environmental monitoring is
suppressed and constraint structures are coherence-preserving rather than exclusivity-enforcing.
These cases do not contradict the framework; they confirm it. What matters is not size, but the
organization and function of constraints.
Conversely, small systems can exhibit high effective constraint density when strongly coupled
to their environment or subjected to restrictive boundary conditions. Rapid decoherence in micro-
scopic systems illustrates that quantum behavior is not guaranteed by scale alone. Scale correlates
with constraint density only insofar as it increases opportunities for interaction, redundancy, and
incompatibility.
This framing resolves a persistent ambiguity in discussions of the quantum–classical transition.
Rather than positing a sharp boundary or invoking scale-dependent regimes of law, QCT treats the
transition as continuous. As constraint density increases, admissibility narrows. As admissibility
narrows, persistence stabilizes. No categorical jump is required. Quantum and classical behaviors
occupy different regions of the same constraint landscape.
Scale-dependence in Quantum Coherence Theory is therefore descriptive rather than ontological.
It reflects how constraints aggregate under interaction and composition, not a division of reality into
separate domains. A single system may exhibit quantum behavior under one partition and classical
behavior under another, depending on how constraints are evaluated and at what resolution.
In summary, scale matters in Quantum Coherence Theory only because constraints tend to
accumulate—or, in some cases, align—through interaction, aggregation, and coupling. Where
constraint density is low, permissiveness persists. Where constraint density is high, stable identity
and predictability emerge. The quantum–classical transition is not imposed by size, but revealed
by the structure and function of constraints acting across scale.

4.1 Stability of Coherence at Low Constraint Density
Quantum coherence, as treated within Quantum Coherence Theory, refers to the sustained
mutual compatibility of multiple admissible configurations under a given constraint set. In this
section, coherence is not introduced as a special quantum oddity, nor as a paradoxical coexistence of
mutually exclusive realities. Instead, it names a structural condition: a regime in which the limiting
conditions acting on a system are insufficient to enforce exclusion among alternative configurations.
Coherence, in this sense, is not a mystery requiring resolution, but the expected behavior of systems
operating under permissive constraint environments.
At low effective constraint density, limiting conditions are sparse, weakly coupled, or non-
aggregating. Interactions do not yet impose incompatibility across the full space of admissible
configurations, and prior structure does not strongly restrict future continuation. As a result,
multiple possibilities can remain jointly admissible without generating contradiction or instability
within the description. Coherence persists not because the system is indecisive or indeterminate,
but because the structural pressure required to differentiate and exclude alternatives has not yet
accumulated.
This framing applies across the quantum regime and underlies the phenomena examined through-
out this section. What are often presented as distinct quantum effects—such as superposition,
interference, tunneling, and entanglement—are treated here as specific manifestations of coher-
ence under permissive constraint conditions rather than as independent primitives. Each reflects a
situation in which exclusion has not yet been structurally enforced.
Seen in this light, superposition does not assert that reality is “many things at once” in any
absolute sense. It reflects underdetermination rather than multiplicity. Superposition marks a
condition in which constraints have not yet forced differentiation among admissible configurations.
The system’s description remains open not because of ignorance or vagueness, but because exclusion
has not yet been required by the constraint structure.
Admissibility is therefore central. A coherent system is one for which multiple state descriptions
remain admissible under the same constraint set. These descriptions are never arbitrary and are
never unconstrained. They are bounded by conservation relations, boundary conditions, coupling
structure, and prior interaction history. Coherence is thus freedom within limits: permissiveness
structured by constraints that have not yet become mutually incompatible.
This explains why coherence is most robust in regimes characterized by isolation, limited inter-
action history, and weak or carefully structured environmental coupling. Small systems, short-lived
processes, and controlled experimental arrangements often exhibit coherence not because small-
ness confers special privilege, but because constraint accumulation remains minimal. Under such
conditions, coherence is not fragile or exceptional. It is the default behavior of permissive dynamics.

evolve, interfere, exchange energy, and undergo complex transformations while remaining coherent,
provided such evolution does not introduce constraints that partition admissible alternatives into

coherence is exclusion: the accumulation of constraints that render alternatives no longer jointly

In physical terms, constraint accumulation corresponds to the progressive creation of correla-

lies in their aggregation. Coherence degrades when the combined constraint structure no longer
permits multiple configurations to remain mutually compatible as continuations of the same de-

long as permissiveness remains structurally viable, and no longer. The transition from coherent to

Coherence, in this framework, is neither metaphysical excess nor epistemic artifact. It is the
predictable behavior of systems whose constraint environment has not yet enforced differentiation.
Wherever limiting conditions remain insufficient to impose exclusion, coherence will persist. Wher-

governs the quantum regime examined in this section and prepares the ground for the transition
mechanisms addressed in the following section.
4.2 Wave–Particle Duality as Constraint-Dependent De-
scription
Wave–particle duality is among the most persistent ideas in quantum mechanics, yet it is also
among the most misleading. The term survives primarily as a legacy label for the mismatch between
classical categories and quantum formalism, not because it reflects a genuine duality in the nature
of quantum systems. What it describes is not a system alternating between two physical modes,
but a difference in how a single quantum state is manifested under different interaction contexts.
Quantum mechanics does not contain separate “wave” and “particle” dynamics. It contains
a quantum state—represented by a state vector or density operator—that evolves unitarily when
sufficiently isolated, together with rules for assigning probabilities to outcomes when the system
undergoes a measurement interaction. The phenomena historically called “wave-like” arise from
29

===== PAGE 30 =====
coherent superposition with phase-sensitive effects, while those called “particle-like” arise from
discrete, localized detection events associated with particular measurement records. Both behaviors
emerge from the same formal structure.
The classical language of duality arose because early experiments violated the sharp distinc-
tion between waves and particles inherited from pre-quantum physics. Quantum systems produced
interference patterns characteristic of waves while also yielding discrete interaction events charac-
teristic of particles. In the absence of a framework capable of describing coherence under interaction
and constraint, both classical categories were retained, and their coexistence was labeled a duality.
While pedagogically convenient, this language obscures the underlying structure.
From a coherence-based perspective, the distinction reduces to whether the relevant alternatives
remain coherent or become distinguishable through interaction. When a quantum system evolves
without becoming correlated with degrees of freedom that record which alternative occurred, phase
relations are preserved and interference can be observed. When the system interacts with an
apparatus or environment in a way that correlates distinct alternatives with stable records, those
phase relations become inaccessible, interference is suppressed, and outcomes appear as discrete,
localized events. Nothing changes in the ontology of the system itself; what changes is the constraint
structure imposed by the interaction.
A common source of confusion is the assumption that spatial localization is a property carried
continuously through time. It is not. Between interactions, a quantum state need not be spatially
localized at all. What is preserved in closed-system evolution is unitarity—norm, inner products,
and the conservation laws associated with symmetries—not position. Apparent “loss of coherence”
in realistic situations reflects the redistribution of coherence into entanglement with uncontrolled
degrees of freedom, rather than a fundamental destruction of the quantum state.
The global consistency of quantum predictions already presupposes nonlocal correlation struc-
ture. Entangled states encode correlations that cannot be reduced to independent local descriptions,
even across spacelike separation. At the same time, the theory remains no-signaling: local outcome
statistics do not depend on distant measurement choices. The nonlocality involved is therefore not
one of control or influence, but of correlation encoded in the joint quantum state. Descriptions
invoking global admissibility or constraint should be understood in this sense—as reflecting the
structure of joint states and their consistency conditions, not superluminal dynamics.
In this light, wave–particle duality should not be treated as an explanatory principle. It re-
mains useful as historical shorthand, but it obscures the roles of unitary evolution, measurement
interaction, and decoherence, and it encourages the mistaken impression that quantum systems
alternate between incompatible forms of being. A more accurate description is therefore adopted
here: quantum states evolve coherently under unitary dynamics, and discrete, localized outcomes
arise when interactions generate stable records that render alternatives distinguishable. What dif-
fers between “wave-like” and “particle-like” phenomena is not ontology, but context—specifically,
whether coherence relevant to interference is preserved or constrained by interaction.

classically as an impenetrable barrier corresponds, in QCT terms, to a region of elevated local
constraint density: the space of admissible configurations is narrowed and strongly suppressed, but
not necessarily eliminated.
In standard quantum mechanics, tunneling arises because physical admissibility is determined
by globally consistent solutions to the governing dynamical equations, subject to continuity, nor-
malization, and boundary conditions. A system must satisfy these constraints across the entirety
of configuration space, including regions in which classical motion would be forbidden. In such

This framing avoids the familiar but misleading narrative that tunneling involves “borrowing en-

Energy conservation holds globally, and the system satisfies the same constraints everywhere. In

Constraint density in tunneling admits a direct operational interpretation. Increasing barrier

all practical purposes, and classical impenetrability emerges as a limiting behavior rather than as
a new postulate.

span it. The second arises from environmental coupling.

weight, the other suppresses coherence.
Tunneling does not presuppose observation, measurement, or collapse. It occurs entirely within

explanatory primitives.
Interpreted as constraint-compatible persistence, tunneling ceases to appear anomalous. It is a

than at locally classical slices. The phenomenon demonstrates how global compatibility governs
persistence in low-constraint regimes and how classical exclusion emerges smoothly as constraints
accumulate, providing a continuous bridge between quantum coherence and classical stability within
a single explanatory framework.
4.5 Entanglement as Shared Constraint Structure
In QCT, entanglement is not treated as a mysterious linkage between distant objects, nor as
a signal-carrying mechanism. It is understood structurally, as the emergence and persistence of
shared constraint relations across multiple subsystems such that admissibility can no longer be
evaluated independently for each part.
When two or more systems interact in a manner that correlates their admissible states, the
constraint sets governing their evolution become partially shared. From that point onward, the

admissible configurations of each subsystem are no longer separable from those of the others. What
remains admissible for one is conditional on what remains admissible for the rest. Entanglement is
the persistence of this joint admissibility structure under subsequent evolution.
In QCT, a composite system is entangled iff its joint admissibility structure cannot be rep-
resented as any convex mixture of independent local admissibility structures—i.e., there is no
decomposition of the form “sometimes (A) is admissible in way (i) while (B) is admissible in way

This framing removes the need to treat entanglement as a special kind of influence. No informa-

at the time outcomes are recorded. The correlations arise because the shared constraint structure
was established during prior interaction and has remained intact. Observation does not generate
this structure; it reveals which joint realization within an already-restricted admissible space has
occurred (including the familiar distinction between unconditional local statistics—unchanged at a
distance—and conditional updates once outcomes are compared).
Entanglement therefore reflects relational persistence, not intrinsic connection. The systems
involved need not be spatially adjacent, dynamically coupled at the time of observation, or contin-
uously interacting. What matters is that their admissible configurations were constrained together
in the past and that no subsequent interactions have introduced sufficient constraint incompatibility
to dissolve that shared structure.
Within this view, the non-classical features of entanglement follow directly. Joint outcome
statistics fail to decompose into independent local descriptions because the admissible state space
itself does not decompose into independent parts. Local descriptions become incomplete not be-
cause of hidden mechanisms or instantaneous influence, but because the relevant constraints are
distributed across the composite system rather than localized within its components.
QCT’s structural account is fully compatible with no superluminal signaling, but it does not
reinstate Bell-local factorization. In Bell terms, the joint admissibility constraints for entangled
systems are irreducibly global, so the theory does not assume that outcomes can be generated by
purely local specifications plus shared randomness while preserving the observed quantum correla-
tions. The “no influence at measurement” claim is therefore a no-signaling claim, not a claim of
Bell-local causality.
The stability of entanglement is governed by the same principles as coherence more generally.
At low effective constraint density, shared constraint structures can persist across extended interac-
tion histories. As additional interactions accumulate—particularly those that couple subsystems to
largely independent environments—the shared constraints fragment. When this occurs, admissibil-

or interpretive intervention.
QCT does not treat entanglement as fundamental or universal. It is contingent on how con-
straints arise, align, and persist. Nor does entanglement imply global coherence or universal con-
nectedness. It is a local structural condition, emergent from specific interaction histories and
vulnerable to constraint incompatibility.
Under this framework, entanglement is neither paradoxical nor exceptional. It is the expected
manifestation of coherence when constraint relations span more than one subsystem. Where con-
straints are shared, admissibility is shared. Where admissibility is shared, entanglement appears.

5 Collapse and the Quantum-Classical
Transition
This Section explains collapse and classical emergence as effective transitions produced by con-
straint aggregation rather than as primitive events, observer effects, or fundamental discontinuities.
As interactions accumulate, joint admissibility narrows until multiple configurations can no longer
persist together. Classical behavior emerges as a high-constraint limit of the same underlying
dynamics that permit quantum coherence.
5.1 Constraint Aggregation and Collapse Thresholds
Importantly, collapse is not treated as a primitive event, a stochastic interruption, or a conse-
quence of observation in QCT. It is understood as an effective transition arising from constraint
aggregation: the accumulation and interaction of limiting conditions that progressively reduce the
joint admissibility of multiple configurations until their simultaneous persistence becomes struc-
turally untenable within the description in use.
At low effective constraint density, multiple admissible configurations can coexist without mu-
tual exclusion. In this regime, distinctions remain underdetermined, environmental records are
sparse or non-redundant, and no single realization is structurally forced to dominate. This is the
domain in which quantum coherence and superposition persist. Crucially, the absence of collapse
here does not imply that reality is “waiting on observation,” but that insufficient structural pressure
has accumulated to enforce exclusion among alternatives.
As interactions accumulate, constraints do not merely add linearly; they couple, reinforce,
and overlap. Environmental entanglement, repeated interaction, boundary formation, and coarse-
grained stabilization introduce correlated restrictions on admissible states. When these restrictions
remain mutually compatible—selecting aligned structures or preserving recoverability—joint admis-
sibility can remain open and superposition may persist, even across increasing scale or duration.
When compatibility fails, admissibility narrows, not by decision, but by structural consequence.
Collapse occurs when the aggregated constraint structure becomes incompatible with the si-
multaneous persistence of multiple configurations. At this threshold, exclusion is no longer partial,
local, or practically reversible. One realization stabilizes not because it is selected, observed, or
favored, but because alternative configurations can no longer coexist without violating the com-
bined constraints imposed by interaction and coupling. Collapse, in this sense, is the exhaustion
of joint admissibility, rather than the invocation of a new rule or a discontinuity in the underlying
dynamics.
This framing also clarifies why superposition can, under certain conditions, be extended beyond
minimal scales. Scale alone does not force collapse. What matters is whether increasing interac-
tion introduces incompatible or redundant constraints. Systems engineered or situated such that
interaction pathways remain sparse, aligned, or correctable can support extended coherence, while
systems exposed to heterogeneous, proliferating, or record-forming interactions transition rapidly
into classical stability. Collapse thresholds therefore depend on constraint geometry and coupling
structure, not on size, distance, or duration in isolation.
Constraint aggregation further explains why collapse is typically abrupt in appearance but
gradual in origin. Long before a classical outcome becomes manifest, admissibility may already be

Within this framework, collapse is neither an anomaly nor a failure of quantum theory. It
is the natural consequence of persistence under pressure. Where constraints remain relaxed and
compatible, coherence endures. Where they accumulate beyond tolerance, resolution follows. This

5.2 Decoherence as Enforced Admissibility Reduction
In QCT, decoherence is not treated as a mysterious loss of quantum behavior, nor as an observer-
driven update, nor as a fundamental dynamical rupture. It is understood as a structural process
by which admissible configurations are progressively restricted through interaction, coupling, and
record formation. Decoherence enforces admissibility reduction: it narrows the space of configura-
tions that can remain mutually compatible under sustained constraint.
In low-constraint regimes, quantum systems may support multiple admissible configurations si-
multaneously. Interference persists because no interaction imposes sufficient restriction to privilege
one configuration over others. As systems interact with their environments, however, admissibility
is no longer evaluated in isolation. Coupling introduces external degrees of freedom that partici-
pate in the system’s state space, expanding the effective description while simultaneously restricting
which configurations can persist without contradiction.
From an operational standpoint, this process corresponds to increasing entanglement between
a system and its environment. Degrees of freedom that were previously internal become correlated
with uncontrolled external states. As a result, interference between alternative configurations
becomes inaccessible to subsystems of the whole. In standard quantum formalism, this appears as
the suppression of off-diagonal terms in the reduced density matrix relative to a particular basis.
In QCT terms, it is the enforcement of incompatibility between alternatives under aggregated
constraint.
Crucially, decoherence does not eliminate states. It does not destroy possibilities, collapse
wavefunctions by fiat, or introduce non-unitary dynamics. Instead, it restructures admissibility.
Configurations that were once jointly admissible can no longer coexist because their persistence
would require mutually incompatible constraint satisfaction across interacting degrees of freedom.
Decoherence therefore marks a transition in what can remain coherently realized, not a physical
annihilation of unrealized alternatives.

This framing clarifies why decoherence is both ubiquitous and context-dependent. The rate and
strength of admissibility reduction increase with coupling density, environmental mode availability,
temperature, scattering probability, and record redundancy. Conversely, coherence can persist when
constraint pathways are minimized or aligned—through isolation, cooling, dynamical decoupling,
or carefully structured environments. These are not exceptions to decoherence, but confirmations
of its constraint-based character.
Decoherence also explains the apparent basis-dependence of classical outcomes. The configu-

to the dominant coupling structure. These stable configurations—often termed pointer states in

This account also distinguishes decoherence from collapse. Decoherence explains why interfer-

many, or an effective classical trajectory depends on interpretive commitments external to QCT.

In this sense, decoherence is not a failure of quantum mechanics, but its structural completion
under constraint. It is the mechanism by which permissive descriptions become restrictive enough to
support persistent identity, classical predictability, and stable records. Where admissibility remains
broad, coherence persists. Where admissibility is enforced, classical behavior follows.
Decoherence, then, is not the negation of quantum theory but the condition under which quan-
tum descriptions cease to admit multiple coexisting realizations. It marks the point at which
constraint structure overwhelms permissiveness, transforming quantum possibility into classical
persistence without invoking observers, hidden variables, or additional physical postulates.
5.3 Classical Determinism as High-Constraint Behavior

all relevant degrees of freedom. Classical behavior is therefore not imposed by new rules, but
produced by the saturation of constraints that enforce persistent identity and predictable evolution.
At high constraint density, admissible configurations are so narrowly restricted that effective
freedom collapses to a single viable trajectory. Alternative possibilities are not merely unlikely;
they are structurally inaccessible. Under these conditions, system evolution appears deterministic,
not because the universe has become rigid or fully specified, but because deviation would violate
constraints required for continued persistence. Determinism, in this sense, is the visible trace of
constraint exhaustion.

Within QCT, classical determinism is therefore treated as an emergent regularity, not a founda-

for persistence. Determinism appears where sustainability demands it, and disappears where it

In summary, classical determinism is the consequence of high constraint density acting on

Where constraints are strong and compatible, outcomes are enforced and trajectories stabilize.
Where constraints relax, determinism dissolves. Classical behavior, in this view, is not the opposite

5.4 Continuity Across Regimes
The quantum–classical transition is not a categorical divide, but a continuous shift in constraint
conditions. Quantum and classical behaviors are not governed by separate principles or ontologies;
they occupy different regions within a single explanatory landscape defined by admissibility and
persistence under constraint.
Across this landscape, no sharp boundary separates coherence from collapse or indeterminacy
from determinism. Instead, systems move gradually along a spectrum as constraint density in-
creases or decreases. What appears experimentally as a sudden transition reflects the exhaustion

The underlying dynamics remain continuous even when observable behavior changes abruptly.
This continuity explains why intermediate regimes are both common and difficult to classify.
Mesoscopic systems, weakly measured states, and partially decohered configurations often exhibit
mixed characteristics: residual interference alongside emergent stability, probabilistic behavior con-
strained by robust patterns, or classical trajectories punctuated by quantum sensitivity. These hy-

Within QCT, regime classification is therefore contextual rather than absolute. A system may

This perspective also clarifies why classical descriptions remain extraordinarily successful despite

high constraint density suppresses sensitivity to micro-level variation. When admissibility is nar-

succeed not by capturing fundamental dynamics, but by exploiting constraint-induced redundancy.
Conversely, the re-emergence of quantum behavior under isolation or constraint relaxation does
not represent a violation of classical principles, but a return to a broader admissibility space. As
constraints loosen, distinctions once suppressed become relevant again. Interference, indeterminacy,
and superposition reappear because they were never eliminated—only rendered inaccessible under
prior conditions.
Continuity across regimes therefore supports a unified explanatory account. Quantum mechan-

behavior in regions of high constraint density. Neither description is privileged absolutely. Each is
valid within the domain where its assumptions about admissibility and persistence hold.
By framing the quantum–classical relationship in this way, QCT avoids both reductionism and
fragmentation. It does not seek to derive one regime entirely from the other, nor to isolate them as
fundamentally distinct. Instead, it explains their coexistence as expressions of the same constraint-
governed structure operating under different conditions.

more generally. Observational interactions tend to align with the thermodynamic arrow because

or agency, but by the asymmetry introduced when constraints propagate beyond local control.

In this sense, collapse without consciousness is not a radical proposal. It is the minimal claim
required for a coherent, observer-independent account of physical stability. The universe does not
wait to be noticed in order to persist.
6.3 Observerless Quantum Systems
Any account of stabilization, decoherence, or collapse must apply universally, including in the
absence of observers. Physical systems evolve, interact, and stabilize whether or not they are
monitored, recorded, or experienced. Chemical reactions, stellar fusion, geological processes, and
cosmological structure formation all proceed without observation, yet display the same transition
from quantum permissiveness to stable classical outcomes. An explanatory framework that assigns
a necessary causal role to observation must therefore account for why identical stabilizing behavior
occurs where observation is entirely absent.
Within QCT, observers are not privileged entities but physical subsystems embedded within
the same constraint landscape as all others. An observer differs only in its capacity to form stable
internal correlations with existing environmental records. That capacity does not introduce new
dynamics. It extends an already-established network of constraints by adding further physical
correlations. Observation, in this sense, tracks stabilization; it does not create it.
The mechanisms that govern admissibility narrowing operate independently of awareness. Con-
straints arise from interaction structure: coupling Hamiltonians, boundary conditions, environ-
mental entanglement, and coarse-graining. Admissibility refers to the subset of state histories or
configurations that remain dynamically stable and mutually compatible under those constraints
at a given descriptive resolution. As interactions accumulate, admissible alternatives may be re-
duced, but this reduction depends solely on physical coupling and compatibility, not on whether
any subsystem registers the result.

In QCT, collapse is therefore not defined by observation but by constraint structure. Collapse

Any theory that assigns collapse, stabilization, or classicality to conscious registration fails a
basic universality requirement: it cannot account for observerless persistence. QCT instead adopts

6.4 Removal of Observer Exceptionalism
Quantum Coherence Theory rejects observer exceptionalism as an explanatory principle. The
mechanisms that govern the stabilization of physical systems, the loss of interference, and the
emergence of classical behavior must operate universally, including in regimes where no observers
are present and no measurements are read or recorded by conscious agents. Any account that assigns
a privileged causal role to observation or awareness therefore inherits an explanatory burden: it
must explain why the same stabilizing behavior occurs reliably in the absence of observers across
cosmological, geological, chemical, and microscopic domains.

ontological category. An observer is simply a subsystem capable of forming stable internal correla-
tions with existing records. This capacity introduces no new physical law. It extends the network
of constraints already in place by coupling an additional system to structures that have already
stabilized through interaction. Observation, in this sense, participates in constraint propagation;

This framing dissolves the distinction between “observed” and “unobserved” systems at the

7 Informational Closure and Curl Equi-
librium
This section establishes informational closure as explanatory sufficiency rather than metaphysi-
cal finality. It shows how shared constraints determine admissibility across part–whole descriptions,
how unresolved incompatibilities redistribute rather than vanish under closure, and how stability
can emerge without rigidity. The section frames closure as a disciplined boundary on explanation,
not a new dynamics or ontology.
7.1 Informational Closure as Explanatory Exhaustion
Informational closure, as used in Quantum Coherence Theory, refers to explanatory exhaus-
tion rather than metaphysical finality. A description is informationally closed when no additional
ontic primitives are required to account for transitions between behavioral regimes—specifically,
the persistence of coherence under low constraint density and the emergence of classical stability
under high constraint density. Closure marks the point at which explanatory power no longer in-
creases by introducing new entities, forces, or postulates, and instead rests entirely on the structure,
compatibility, and interaction of constraints governing admissible realizations.
This notion of closure is explicitly methodological. It does not assert that reality itself is
complete, finite, or fully captured by the present framework. Nor does it deny the possible existence
of hidden variables, deeper dynamics, or future theoretical extensions. Informational closure claims
only that, within the explanatory scope defined here, invoking such additions is unnecessary to
account for the observed quantum–classical transition. If explanation can be carried to completion
using constraint structure and admissibility alone, then explanatory closure has been achieved,
regardless of what deeper descriptions may later emerge.
Importantly, informational closure does not imply that information is a conserved substance, a
causal agent, or a fundamental physical field. In QCT, information is descriptive of constrained real-
ization: it characterizes how possibilities are restricted, correlated, and stabilized under interaction.
Closure therefore does not rest on information as an ontological primitive, but on the sufficiency
of constraint-based descriptions to account for persistence, decoherence, and stabilization without
remainder.
For shared constraints to function as shared, constraint-relevant information must be able to
circulate across the system within a timescale compatible with identity persistence. Informational
closure therefore presupposes neither instantaneous global coordination nor static isolation, but
finite, tolerance-bounded propagation of constraint correlations. If propagation were effectively
unbounded, local differentiation could not stabilize before global coupling dissolved structure. If
propagation were effectively negligible, parts could not maintain joint admissibility before decoher-
ence fragmented identity. Closure thus depends not on the speed of propagation as a parameter,
but on the existence of finite circulation that enables coordinated persistence without rigid lockstep.
The criterion for informational closure is operational and falsifiable. If explaining the transi-
tion from coherent multiplicity to classical definiteness requires the introduction of an independent
dynamical variable not expressible in terms of constraint structure, admissibility, and their circula-
tion—such as a primitive collapse trigger, observer-dependent intervention, or irreducible stochastic
law—then closure fails. Conversely, if observed behavior can be fully accounted for by how interac-

tions impose compatibility conditions, restrict joint admissibility, propagate constraint correlations,
and render interference operationally inaccessible, then closure holds at the explanatory level.
Closure should therefore not be confused with reductionism or finality. It does not claim that
all phenomena reduce to a single mechanism, nor that future theory is foreclosed. Instead, it
delineates a boundary of sufficiency: beyond this boundary, additional assumptions do not increase
explanatory resolution for the phenomena under consideration. Within that boundary, constraint
structure and its finite circulation provide a complete account of why certain configurations persist
while others fail to remain jointly admissible.
By framing closure in this way, QCT avoids both instrumental minimalism and metaphysical
overreach. It neither dismisses unanswered questions nor inflates its scope to address them prema-
turely. Informational closure marks the point at which explanation has done all the work it can do
using the resources already introduced. What lies beyond that point may be real, significant, or
even necessary for other domains—but it is not required to explain the quantum–classical transition
as a problem of constrained persistence.
In this sense, informational closure functions as a disciplinary safeguard rather than a procla-
mation. It prevents the unnecessary multiplication of primitives while remaining open to future
refinement. The framework is considered closed not because inquiry must stop, but because expla-
nation, at the level addressed here, has reached sufficiency without remainder.
7.2 Part–Whole Equivalence Under Shared Constraints
Part–whole equivalence, within Quantum Coherence Theory, is not a claim about ontological
identity, reducibility, or the elimination of subsystem distinctions. It is a conditional statement
about explanatory sufficiency. When a system and its subsystems are governed by the same con-
straint structure, descriptions at the part level and at the whole level are equivalent with respect
to explaining persistence, exclusion, and stability under those constraints.
This equivalence applies only within a defined domain: systems that share a common constraint
boundary and whose admissible realizations are restricted by the same interaction conditions. Un-
der such circumstances, shifting between part-level and whole-level descriptions does not introduce
new explanatory primitives. The same constraints delimit admissibility regardless of how the sys-
tem is partitioned, and no additional assumptions are required to account for which configurations
persist and which are excluded.
Equivalence here is therefore methodological rather than ontological. It concerns what must
be assumed to explain behavior, not which properties belong to which components. Part–whole
equivalence does not imply that subsystems possess all the properties of the whole, nor that col-
lective phenomena reduce to local attributes. Emergent properties may arise at the composite
level that are not attributable to any single part. The claim is instead that, where constraints are
fully shared, such properties do not require additional explanatory resources beyond those already
present in the constraint structure.
This principle becomes most visible in systems exhibiting strong correlation or entanglement.
In such cases, admissibility is determined at the joint level rather than independently for each com-
ponent. Local descriptions remain operationally useful, but they do not fully specify which global
configurations are stable or excluded. The need for a joint description does not arise from meta-
physical holism, but from the fact that the relevant constraints span the partition. Admissibility,
under these conditions, is a property of the constrained composite, not of its parts in isolation.
Crucially, shared constraints also imply shared incompatibilities. When a configuration is locally
admissible in one part but incompatible with the joint constraint structure, the resulting tension

cannot be resolved by appeal to any single subsystem. Instead, incompatibility must be evaluated
at the level where constraints are shared. This establishes the conditions under which unresolved
contradiction cannot be eliminated locally and must instead persist at the composite level.
Part–whole equivalence therefore sets the stage for understanding how systems maintain stabil-
ity in the presence of incompatibility. When admissibility is evaluated jointly and constraints are
shared, local resolution of contradiction may be impossible without violating global persistence.
Under such conditions, stability is preserved not by eliminating contradiction, but by allowing it
to circulate within the constraint structure without collapsing identity.
Importantly, shared constraints do not guarantee equivalence across all descriptions. When
constraints differ across partitions—when subsystems are subject to distinct boundary conditions,
interaction regimes, or coupling strengths—part–whole equivalence breaks down. Subsystem be-
havior may then diverge from whole-system behavior in ways that require separate explanatory
treatment. Equivalence is therefore conditional, not universal.
By framing part–whole relations in this way, QCT avoids both reductionism and monism. It
neither insists that wholes are nothing more than their parts, nor that parts dissolve into an undif-
ferentiated totality. Instead, it identifies the circumstances under which explanatory distinctions
between part and whole become unnecessary because constraint satisfaction is evaluated at the
same structural level in both descriptions.
Part–whole equivalence under shared constraints thus functions as a guide for explanatory
economy and as a boundary condition for stability. Where the same constraint structure governs
admissibility, multiplying descriptive layers does not deepen explanation. Where constraints di-
verge, distinctions reassert themselves. This principle does not erase structure; it clarifies when
structure must be described jointly and prepares the ground for understanding how unresolved
incompatibilities are sustained without collapse.
7.3 Curl Equilibrium
Curl equilibrium, within Quantum Coherence Theory, is a diagnostic label for a specific struc-
tural pattern that emerges once informational closure has been reached. It denotes the persistence
of residual incompatibilities that cannot be resolved through further local constraint satisfaction
and therefore remain distributed across correlated degrees of freedom. The concept is descriptive
rather than dynamical: it introduces no new forces, operators, or laws of evolution, but character-
izes how unresolved constraint demands are organized after all reducible inconsistencies have been
exhausted.
Constraint closure, as used here, denotes the condition in which all propagatable compatibility
conditions have been enforced. At closure, no further local reconfiguration can reduce incompatibil-
ity without generating it elsewhere. What remains is not a failure of explanation, but a remainder
supported by the global structure of constraints themselves. Curl equilibrium names this condition
of globally consistent closure accompanied by irreducible, non-localizable residual incompatibility.
The term curl is used analogically, not as a vector calculus operator. It indicates that residual
incompatibility is supported on cycles or loops within the constraint structure rather than at any
single site. In such cases, incompatibility cannot be eliminated outright; it can only be redistributed
across the cycle. Any local adjustment that resolves it at one point necessarily reintroduces it
elsewhere. The equilibrium consists in the containment and circulation of this residual within the
closed structure of admissibility.
Metaphorically, curl equilibrium may be understood as unresolved informational incompatibility
that can no longer propagate outward into resolution. Once constraint closure has been achieved,
49

===== PAGE 50 =====
no additional degrees of freedom remain available to absorb or discharge residual incompatibility.
What persists is therefore not an outward flow of unresolved information, but its confinement
within the internal topology of the constraint structure itself. In this sense, information may be
said to “curl inward,” not spatially or causally, but structurally—remaining trapped on cycles of
admissibility where it can neither be eliminated nor localized.
A simple illustrative case is a triangular constraint structure in which three variables are pairwise
constrained in mutually incompatible ways. Any two constraints can be satisfied simultaneously,
but not all three. After closure, one incompatibility must remain. Importantly, which constraint
appears violated depends on representation: through reparameterization or redistribution, the
residual incompatibility can be shifted from one edge of the triangle to another, but never eliminated
entirely. The incompatibility is cycle-supported rather than point-localized. This pattern is what
curl equilibrium denotes.
Residual incompatibility in this sense should not be interpreted as logical inconsistency or
paradox. It refers specifically to non-co-satisfiable admissibility demands: sets of constraints that
cannot all be simultaneously realized within a single configuration, even though each is individually
valid. Curl equilibrium therefore describes a bookkeeping property of admissibility under closure,
not a breakdown of consistency, causality, or physical law.
This pattern has analogues in familiar physical and mathematical contexts. In constrained sys-
tems containing cycles—such as frustrated networks or contextual correlation structures—global
consistency can coexist with irreducible local tradeoffs. Similarly, in correlated quantum systems,
admissibility structures may exist in which no single global assignment satisfies all local contexts,
even though each context is internally consistent. Curl equilibrium is intended to remain compat-
ible with such phenomena without importing their formal machinery or committing to a specific
interpretation.
Curl equilibrium arises only under identifiable conditions: (i) a constraint structure containing
cycles or equivalent non-tree-like dependencies, (ii) mutually incompatible constraints on at least
one such cycle, and (iii) closure of all reducible incompatibilities. When these conditions are met,
residual incompatibility cannot permanently localize; it remains supported on the cycle space of
the constraint structure.
The concept carries limited but definite diagnostic content. If, after closure, residual incompat-
ibilities were observed to vanish entirely in systems whose constraint geometry contains irreducible
cycles, curl equilibrium would not apply. Likewise, if residual strain consistently localized to a
single site without redistribution under reparameterization, the notion would be inappropriate.
Curl equilibrium therefore does not claim universality; it applies only where constraint geometry
supports it.
Finally, curl equilibrium should not be mistaken for a mechanism of stabilization, a principle
of optimization, or a source of dynamics. It does not explain how systems evolve. Instead, it
clarifies how unresolved incompatibility is organized once evolution and interaction have enforced
all possible local consistency. In this sense, curl equilibrium functions as a descriptive invariant of
closed admissibility structures rather than an additional explanatory engine.
7.4 Closure Without Rigidity
Informational closure does not imply causal isolation, ontic completeness, or predictive finality.
It does not assert that a system contains all information required to determine its future, nor that
external influences are absent. Closure, as used here, is a property of an explanatory account at

a given resolution: the transition from quantum coherence to classical stability can be explained
without introducing additional ontic primitives beyond constraint structure, admissibility, and
standard physical dynamics.
This distinction is essential. Informational closure constrains explanatory vocabulary rather
than physical behavior. It specifies that no supplementary collapse triggers, observer-dependent
interventions, teleological principles, or ad hoc classicality postulates are required to account for
persistence, decoherence, and stabilization. It does not deny unitary evolution, Hamiltonian dy-
namics, or established physical laws. Instead, it asserts that such dynamics, when combined with
interaction-induced constraints and admissibility reduction, are sufficient for explanation at the
level addressed by QCT.
Closure therefore does not entail rigidity or strict determinism. It does not fix unique trajec-

of high constraint density—regimes in which environmental coupling, coarse-graining, and rapid
decoherence narrow admissibility so severely that alternative configurations cease to persist. This
determinism concerns predictability and stability, not ontic inevitability. Where constraint density

Probabilistic descriptions remain fully legitimate within a closed explanatory framework. Two
roles of probability are preserved. First, probability may reflect incomplete constraint specification,
coarse-graining, or limited access to environmental correlations—an operational or epistemic role.
Second, probability arises formally within quantum theory itself, as in Born-weighted statistics.
Quantum Coherence Theory does not take a position on the metaphysical status of quantum
probability. Its claim is narrower: probabilistic models remain valid effective descriptions under
closure, provided they respect the same constraint and admissibility structure.
Informational closure also excludes teleology and optimization as explanatory primitives. Sta-
bility does not arise because systems minimize, maximize, or pursue preferred outcomes. It arises
because incompatible configurations fail to remain jointly admissible under interaction. Persistence
is therefore explained through exclusion rather than selection: outcomes endure because alterna-
tives are structurally disallowed, not because they are chosen or favored.
Nor does closure collapse all descriptions into a single scale or perspective. Multiple effective
models—quantum, classical, probabilistic—may coexist so long as they agree on shared invariants,
respect the same constraint structure, and yield consistent predictions in overlapping regimes.
Descriptive plurality is constrained but not eliminated. Closure restricts explanatory inflation,
not legitimate modeling diversity. Finally, informational closure remains conditional and revisable.
If future empirical findings require the introduction of a genuinely new explanatory primitive—such
as an irreducible, context-dependent collapse dynamics not expressible in terms of constraint ag-
gregation or admissibility reduction—then closure, as defined here, would fail for those phenomena.
Until such necessity is demonstrated, closure functions as a discipline rather than a doctrine: it
prevents unnecessary multiplication of primitives while remaining open to refinement.
Closure without rigidity thus preserves explanatory sufficiency alongside physical openness. It
explains persistence without overdetermining behavior, accommodates variability without mystifi-
cation, and maintains continuity with established physics while setting clear boundaries on what
additional assumptions are warranted.

8.1 Non-Sequential Informational Consistency
This section addresses how quantum correlations and collapse-like transitions can be explained

correlation.
QCT treats admissibility as prior to temporal narration. Admissibility denotes the set of re-

within QCT.
Non-sequential consistency does not imply that physical events occur outside time, nor that
time is illusory. It asserts only that the consistency conditions governing joint outcomes need not
be expressed as a step-by-step causal chain ordered in time. In particular, correlations observed
in entangled systems need not be explained by signals propagating between subsystems, nor by
a privileged temporal slicing of the experimental arrangement. Instead, joint outcomes satisfy a
shared constraint structure defined by preparation conditions, interaction topology, and record-

admissibility is evaluated relative to the complete constraint configuration of an experiment, local
interventions remain free boundary conditions within that configuration. Parameter independence
and no-signaling are preserved: no agent can exploit global consistency to transmit information or
influence outcomes at a distance. The global aspect constrains joint realizations, not transmittable
causal influence.
Collapse-like transitions, in this framework, correspond to admissibility reduction under con-
straint aggregation. As interactions with an environment produce stable, redundant records, the
set of admissible realizations compatible with those records contracts. This reduction is effectively
irreversible at macroscopic scales. QCT does not fix whether this reduction should be interpreted
as a physical pruning of realized configurations or as an effective update of physically grounded de-
scriptions; it characterizes only the structural conditions under which collapse-like behavior appears
unavoidable.

8.2 Global Constraint Satisfaction Versus Local Causa-

Global constraint satisfaction is introduced to address a specific explanatory gap: how joint
outcome structures arise in correlated systems without appeal to superluminal influence, observer
privilege, or hidden sequential mechanisms. It is not proposed as a competing causal process, nor
as a replacement for local dynamical laws. Local causation remains sufficient and indispensable for
describing controllable interventions, signal propagation, and ordinary physical interactions. Global
constraint satisfaction operates at a different explanatory level, accounting for the compatibility
relations that govern which joint configurations are admissible once all relevant constraints are in
place.
In this framework, a constraint refers to any physical or formal condition that restricts the set
of admissible joint realizations of a system. These include, but are not limited to, the prepared
quantum state, interaction structure, boundary conditions imposed by experimental settings, con-
servation laws, and the formation of stable records through environmental coupling. A realization
denotes a complete assignment of outcome records for a given experimental run that is consistent
with these conditions. Admissibility is therefore a support concept: it distinguishes configurations
that can persist under the full constraint structure from those that cannot, without itself assigning
probabilities or weights.
Quantum correlations present cases in which admissibility is determined at the composite level
rather than by independent local assignments. In such systems, joint outcome distributions cannot
be factorized into products of locally causal contributions without loss of consistency. This is not
merely an inconvenience of representation, but a structural fact: the relevant constraints act across
the partition, restricting which combinations of local outcomes can jointly occur. The failure here
is not of operational locality, but of Bell-style outcome independence. Parameter independence
and no-signaling conditions are preserved; what fails is the assumption that joint outcomes can be
decomposed into independent local result assignments conditioned only on shared past variables.
Global constraint satisfaction therefore does not imply global signaling or controllable nonlocal
influence. Constraints restrict the set of jointly admissible outcomes, but they do not provide agents
with leverage to manipulate distant results beyond what is already encoded in the shared constraint
structure. Local interventions alter local boundary conditions—such as measurement settings or
coupling regimes—but they do not enable the transmission of information outside locally causal
channels. The global character of admissibility concerns correlation structure, not causal control.
Local causation retains its full operational role within this account. Where agents intervene,
vary experimental parameters, or propagate signals, causal descriptions remain appropriate and
necessary. QCT does not weaken or replace such descriptions. Instead, it situates them within

a broader explanatory context: local causal narratives describe how boundary conditions are im-
plemented and propagated, while global constraint satisfaction describes why only certain joint
configurations remain admissible once those boundaries are fixed. The two levels address different
questions and are not in competition.
This distinction clarifies why purely sequential causal stories often fail to account for quantum
correlations. Attempts to force a time-ordered micro-causal explanation typically introduce addi-
tional assumptions—hidden variables, privileged foliations, or implicit signaling mechanisms—that
exceed what is empirically required. QCT avoids these additions by recognizing that admissibil-
ity is evaluated relative to the complete constraint configuration of the experimental context, not
generated step-by-step through local influence. The absence of a sequential causal microstory is
therefore not a deficiency, but a reflection of the explanatory level at which the phenomenon is
constrained.
Importantly, global constraint satisfaction does not introduce teleology, optimization, or goal-
directed behavior. Systems do not select outcomes in order to satisfy constraints. Rather, config-

correspond to any realization consistent with the full set of conditions. Stability arises through ex-
clusion, not choice. The language of satisfaction refers to compatibility within a constraint system,
not to evaluation, preference, or purpose.
By distinguishing global constraint satisfaction from local causation, this framework preserves
the integrity of causal explanation while accounting for phenomena that exceed its scope. Local
causation governs how changes propagate and how interventions take effect; global constraints
govern which joint configurations are admissible once all relevant conditions are specified. This
separation allows QCT to explain nonlocal correlation without invoking nonlocal causation, to
maintain no-signaling and intervention locality, and to remain continuous with established physical
practice while extending explanatory reach where purely sequential accounts prove insufficient.
8.3 Emergence of Temporal Asymmetry Under Entropy
Temporal asymmetry is not treated as a fundamental property of microscopic law, but as an
emergent feature of constraint aggregation and admissibility reduction in high-constraint regimes.
The framework does not posit an intrinsic arrow of time at the level of unitary dynamics. Instead,
it explains why time-directed behavior reliably appears once interaction, record formation, and
environmental coupling narrow the space of admissible realizations in a manner that is effectively
irreversible.
Entropy, in this account, functions as a descriptive proxy rather than a causal driver. Rising
entropy reflects the proliferation and entrenchment of constraints imposed by interaction, coarse-
graining, and record redundancy. As systems interact with increasingly complex environments,
admissible configurations compatible with prior records contract rapidly. This contraction is asym-
metric in practice, not because the underlying dynamics forbid reversal, but because reversing the
accumulated constraints would require coordinated control over an astronomically large number
of correlated degrees of freedom. QCT therefore presupposes low initial constraint density as a
boundary condition for temporal asymmetry, without attempting to explain why such a condition
obtained.
Irreversibility, as used here, is operational rather than fundamental. Microscopic laws may
remain time-reversal invariant, but admissibility reduction becomes effectively one-directional once
constraint density passes a threshold at which recoherence or global reversal is no longer practically

attainable. Configurations compatible with existing records dominate the admissible space, while
alternatives shrink to measure-zero sets relative to realistic control and access. The arrow of
time thus emerges from constraint geometry and record stability, not from an added dynamical
asymmetry.
This perspective clarifies why temporal asymmetry is graded rather than absolute. Different
subsystems may exhibit different degrees of reversibility depending on their coupling to the envi-
ronment and the density of constraints they experience. Weakly coupled or carefully isolated sub-
systems may retain low constraint density and support reversible or interference-capable behavior,
even while the larger environment exhibits robust classical irreversibility. Temporal directionality
is therefore not a universal switch but a spectrum tied to constraint aggregation.
Importantly, this account avoids teleology. Systems do not evolve toward preferred futures, min-
imize entropy, or optimize outcomes. Stability arises through exclusion: configurations incompati-
ble with accumulated constraints fail to remain jointly admissible, while compatible configurations
persist. Temporal asymmetry reflects the asymmetry of admissibility under record-conditioned
interaction, not the pursuit of an end state.
Finally, the emergence of temporal asymmetry under entropy is compatible with established
physical descriptions. It aligns with decoherence-based accounts, redundancy of environmental
records, and thermodynamic irreversibility, while reframing them in constraint-based terms. QCT
does not replace these frameworks; it explains why they jointly produce a stable arrow of time
without requiring fundamental temporal bias. Time’s direction, in this view, is not imposed by law
but arises from the structural consequences of constrained persistence.
8.4 Compatibility With Established Physical Laws
This framework is explicitly constrained by compatibility requirements rather than motivated

9 Shared Invariants and Mutual Con-
straint Participation
This Section formalizes a central requirement of interaction and persistence: systems can only
participate in a shared world if they are reducible to at least one shared invariant. Interaction is
understood as mutual constraint participation, and shared invariants are shown to be the minimal
structures that make coherence, compatibility, and viability across systems possible.
9.1 Shared Invariants and Reducibility
The preceding sections have established that persistence, identity, and information all require
constraint, and that constraint enforces persistence only through invariance. A structure persists
only insofar as something about it remains fixed across admissible transformation. Information
exists only where realized differentiation remains stable relative to excluded alternatives, and record
authority arises only when such stability constrains future interaction. These results apply locally,
at the level of individual systems and interactions, and do not yet presuppose any global ontological
commitment.
A further structural consequence now follows. If multiple physical structures interact in a way
that produces persistent joint effects—durable correlations, records, or stabilized mutual restric-
tions—then their interaction must be expressible in terms of at least one shared invariant. Without
a shared invariant, no joint constraint can be enforced, and no persistent interaction can occur.
Apparent coordination may arise momentarily, but it cannot stabilize as an authoritative restriction
on admissible evolution.
In Quantum Coherence Theory, interaction is defined as mutual participation in a constraint
relation that restricts admissible evolution for all participants. For such a restriction to be jointly
binding, it must be referenced to something that remains invariant across the coupled evolution
of the interacting structures. This invariant need not be fundamental, scalar, or unique; it may
be relational, approximate, or emergent. It need only be sufficient to support re-identification and
exclusion across interaction. Where no such invariant exists, interaction collapses into transient
coincidence rather than enforceable constraint.
This requirement is already implicit in the earlier treatment of persistence and identity. Persis-
tence requires invariance, and when persistence extends across interacting systems, invariance must
be shared. Otherwise, admissibility fragments into incompatible descriptions that cannot jointly
constrain future evolution. In such cases, each system may exhibit local stability, but no globally
authoritative interaction can be sustained.
If the ontology under consideration is closed—that is, if all interaction, persistence, and record
formation occur entirely within a single self-contained descriptive boundary—then a stronger re-
quirement follows. In a closed ontology, all shared invariants that support persistent interaction
must themselves be mutually reducible. If they were not, the ontology would decompose into
disjoint constraint domains incapable of enforcing global admissibility. Records stabilized in one
invariant regime would lack authority in another, and persistence would become local but not
transferable.
This reducibility requirement does not imply that all systems are identical, nor that all dynamics
collapse to a single variable. It is not eliminative reduction. It is a condition on comparability

and mutual constraint participation. For two structures to jointly constrain one another within a
closed ontology, their invariants must be mappable—directly or indirectly—into a common invariant
framework. Where no such mapping exists, interaction cannot be globally authoritative.
The same condition applies to information. Stabilized information can propagate and remain
mutually accessible only if the exclusion relations that define it are preserved under interaction.
That preservation is impossible unless the interacting systems share a reducible invariant structure.
Information that cannot be reduced to a shared invariant cannot survive interaction; it decoheres
into incompatibility rather than persisting as constraint.
Importantly, this claim remains structural rather than metaphysical. It does not assert what
the shared invariant is, nor that it is unique in any ultimate sense, nor that it is directly observable.
It asserts only that viability within a closed constraint system requires invariance closure: all local
invariants that participate in persistent interaction must ultimately be mutually reducible. Without
such reducibility, persistence cannot be globally enforced, and a coherent world-description cannot
be sustained.
With this condition made explicit, the internal architecture of Quantum Coherence Theory is
complete. Constraint enforces admissibility. Invariance enables persistence. Shared invariance en-
ables interaction. Reducibility of invariants enables global enforceability within a closed descriptive
boundary. What remains is not a further refinement of these principles, but an evaluation of which
ontological descriptions can satisfy them without contradiction.
9.2 Modes of Shared Invariance and Reducibility
The information throughout this work established a necessary structural condition of Quantum
Coherence Theory: persistent interaction within a closed constraint system requires shared invari-
ance, and global enforceability requires that such invariants be mutually reducible. That condition,
however, does not specify a single form that invariance or reducibility must take. Shared invariance
may be exact or approximate, local or global, fundamental or emergent. Reducibility may be direct,
indirect, structural, or statistical. The requirement imposed by QCT is not uniformity of form, but
preservation of constraint authority across interaction.
In QCT, an invariant is defined operationally by its role rather than its representation. An
invariant is whatever stabilizes exclusion and re-identification under admissible evolution. It is
that which allows a realized differentiation to remain authoritative across interaction, disturbance,
and time. Shared invariance therefore means that interacting structures preserve a common basis
for exclusion and identity, even if that basis is encoded differently within each system. Reducibility,
in turn, requires that these bases be translatable in a way that preserves their constraint-enforcing
role.
In practice, invariants are often approximate rather than exact. Persistence is therefore always
relative to a tolerance and a timescale. A shared invariant may stabilize interaction only within a
bounded regime, degrading gradually as drift, noise, or coupling erodes its reliability. QCT treats
persistence as graded rather than absolute: record authority weakens continuously as invariance
degrades, rather than disappearing abruptly. This does not weaken the framework; it reflects the
operational conditions under which constraint enforcement occurs in real systems.
Shared invariants may also be emergent rather than fundamental. Many interaction-relevant
invariants—thermodynamic variables, functional states, computational configurations—do not re-
duce to unique micro-level descriptors. QCT permits such emergence provided that the invariant
continues to support exclusion and re-identification under interaction. Reducibility in this context

does not require one-to-one correspondence. It requires only that there exist a mapping, possibly
many-to-one, that preserves the constraint-relevant structure. What matters is not preservation of
detail, but preservation of enforceability.
Reducibility likewise need not take the form of literal identity. Distinct invariant frameworks
may encode the same constraint regime through different structures that are nonetheless inter-
translatable. Such equivalence may be indirect or mediated by intermediate descriptions, and it
may operate at the level of relations rather than variables. QCT treats these cases as admissible
so long as the translations preserve exclusion relations and re-identification conditions. Dual or
complementary descriptions therefore do not fragment the ontology unless their invariants fail to
compose under interaction.
Stochastic reducibility is also compatible with closure. In many systems, constraint authority
is preserved only in expectation or within bounded variance. Statistical mechanics, measurement
processes, and adaptive systems often operate in this regime. QCT allows such cases provided
that the stochastic translation preserves admissibility and record authority at the level governing
interaction. Reducibility need not be deterministic to be effective; it must only be sufficient to
maintain constraint enforcement across coupling.
Finally, QCT distinguishes genuine shared invariants from representational artifacts. Gauge
freedoms, coordinate conventions, and descriptive redundancies do not count as invariants unless
they participate in interaction constraints. Reducibility is imposed only on invariants defined over
the physically admissible configuration space—after quotienting out purely representational degrees
of freedom. This ensures that closure tracks real interaction capacity rather than notational overlap.
Taken together, these modes clarify the scope of the reducibility requirement without weakening
it. Invariance may be approximate, emergent, dual, or statistical; reducibility may be homomorphic,
indirect, or probabilistic. Across all cases, the criterion is the same: whatever carries constraint
authority across interaction must remain translatable without breaking persistence. This preserves
the internal completeness of QCT while preparing the ground for a subsequent evaluation of which
ontological descriptions can satisfy this requirement without contradiction.
9.3 Invariants as the Basis of Mutual Constraint
Persistence requires invariance and that interaction, in Quantum Coherence Theory, is defined
as mutual participation in a constraint that restricts admissible evolution for all participants. This
definition already excludes purely causal influence or coincidental correlation. A constraint that is
genuinely mutual must bind each participant under the same admissibility conditions across time.
The question this section addresses is what makes such mutual binding possible at all.
For a constraint to be mutual rather than merely asymmetric, it must possess identity across the
interaction history. A restriction that applies at one moment but is not the same restriction at the
next cannot function as an enforceable boundary on admissibility. Without continuity, exclusions
cannot accumulate, and no structure can stabilize. Invariance supplies the identity conditions of
the constraint itself, fixing what counts as the same restriction across coupled evolution.
When two or more systems interact in a way that produces durable joint effects, their interaction
must therefore be organized around at least one invariant that all participants remain answerable
to. This invariant need not dictate the internal dynamics of each system, nor require similarity of
structure or mechanism. It functions instead as a shared criterion of compatibility: each system
may vary internally, but only variations that preserve the invariant remain admissible within the
interaction. In this sense, the invariant is not merely conserved through interaction; it is what
interaction coheres around.

If no such shared invariant exists, apparent mutual constraint collapses upon inspection. One
system may influence another, but the influence cannot be reciprocally binding. Correlations may
appear momentarily, but nothing enforces their continuation. Exclusions imposed at one moment
cannot be re-imposed coherently at the next, because there is no preserved reference that fixes
what exclusion means. What remains is mutual effect without mutual authority.
This distinction clarifies why invariance precedes records and information in QCT. A record
constrains future interaction only if later admissibility remains answerable to the same exclusion
relations that defined the record. That answerability presupposes an invariant structure that per-
sists across time and coupling. Records therefore do not create mutual constraint; they are products
of an interaction regime already stabilized by shared invariance.
In Quantum Coherence Theory, invariants are thus not passive quantities that happen to sur-
vive interaction. They are the structural basis of mutual accountability. They fix the identity of
constraints across time, make exclusions enforceable, and allow interaction to rise above transient
coincidence. Without shared invariance, there is no mutual constraint; without mutual constraint,
there is no persistent interaction to be explained.
9.4 Shared Invariants and Record Authority
If interaction requires a shared invariant, then durable interaction requires more than momen-
tary constraint satisfaction. It requires the ability to preserve interaction-state across time such
that participants can re-identify what counts as “the same interaction” despite temporal separa-
tion, partial observation, or independent reconstruction. This persistence cannot be achieved by a
shared invariant alone. It requires an invariant-preserving record.
Here, “record” does not mean a ledger, log, or centralized store. It refers to the minimal
commitment state sufficient for participants to distinguish admissible continuations of interaction
from noise. In other words, a record is the minimal invariant-reducible structure that allows
interaction history to be reconstructed up to equivalence. Full historical detail is neither required
nor generally possible.
This distinction matters because a shared invariant can constrain valid moves in the present,
but it cannot by itself support temporal properties such as:
•distinguishing novelty from repetition,
•detecting omission, duplication, or reordering,
•preserving commitments across interaction boundaries.
These are diachronic constraints. A record is the mechanism by which the invariant is applied
across time.
Record authority arises not from control, consensus, or enforcement, but from invariance. A
record is authoritative to the extent that any participating system can independently verify that
it belongs to the invariant-defined equivalence class of admissible histories. Authority here is not
permission to write, but admissibility under shared constraint. Consensus mechanisms, when they
exist, do not create authority; they merely help systems remain within invariant bounds.
Crucially, authoritative records are not located inside systems. They are maintained between
systems as invariant-preserving relations. Individual systems may store internal representations,
but those representations derive their authority only insofar as they remain reducible to the shared

invariant. When representations diverge, the invariant adjudicates—not by force, but by viability.
What cannot be reduced together cannot be preserved together.
This means that purely private records cannot ground interaction. A private memory may feel
authoritative locally, but unless it can be reconstructed and validated by others under the shared
invariant, it cannot participate in mutual constraint. The moment interaction resumes, invariant
compatibility—not belief, confidence, or recollection—determines what persists.
Record authority therefore precedes semantics. Participants may disagree about meaning, in-
terpretation, or intent while still maintaining a shared record, so long as the invariant-relevant
structure is preserved. Structural agreement enables stable disagreement. Meaning operates on
top of record authority; it does not establish it.
There is also a hard limit: no record can exceed the expressive capacity of the invariant that
sustains it. Distinctions not constrained by the shared invariant cannot be jointly preserved. They
are not false, but non-shareable. Attempts to encode excess structure either decay into noise or
fracture interaction viability. Record sufficiency is therefore a constraint, not a deficiency.
Finally, the authority of records depends on the openness or closure of the underlying ontology.
In open ontologies, records remain provisional and revisable as new shared invariants emerge. In
closed ontologies, record authority hardens: once invariant sufficiency is saturated, no further
mutually admissible reduction is possible. This closure is structural, not procedural, and marks
the transition from revisable interaction to finalized history.
In sum, shared invariants do not merely permit interaction. They determine what can be jointly
remembered. Record authority is not granted, stored, or enforced; it is earned through invariance.
What cannot be reconstructed together cannot function as part of a shared reality.
9.5 Closed Ontology and Invariant Sufficiency
The preceding sections have established that persistent interaction requires shared invariants,
and that record authority depends on the preservation of exclusion relations under interaction.
These results, however, do not yet specify what is required for an ontology to function as a single
world-description rather than a collection of locally coherent domains. That distinction becomes
decisive once closure is considered.
An ontology is closed if all interaction, persistence, and record formation occur within a single
self-contained domain of admissibility. No appeal is made to external enforcement, privileged
observers, or auxiliary structures outside the ontology itself. In such a setting, any constraint that
acquires authority must do so entirely through relations internal to the ontology.
Closure therefore raises a stricter question: not merely whether invariants exist, but whether
they are sufficient to coordinate constraint participation across the whole. Invariant sufficiency
concerns whether the set of shared invariants operative within an ontology is adequate to sup-
port globally authoritative interaction. Local interactions may be stabilized by distinct invari-
ants—emergent, approximate, or regime-specific—without difficulty. However, if those invariants
cannot be mutually related, then constraint authority becomes fragmented. Records formed un-
der one invariant regime cease to bind admissibility under another. Interaction remains locally
meaningful but globally incoherent.
In a closed ontology, such fragmentation is not a benign feature. If invariant regimes are
mutually incommensurable, then the ontology decomposes into effectively independent constraint
domains. Each may sustain persistence internally, but none can claim authority across the whole.
From within any one domain, other domains appear either opaque or irrelevant; from the standpoint

===== PAGE 62 =====
of the ontology as a whole, there is no unified admissibility structure. Worldhood dissolves into
partition.
Invariant sufficiency therefore requires more than the mere presence of shared invariants. It
requires that all invariants which participate in persistent interaction be jointly admissible—that
is, capable of being situated within a single invariant framework that preserves constraint authority
across domains. This does not demand a single primitive invariant, nor a reduction to a unique
variable. It requires only that invariant regimes not be mutually exclusive in their constraint
implications.
This condition is structural rather than metaphysical. It does not specify what the sufficient
invariant framework must be, how it is realized, or at what scale it operates. It specifies only what
failure looks like: an ontology in which constraint authority cannot compose globally cannot sustain
a coherent account of persistence, identity, or record authority across its full descriptive scope.
Quantum Coherence Theory stops at this boundary. It identifies invariant sufficiency as a
necessary condition for closure, but does not attempt to determine whether any particular ontol-
ogy satisfies it. The task of assessing sufficiency—of determining whether an ontology’s invariant
structure genuinely supports global admissibility without fragmentation—lies beyond QCT’s ex-
planatory role.
What QCT establishes is the limit: without invariant sufficiency, closure fails. And where
closure fails, no description can claim to represent a world rather than a collection of compatible
but disconnected structures.
9.6 Viability as Reducibility to Shared Invariants
Progressively throughout this work, the structural requirements for persistence, interaction,
and record authority have been established. Constraint enforces admissibility. Invariance enables
persistence. Shared invariance enables interaction. Where interaction produces durable records
or stabilized exclusions, those effects are authoritative only insofar as the participating structures
remain jointly answerable to invariant constraints. These results have been established without
appeal to interpretation, probability, or external selection.
A further consequence now becomes unavoidable. If a system of interacting structures is to
remain viable as a single world-description—capable of sustaining persistence, interaction, and
record authority across its full domain—then all constraint-relevant invariants that participate in
those processes must be mutually reducible. Reducibility here does not mean identity or eliminative
reduction.
It means that invariants must be translatable in a way that preserves admissibility, exclusion,
and re-identification across interaction. Without such reducibility, persistence fragments into locally
coherent but globally incompatible domains.
Viability, in this sense, is not an additional physical property layered onto an ontology. It is
a structural condition on whether constraint can remain authoritative across the entirety of what
the ontology claims to describe. An ontology may permit local persistence, localized records, or
sector-relative stability while still failing to be viable as a unified world-description. Global viability
requires that no interaction-authoritative invariant exist in isolation from the others that govern
the same descriptive boundary.
This condition follows directly from the definition of record authority. A record constrains
future interaction only if its exclusions remain meaningful across subsequent interactions. That
meaningfulness depends on the ability of later systems to remain answerable to the same invariant

structure, either directly or through invariant-preserving translation. Where invariants are not
mutually reducible, record authority cannot propagate. History becomes parochial, persistence
becomes compartmentalized, and interaction loses global enforceability.
Quantum Coherence Theory therefore yields a precise criterion for viability: a system is viable
as a closed world-description if and only if all invariants that participate in persistent interaction
are reducible within a single constraint framework sufficient to enforce admissibility globally. This
criterion is structural rather than metaphysical. It does not specify the nature of the invariant, its
origin, or its ultimate form. It specifies only what must hold if persistence, interaction, and record

At this point, the internal work of Quantum Coherence Theory is complete. QCT has identified
the minimal structural conditions required for persistence, interaction, and information to function
as physically authoritative features of a world. It has also identified the point at which those con-
ditions exceed the scope of a theory concerned solely with constraint and invariance. Determining
whether a given ontology satisfies the criterion of global viability—whether its invariants are in fact
reducible in the required sense—is no longer a question of coherence theory alone.
What emerges from QCT, then, is not a unification, but a boundary. On one side lies the internal
logic of constraint, invariance, and admissibility. On the other lies the evaluation of entire world-
descriptions for their capacity to sustain those structures without fragmentation. That evaluation
requires a framework whose primary task is not to describe interaction, but to assess whether a
world can continue to exist as a world.
9.7 What This Claim Does Not Assert
The viability criterion established in the preceding section is structural in scope and limited
in intent. It specifies necessary conditions for global persistence within an interacting ontology.
It does not, by itself, resolve questions of ontology selection, metaphysical priority, or ultimate
explanation. To avoid misinterpretation, several clarifications are required.
First, this claim does not assert that all invariants reduce to a single law, quantity, or equation.
Reducibility to shared invariants is not eliminative unification. It does not demand that diverse
constraint structures collapse into one description, nor that higher-level invariants disappear into
lower-level formalisms. The requirement is only that invariant frameworks admit mappings that
preserve exclusion relations and re-identification conditions. Structural compatibility, not descrip-
tive uniformity, is the criterion.
Second, this claim does not assert that local descriptions lacking such reducibility are false
or meaningless. Many frameworks may remain valid within restricted domains, under limited
interaction, or for finite durations. The viability criterion distinguishes global persistence from local
adequacy. A description may succeed empirically while failing to function as a world-description.
This distinction concerns scope, not correctness.
Third, this claim does not assert that a viable ontology must be complete, closed under all future
discovery, or immune to revision. Reducibility to shared invariants concerns the preservation of
constraint authority under interaction, not the exhaustion of explanatory content. A world may
remain viable while its descriptions evolve, provided that new constraints remain translatable into
the shared invariant framework.
Fourth, this claim does not assert that shared invariants are directly observable, simple, or
human-interpretable. The existence of a shared invariant framework is a structural necessity in-
ferred from persistence, not a guarantee of epistemic accessibility. The theory does not presume

===== PAGE 64 =====
that such invariants are discoverable in final form, nor that they can be expressed without approx-
imation or mediation.
Fifth, this claim does not assert that quantum coherence alone determines world viability.
Quantum Coherence Theory establishes conditions under which constraint accumulation, interac-
tion, and record authority are possible. It does not specify how these conditions are realized across
scales, nor how competing invariant regimes are reconciled. The theory identifies a boundary, not
a construction.
Finally, this claim does not assert inevitability. It does not guarantee that any given ontol-

promise. Where reducibility to shared invariants fails, fragmentation, parochial persistence, or on-

These clarifications delimit the scope of Quantum Coherence Theory. The theory establishes
what must be true if a world is to persist as a world. It does not declare which worlds do so, nor how
such persistence is achieved in practice. Those questions require criteria beyond local coherence
and constraint behavior.
With this boundary in place, Quantum Coherence Theory reaches explanatory closure. It has
identified the structural conditions under which interaction, record authority, and persistence can
coexist. What remains is not further explanation of quantum behavior, but evaluation of world-
descriptions against the viability criterion now established.

10 Explanatory Closure
Section X closes Quantum Coherence Theory on its own terms by clarifying what QCT ex-
plains and, just as importantly, what it deliberately does not. QCT is shown to be explanatory

10.1 What QCT Explains
This section addresses a specific explanatory gap in quantum foundations: how classical def-
initeness, stable records, and shared macroscopic structure arise without appealing to observer-
dependent collapse, global selection rules, or unreduced outcome proliferation. The scope is inten-
tionally narrow. The aim is not to explain why the universe exists, why its laws take the form
they do, or why particular initial conditions obtain. The aim is to explain how, given physical
interaction and constraint aggregation, a single macroscopic continuation becomes authoritative.
Within this scope, collapse is treated as a structural phenomenon rather than a primitive
event. It is not a stochastic jump, a mental act, or a global selection rule. It is the physical
narrowing of admissibility that occurs when interactions generate sufficiently dense, redundant,
and stabilizing constraints. When incompatible configurations can no longer jointly persist under
the accumulated constraint network, admissibility is reduced. What remains is not selected by
preference or probability, but enforced by physical incompatibility.
This framing clarifies why decoherence alone is insufficient for definiteness. Decoherence ex-
plains the dynamical autonomy of alternatives: why they cease to interfere. It does not, by itself,
explain why only one macroscopic configuration governs future interaction. That additional step
requires recognizing that once records are physically stabilized, they function as constraints on sub-
sequent dynamics. Incompatible macroscopic continuations are not merely non-interfering; they are
rendered inadmissible. Collapse, in this sense, is the lawful consequence of constraint aggregation,
not an interpretive supplement.
The same structure explains record authority. A record is not merely a correlation or a narrative
label for what happened. It is a physical structure whose presence alters what can happen next.
Downstream interactions depend on it counterfactually. That dependence is objective and observer-
independent. Record authority is therefore explained by constraint enforcement rather than by
epistemic access, self-location, or perspective.
Classical robustness follows naturally. Classical behavior persists not because it is favored,
optimal, or typical, but because it occupies regions of state space that remain admissible under
dense interaction. As constraint density increases, incompatible configurations are progressively
excluded. Classical regularities emerge as stable survivors of constraint pruning, not as outcomes
selected by global rules or anthropic filtering.
Crucially, this account does not commit to a full ontology of worlds. It does not claim that only
one possible universe exists, nor does it deny the mathematical existence of alternative solutions
or histories. Its claim is conditional and physical: once constraint conditions sufficient for record
stabilization obtain, admissibility collapses to a single macroscopic continuation. This claim is
local, interaction-based, and in principle testable through its implications for stability, redundancy,
and exclusion.

explain why constraint structures exist in the first place. It does not explain why the universe

For this reason, the framework should be read as a test case rather than a world theory. It

10.2 Constraint Based Collapse
In Quantum Coherence Theory, collapse is not defined as a fundamental dynamical interrup-
tion, nor as the literal elimination of representable alternatives. It is defined as a transition in
admissibility: the point at which accumulated physical constraints render macroscopic continua-
tion (up to a specified coarse-graining C) conditionally singular with respect to future interactions.
At this point, all but one candidate macroscopic continuation lack any physically realizable robust
extension that preserves the stabilized records under the instantiated constraint network within
tolerance.
Decoherence explains the suppression of interference between macroscopically distinct configu-
rations. Quantum Darwinism explains why certain states become redundantly recorded and effec-
tively objective within dynamically autonomous sectors. Neither, by itself, explains why a particu-
lar macroscopic record comes to function as an authoritative constraint on what can happen next.
Quantum Coherence Theory addresses this gap by defining collapse in terms of constraint-based
admissibility rather than representational exclusivity, epistemic update, or branch deletion.
A macroscopic continuation is admissible relative to a given coarse-graining, tolerance, and
constraint network if there exists a physically realizable extension of the present state in which
all stabilized records are preserved under the actual couplings, boundary conditions, and noise
environment the system admits. Admissibility is therefore a matter of dynamical accessibility
and robustness, not of description, awareness, or perspective. A continuation that cannot be
extended without destabilizing existing records or amplifying error beyond tolerance is inadmissible,
regardless of whether it remains mathematically representable.
For clarity, admissibility may be treated schematically as a context-relative set. Given a coarse-
graining C, a stability toleranceϵ, and a constraint network D, one may consider the set A(C,ϵ,
D) of coarse-grained macroscopic continuations that admit physically realizable robust extensions
preserving the stabilized record structure under D withinϵ. Collapse, in this sense, occurs when
A(C,ϵ, D) becomes singleton up to tolerance. This singularity is approximate and scale-relative.
It is defined by robustness criteria, not by exact microstate uniqueness.
A record, in this context, is not an arbitrary correlation. It is a macroscopic correlation pat-
tern redundantly instantiated across degrees of freedom that are sufficiently decoupled to persist
under expected perturbations. A record is stabilized when it remains robust under the actual cou-
plings and noise the system admits. Stabilized records function as constraints because subsequent
interactions must remain consistent with them in a counterfactual sense.

permitted interventions systematically alters downstream constraints and records, while attempts

Authority is therefore a physical property of constraint integration, not a function of observation,
awareness, or representational status.
Within this framework, thermodynamic irreversibility aligns naturally with increasing con-

cies grow, and admissible macroscopic continuations narrow. What is conventionally described
as entropy increase corresponds, at the macroscopic level, to the progressive restriction of future
admissibility imposed by accumulated constraints. This does not redefine entropy or reduce ther-
modynamics to QCT. It clarifies why entropy increase and irreversibility track the same structural
phenomenon: once constraints are instantiated and redundantly enforced, restoring earlier admis-
sibility would require unrealizable coordination across the constraint network.
Crucially, Quantum Coherence Theory does not assert that incompatible decohered configura-
tions are dynamically erased, nor does it require that a global mathematical state reduce to a single
term. The claim is narrower and structural. Once collapse occurs, only one macroscopic record re-
tains counterfactual authority conditional on the realized constraint network. Other representable
alternatives lack the capacity to govern future interactions within that context.
This definition is neutral with respect to deeper ontology. Under Everett-style interpretations,
collapse is sector-relative: conditional singular admissibility holds within each dynamically au-
tonomous sector. Under single-world realism, an additional ontic selector is required to determine
which admissible continuation is realized. Quantum Coherence Theory does not supply that selec-
tor. It specifies what collapse must amount to structurally in order to explain persistence, record
authority, and shared classical structure.
Collapse is not probabilistic selection. Probability may characterize distributions over micro-
conditions or over which constraint networks become instantiated, but probability does not itself
enforce admissibility. In Quantum Coherence Theory, narrowing is achieved through constraint
enforcement. How probabilistic regularities arise from this process is a separate question and is not
presupposed by the definition of collapse.
There is no universal collapse threshold or fixed timescale. The onset, scale, and sharpness of
collapse depend on constraint density, redundancy, coupling strength, and error tolerance in the
physical context. Collapse is therefore emergent, context-sensitive, and progressive rather than
instantaneous or fundamental.
In summary, collapse in Quantum Coherence Theory is the transition to conditional singular
admissibility: the point at which, given the stabilized record structure actually instantiated, only
one macroscopic continuation remains capable of exerting counterfactual control over future in-
teractions. It is not decoherence, not epistemic update, not branch deletion, and not a primitive
postulate. It is the moment at which constraint aggregation renders macroscopic authority unique
within a realized constraint network, thereby grounding persistence and shared classical structure.
10.3 Shared Classicality Without Selection
The persistence of a shared classical world is often treated as a mystery that demands resolution
by selection. Why this outcome rather than another. Why one world rather than many. Why

agreement rather than fragmentation. Across interpretations, this pressure repeatedly reappears
as an appeal to chance, preference, observation, optimization, or purpose. Each of these attempts
seeks to explain shared reality by choosing among alternatives.
Within a constraint-first framework, no such choice is required. What must be explained is not
why a particular macroscopic outcome is selected, but why macroscopic structure exhibits stable,
intersubjective continuity across interactions. That continuity does not arise because a world is
chosen. It arises because constraint-enforced records possess unique authority over what can happen
next.
Once collapse is understood as conditional singular admissibility, shared classicality follows
directly. When a macroscopic record becomes stabilized, it does not merely describe what has
occurred. It becomes a physical constraint on subsequent interactions. Any future process that
remains dynamically admissible must preserve consistency with that record within tolerance. In-
teractions that fail to do so do not propagate as robust extensions of the world. They are not
forbidden by fiat. They are rendered dynamically inadmissible by the constraint network itself.
Because constraint authority is physical rather than epistemic, it applies uniformly across sub-
systems. Observers do not coordinate their perceptions in order to agree on reality. They encounter
the same constraint structure. Agreement is enforced not by communication or consensus, but by
the fact that all admissible interactions are already constrained to be mutually consistent with the
same stabilized records.
This is why shared classicality does not depend on observation. An observer is simply a sub-
system interacting with an already constrained environment. Whether or not a record is observed,
it continues to exert authority. Whether or not it is represented, it continues to shape what can
persist. The world does not wait for acknowledgment in order to remain coherent. Disagreement,
illusion, and error are not excluded by this account. Local subsystems may encode false or incom-
plete correlations. But such deviations lack authority. They do not propagate stably across the
broader constraint network. They are overwritten, damped, or confined to contexts where they
cannot govern future macroscopic structure. What persists at scale is not belief, but constraint
compatibility.
This also explains why shared classicality does not require the elimination of alternatives. Rep-
resentationally distinct configurations may remain mathematically describable. Decohered sectors
may remain dynamically autonomous. What matters is not representability, but authority. Only
those macroscopic records that are compatible with the realized constraint network can function
as sources of future constraints. Alternatives that lack this capacity may exist as descriptions, but
they cannot act.
In this sense, the classical world is singular not because it is uniquely selected, but because
constraint authority is uniquely instantiated. There is no global preference. There is no ranking of
outcomes. There is only the progressive narrowing of what remains dynamically viable as records
accumulate and stabilize.
This account also avoids teleology. Persistence does not occur because a configuration is optimal,
successful, or favored. It occurs because it remains admissible under the constraints that exist.
Survival is not achievement. It is compatibility. Apparent regularity reflects constraint satisfaction,
not goal fulfillment.
Nor does shared classicality require ontological commitment beyond the structure already de-
scribed. Under sector-relative interpretations, constraint authority is local to each dynamically
autonomous context. Under single-world realism, an additional fact must determine which context
is realized. In either case, the mechanism by which shared classicality arises is the same. QCT
specifies the structure that any such account must respect, without supplying a selector of its own.
The world appears unified because it is constrained to be so.

Once stabilized records possess authority, divergence cannot scale. Branching may occur rep-
resentationally. Possibilities may proliferate mathematically. But macroscopic structure remains
shared because only one continuation remains capable of governing what follows within a real-
ized constraint network. The classical world is therefore not imposed, selected, or declared. It is
enforced. Not by observers. Not by purpose. Not by probability. But by the accumulation of
constraints that render all but one macroscopic continuation dynamically inadmissible as a robust
extension. This is what it means for a world to persist.
10.4 What QCT Leaves Open by Design
The preceding sections have not proposed a mechanism for choosing outcomes. They have
instead identified a structural condition that any account of persistence, record authority, and
shared classical structure must satisfy. Collapse, as defined here, is not an event appended to
dynamics, nor a metaphysical declaration about what exists. It is a diagnostic boundary: the
point at which accumulated constraints render macroscopic continuation conditionally singular
with respect to future interaction.
This reframing shifts the explanatory task. Traditional debates ask how outcomes are selected
from among alternatives. A constraint-first account shows that selection is not the operative macro-
scopic phenomenon. What matters is whether stabilized records acquire the capacity to function
as authoritative constraints on what can happen next. Where they do, persistence follows. Where
they do not, classical structure fails to form—regardless of how many alternative outcomes remain
representable in principle.
Seen this way, collapse is not a solution to the measurement problem. It is a criterion for
ruling out explanations that cannot, even in principle, support persistence under interaction. Any
framework that leaves incompatible macroscopic continuations equally admissible after record sta-
bilization fails this criterion, because it leaves record authority physically ungrounded. Frameworks
that appeal instead to epistemic update, global optimization, or irreducible selection also fail—not
because they are incoherent, but because they relocate authority into something other than con-
straint: belief, preference, luck, or purpose.
The consequence is subtle but decisive. Once constraint authority is established, the question
“why this outcome rather than another?” loses its force. The correct question becomes: which
structures are capable of governing future admissibility? Classical history is not chosen; it is
enforced. Agreement is not coordinated; it is compelled. Error is not eliminated; it is rendered
non-authoritative.
Nothing in this account privileges a particular microphysical ontology. The constraint-based
criterion applies equally to unitary, stochastic, hidden-variable, or sector-relative interpretations.
What it excludes are not theories as such, but explanatory moves: those that attempt to recover
persistence without providing a way for records to become binding constraints on subsequent in-
teraction.
This boundary also clarifies the status of operational frameworks that treat coherence as a quan-
tifiable resource within controlled regimes. Resource-theoretic approaches, for example, presuppose
environments in which coherence persists long enough to be counted, converted, or expended. QCT
neither competes with nor subsumes such frameworks. It addresses a prior condition they assume
but do not explain: the structural circumstances under which coherence can acquire durability and
authority under interaction, rather than evaporating immediately upon coupling.
This diagnostic role is intentional. The aim is not to replace existing formalisms, but to clarify
what any viable world-description must already accomplish if it is to explain why anything like
69

a shared, stable world persists at all. Collapse, in this sense, is not the answer to the story of
classicality. It is the point at which non-viable stories can no longer continue.
•The Viability Test: A framework passes the viability test only if, given its own admissible dy-
namics, stabilized records automatically acquire authority as constraints on future interaction.
Where authority requires supplementation—observer, chance, optimization, or decree—the
framework fails. Viability demands that persistence be self-enforcing through constraint, not
externally justified by selection.
•Contrast Case: Record stabilization without authority yields worlds that do not cohere. De-
coherence occurs, records form, but nothing binds them. Each stabilization event becomes a
local correlation lacking power to govern subsequent admissibility. Classical structure frag-
ments into isolated pockets, each internally stable but globally non-persistent. A world of
that kind can be written, but not lived in.
What remains is not to decide between outcomes, but to determine which theoretical struc-
tures can sustain constraint authority without supplementation. That question does not belong to
collapse itself. It belongs to viability.
10.5 Deferred Role of Mathematical Formalism
Quantum Coherence Theory (Sections 1-10) intentionally refrains from introducing a fixed
mathematical formalism in this work. This decision is neither a denial of formalizability nor a
claim that QCT operates outside mathematical description. It is a scope choice. The present
framework is concerned with identifying structural relations that govern persistence, admissibility,
and stabilization across regimes, prior to committing to domain-specific representations.
Mathematical formalism is not excluded by QCT. On the contrary, multiple formalisms may
be appropriate depending on the system under consideration, including Hilbert-space dynamics,
information-theoretic measures, dynamical systems models, or other constraint-based represen-
tations. Premature commitment to a single formal language would risk conflating explanatory
structure with a particular implementation.
QCT therefore treats mathematical constructs as downstream instruments rather than foun-
dational primitives. Formal models are expected to arise where explanatory pressure demands
precision, prediction, or system-specific instantiation. Their absence here reflects the aim of estab-
lishing cross-domain explanatory coherence rather than delivering a closed technical formalism.
This stance preserves both rigor and flexibility. It allows QCT to remain compatible with
existing mathematical frameworks while avoiding artificial restriction at the level of principle. For-
malization is not rejected; it is deferred until necessity, rather than convention, determines its form.
10.6 Empirical Commitments, Distinguishers, and Fal-

This work is not introduced as a modification of quantum dynamics, nor as a competing collapse
model. It does not posit new forces, additional variables, or departures from unitary evolution at

makes distinguishable claims relative to other explanatory frameworks, and specifies the conditions
under which the theory would be undermined.
Empirical Commitments
QCT commits to the following empirically grounded claims:
•Decoherence and Classical Emergence Track Interaction Structure, not Intrinsic Scale: Loss of
observable quantum coherence correlates with the accumulation, alignment, and redundancy
of system–environment couplings, rather than with mass, size, duration, or complexity in
isolation. Experiments in interferometry, mesoscopic systems, superconducting circuits, and
optomechanics are interpreted as confirming this structural dependence.
•Classical Determinism Emerges as a Limiting Behavioral Regime: Deterministic classical
descriptions become effective when constraint density is sufficiently high to restrict admissible
evolution to narrow, stable manifolds. Determinism is therefore contingent and context-
sensitive, not fundamental.
•Pointer States and Classical Stability Reflect Constraint Compatibility: The basis in which
classical behavior appears is determined by the dominant coupling structure. Configura-
tions that persist are those least disrupted by aggregated constraints, not those selected by
observation or imposed collapse rules.
•Quantum Behavior Can Re-Emerge when Constraints Are Relaxed: Isolation, cooling, shield-
ing, dynamical decoupling, and engineered environments can broaden admissibility and re-
store quantum coherence. Such reversals are not anomalies, but direct consequences of re-
duced constraint density.
These commitments align QCT with the empirical record while assigning explanatory priority
to constraint geometry and interaction structure.
Distinguishers
QCT is distinguishable from other approaches in how it organizes and interprets these facts:
•Against Observer-Centric Accounts: QCT attributes neither decoherence nor classical emer-
gence to observation, measurement updates, or epistemic intervention. Observers are treated
as physical subsystems subject to the same constraints as any other.
•Against Intrinsic Collapse Models: QCT does not posit stochastic collapse triggers, mass
thresholds, or gravity-induced reductions. Apparent collapse is understood as the exhaustion
of joint admissibility under constraint aggregation, compatible with globally unitary dynam-
ics.
•Beyond Descriptive Decoherence: While standard decoherence theory describes how inter-
ference is suppressed, QCT advances a unifying structural principle: admissibility reduction
under accumulated constraint explains why decoherence, pointer selection, classical determin-
ism, and scale dependence exhibit the patterns they do.

•Interpretation-Neutral: QCT brackets questions of single-outcome realization, branching on-

not the metaphysical status of unrealized alternatives.
Falsifiers and Limits
Because QCT does not introduce new dynamics, its falsification conditions concern structural
failures, not parameter mismatches. The framework would be undermined if any of the following
were reliably observed:
•Sustained Macroscopic Coherence under High, Heterogeneous Constraint Density: If systems
exhibiting dense, redundant, and incompatible environmental coupling nonetheless main-
tained long-lived, recoverable quantum interference, the central claim that constraint aggre-
gation enforces admissibility reduction would fail.
•Classical Determinism Emerging without Constraint Saturation: If deterministic classical
behavior were shown to arise robustly in regimes lacking strong coupling, redundancy, or
environmental embedding, QCT’s account of determinism as a constrained limit would be
incorrect.
•Decoherence Rates or Pointer Bases Independent of Coupling Structure: If experimental

system properties rather than interaction geometry, the constraint-first interpretation would
be invalidated.
•A Confirmed Need for Intrinsic Collapse Dynamics: Empirical validation of non-unitary col-
lapse mechanisms operating independently of interaction structure would place QCT outside
the correct explanatory class.
Scope Clarification
QCT does not claim to derive the Born rule, resolve the ontology of outcomes, or replace
existing quantum formalism. Its claim is narrower and more structural: constraint aggregation
is sufficient to explain the emergence of classical stability, determinism, and predictability from
quantum permissiveness. Any viable interpretation or extension of quantum mechanics must be
compatible with this constraint-based structure.
In this sense, QCT is not an alternative to quantum theory, but a refinement of its explanatory
architecture. It identifies where permissiveness ends, where stability begins, and why classical
behavior appears not as a separate law, but as the constrained limit of quantum possibility.
10.7 Why QCT Is Explanatory, Not Instrumental
This framework is not introduced as a rival predictive formalism, nor as an auxiliary rule for
generating outcomes. It does not modify the quantum calculus, introduce new probabilities, or
specify procedures for selecting results. Its role is explanatory rather than instrumental. The
question it addresses is prior to prediction: what must be structurally true of a world for stable
records, persistence through interaction, and a shared classical domain to be possible at all.

Instrumental approaches focus on prediction. They ask how to compute probabilities, expec-
tation values, or outcome distributions. The concern here is different. It asks what makes such
computations interpretable as describing a single, persisting world rather than merely correlating
reports. The target is not operational success, but explanatory adequacy at the level of world-
conditions.
This distinction matters because predictive accuracy alone does not secure ontological coherence.
A formalism may reproduce observed statistics while leaving unanswered why macroscopic records
are binding rather than ephemeral, why facts persist rather than fragment, or why intersubjective
agreement does not dissolve under continued interaction. The gap appears wherever outcome talk
is possible without an account of why stabilized structure constrains what can happen next.
Many interpretive strategies attempt to bridge this gap by adding selection rules: stochas-

Even when such strategies reproduce empirical frequencies, they often relocate the explanatory
burden rather than resolve it. They explain which outcome is registered, but not why stabilized
macroscopic structure enforces persistence rather than remaining a passive correlation.
The constraint-first approach reframes the burden entirely. Definiteness is not treated as an
outcome-selection mechanism layered onto dynamics, but as a condition on admissibility. Once sta-
bilized records exist, admissibility narrows. Incompatible macroscopic continuations are excluded
not by preference, probability, or observation, but because they cannot be extended as robust con-
tinuations under the realized constraint network. Definiteness, on this view, is not injected into
dynamics; it emerges when record-stabilized structure acquires authority over future interaction.
Because this analysis operates at the level of structural preconditions, it is deliberately silent on
several questions often treated as foundational. It does not derive probabilities, specify sampling
rules, or choose between ontological interpretations. Those tasks are instrumental. Probability,
observation, and outcome talk presuppose persistence and record authority; they cannot replace
them as foundations.
For this reason, the framework should not be read as an interpretation in the usual sense. It
does not propose a picture of what ultimately exists. It specifies what any viable world-description
must already satisfy if it is to explain why a shared, stable classical world persists at all. Its
contribution is not a mechanism, but a criterion: an account of the structural conditions under
which facts can become binding rather than merely reported.
In this sense, the contribution is explanatory rather than instrumental. It clarifies the de-
pendency order between persistence, record authority, and the calculational machinery of physical
theory. What remains—how different ontologies realize or supplement these conditions—is not a
question of collapse or selection. It is a question of viability.

11 Summary and Synthesis of QCT
(Sections 1-10)
Quantum Coherence Theory (QCT) does not introduce new dynamical mechanisms, nor does it
propose revisions to quantum formalism. Its contribution is explanatory and diagnostic. It clarifies
what coherence must mean if quantum theory is to account for persistence, record authority, and
shared classical structure in an interacting world.
At the experimental level, QCT aligns naturally with results across matter-wave interferome-
try, engineered qubit platforms, and decoherence studies by reframing coherence as a property that
scales with environmental interaction rather than intrinsic isolation. The persistence of superpo-
sition is shown not to depend primarily on size or energy, but on the structure and accumulation
of constraints through which which-alternative information is suppressed, redirected, or rendered
non-authoritative. Coherence fails not because systems grow large, but because unconstrained
interaction channels overwhelm stabilizing structure.
This reframing extends the definition of coherence beyond internal phase consistency to include
interactive survivability. A state is coherent not merely when its internal relations are well-defined,
but when those relations persist under coupling and acquire authority as constraints on future
interaction. From this perspective, the quantum–classical transition is no longer a regression away
from quantum behavior, but a forward progression toward higher constraint density and stabilized
admissibility. Classicality is not imposed by measurement or selection; it is enforced by constraint.
Within this framework, energy is properly situated as a constraint regime rather than an ex-
planatory primitive. Energy budgets, dissipation rates, and thermal effects remain indispensable
to practice, but they function as bookkeeping measures of the cost of maintaining structure, not
as the source of coherence or persistence themselves. Structural organization determines whether
coherence survives; energy tracks what it takes to sustain that organization.
QCT also demystifies non-locality without invoking action at a distance. Locality appears as
a high-constraint satisfaction regime in which admissible interactions are tightly restricted. When
constraint structures span extended systems, correlations can remain binding without localized
mediation. What appears non-local is, in this sense, globally constrained.
Central to this account is the explicit identification of record authority as a necessary structural
feature of persistence. Standard treatments often assume that records, once formed, persist simply
because dynamics continue. QCT shows this assumption to be insufficient. In purely dynamical
systems—particularly at the quantum level—stabilized correlations would remain locally descriptive
but globally violable under subsequent interaction. That this does not occur indicates that record
authority is not an emergent convenience but a requirement. Once records stabilize, they constrain
future admissibility. The past is not merely remembered; it is enforced.
This enforcement implies the necessity of shared invariant structure. Persistence across inter-
action requires global consistency, and global consistency requires invariant constraints respected
across domains. QCT does not posit the nature of such invariants as primitives; it demonstrates
their necessity diagnostically. Worlds lacking shared invariant structure cannot support authorita-
tive records and therefore cannot sustain shared classical history. The implications of this require-
ment are explored more fully in Viable Worlds Theory.
From these considerations follow QCT’s closure conditions. The theory does not privilege uni-
tary, stochastic, hidden-variable, or sector-relative interpretations. What it excludes are not theo-
ries as such, but explanatory moves: those that attempt to recover persistence without providing

13 Hypothesis Under Stress
Section 13 subjects the Structural Admissibility Hypothesis of§12.3 to systematic stress under
quantitative incompatibility. The aim is not yet to prove a rigidity theorem, but to make precise
what would count as a robust violation and to clarify which ingredients are genuinely dynamical
or generic versus purely kinematic.
We proceed in three moves. First (§13.1), we harden the hypothesis by fixing an explicit
parameter space, topology, and continuity assumptions, and we formulate a falsification criterion
in terms of open neighborhoods. Second (§13.2), we present an adversarial engineering template
isolating what would be required to realizeDSRδ(X,Z;t) for incompatible observables while keeping
ρS(t) at positive trace distance fromC XZ . Third (§13.3), we drop genericity assumptions entirely
and analyzeDSR δ(X,Z;t) as a fixed-state predicate, introducing a multiplicity-refined variant to
track how obstruction strengthens with redundancy counts.

Class III: Engineered Multi-Channel Measurement Architectures
One may intentionally construct interactions in which distinct fragment subsets couple pre-

Class IV: Nonlocal or Global Couplings
If interaction operators act nonlocally across multiple fragments, the fragment decomposition
ceases to define dynamically independent record carriers. In such cases the notion of disjoint
fragment redundancy may lose operational meaning. Hypothesis 14.1 is therefore intended only for
fragment-local architectures preserving subsystem individuality.

finite-dimensional models. In pointer-dominated and random fragment-local regimes, disjoint si-

the geometric rigidity hypothesis. They do not derive it and do not strengthen it.

15 Construction and Attainability
Section 15 clarifies the distinction between logical attainability and structural genericity. The
constructions above demonstrate that strong two-task record behavior for incompatible observables
can be engineered under controlled, fresh-initialization architectures. In particular, tested-channel
redundancy forXandZcan be realized in separate runs without contradicting the entropic ob-
struction of§14.
However, such constructions do not by themselves establish disjoint simultaneous redundancy
(DSR) on a single unknown joint state. They require coordinated initialization, controlled schedul-
ing, or parameter tuning, and therefore occupy structured regions of parameter space rather than
representing typical fragment-local dynamics.
Accordingly,§15 does not refute the geometric rigidity hypothesis of§14; it calibrates it. En-
gineered attainability is compatible with the claim that DSR, when realized under fragment-local
product architectures, is structurally constrained and potentially nongeneric. The question of
whether DSR forces trace-distance proximity toC XZ remains conditional on Hypothesis 14.1 and
its possible quantitative refinements.
We now turn to results that are logically independent of rigidity and typicality. Section 16
establishes unconditional stability and distributed-erasure constraints for redundancy certification,

15.1 Fully Worked Toy Model: Engineered Tested-Cha-
nnel Redundancy (Two Tasks)
We give an explicit finite-dimensional construction showing that strong tested-channel redun-
dancy for two incompatible observables can be engineered under controlled initialization and sepa-
rate runs. In one experiment a disjoint fragment family reliably recordsX, and in another a disjoint
family reliably recordsZ. This does not constitute the predicateDSR δ(X,Z;t) (Eq. (12.15)) for
a single unknown joint state evolving once. Rather, it provides an existence witness for two-task
tested-channel redundancy under separate controlled initializations, and calibrates the degree of
fine-tuning required to achieve it. The construction therefore clarifies the distinction between logi-
cal attainability and structural typicality.
15.1.1 Scope and Intent (Tested-Channel Redundancy)
The construction in this subsection analyzes tested-channel redundancy. The system is freshly
initialized in product form, evolved under a fixed Hamiltonian, and redundancy is evaluated on
the resulting fragment records obtained in each controlled run. This notion differs from disjoint
simultaneous redundancy (DSR), which concerns a single fixed joint state and L¨ uders-conditioned
ensembles defined on that state.
The present construction does not claim to instantiateDSR δ(X,Z;t) for a single unknown joint
input undergoing one uninterrupted evolution. Rather, it demonstrates the logical attainability of
tested-channel redundancy under fresh initialization. Accordingly, no claim is made that a single
unknown input state evolving once under the Hamiltonian produces simultaneous fragment-level
records of incompatible observables.

Purpose
We present a concrete finite-dimensional construction that realizes tested-channel redundancy
(two tasks) in an explicitly engineered, non-generic way. Specifically, two disjoint fragment families
redundantly encode their respective observables under separate controlled initializations. We do not
claim that a single unknown input undergoing one uninterrupted evolution produces simultaneous
records of incompatible observables.
Can disjoint fragments carry near-complete records of two incompatible PVMs
under separately tested record channels, without asserting rigidity or generic
attainability?
The answer here is “yes” in a deliberately engineered sense. We construct a two-stage in-
strument and define two tested record channels, one per observable, on classical inputs. The result
establishes logical attainability together with a quantitative fine-tuning estimate. It does not assert
simultaneity for unknown inputs, nor any rigidity or genericity theorem.

15.1.5 Structural Fragility, Regime Separation, and Conclusion
The preceding construction establishes logical attainability of tested-channel redundancy (two
tasks). However, the admissible parameter region occupies vanishing measure as the discrimination
threshold tightens. For each observable, the admissible one-dimensional parameter region has half-
width Θ(√α/τ). Thus the total admissible measure in [−Λ,Λ] scales asO( √α) for fixed Λ. Because
theXandZconstraints separate in (λ X,λZ), the admissible region in the square [−Λ,Λ] 2 has area
O(α).
Asα→0, meaning near-complete distinguishability is required, the admissible region shrinks to
measure zero. Small perturbations larger thanO( √α/τ) generically destroy the tested-channel re-
dundancy condition. In this sense the construction is structurally fragile. It demonstrates existence
but not robustness.
This clarifies the regime distinction:
•Tested-channel redundancy (two tasks) is logically attainable under engineered, fine-tuned
parameters.
•It is not generic and is unstable under perturbation.
•Therefore the toy model does not contradict any structural obstruction to uniform or robust
realization of the predicateDSR δ(X,Z;t).
The model occupies a nongeneric, measure-suppressed regime within parameter space, while
the obstruction arguments apply to the robust regime.
Conclusion
This construction establishes logical attainability of the tested-channel redundancy notion
within a controlled engineered regime. It does not assert generic realization, dynamical inevitabil-
ity, or disjoint simultaneous redundancy for a single unknown input. The example is explicitly
non-generic and requires parameter tuning within bounded windows. Its purpose is structural: to
demonstrate compatibility and scaling behavior within a precisely specified parameter regime under
explicit dynamics.

16 Stability, Witness Approximation,
and Distributed Erasure
Section 16 is deliberately unconditional: it does not assume rigidity, typicality, or any special dy-
namics, but instead studies how redundancy certification behaves under arbitrary transformations
between joint states.
The first result is a forward stability statement. If a fragment certifies theδ-record predicate
with strict margin at a reference state, then sufficiently small subnormalized disturbance of the
conditioned fragment ensemble guarantees that the same finite-outcome witness continues to certify
aδ-record after perturbation, with controlled margin degradation. The second result shows that,
for the purposes of this stability analysis, finite-outcome near-optimal witnesses suffice; no infinite-
outcome optimization is required. The third result is a distributed erasure principle. If many
previously certified fragments lose record status under a state change, then many fragments must
have experienced super-threshold disturbance. Redundancy therefore converts large-scale record
loss into a multi-fragment disturbance requirement.
These statements are structural constraints on state-to-state transformations. They are com-
patible with, but logically independent of, the conjectural rigidity narrative of§§14–15.

16.4 Structural Interpretation and Scope
Throughout this subsection, “certified” means (δ,γ)-certified per Definition 16.1. All stability
and distributed-failure statements below apply only to fragments that satisfy this interior certifi-
cation condition at the reference state. No stability claim is made for boundaryδ-records without
strict margin.
Summary of Section 16
Sections 16.1–16.3 isolate two logically distinct structural phenomena governing fragment-level
accessibility certification within the interior, margin-certified regime. Section 16.2 supplies the
finite-outcome closure required to parameterize these statements in terms of finite alphabets.
First, Lemma 16.1 establishes local stability of margin-certified accessibility. If a fragment
certifiesXwith strict margin, then sufficiently small subnormalized perturbations of the conditioned
fragment ensemble preserve certification with degraded margin. Margins degrade continuously.
This is a finite-dimensional continuity statement based on classical entropy bounds together with
the certification assumption that a finite-outcome witness exists. Second, Theorem 16.2 establishes
a margin-conditioned distributed failure constraint. For fragments that are (δ,γk)-certified, removal
ofδ-record status frommsuch fragments requires exceeding the corresponding stability threshold
on at leastmfragments. Widespread certified-record loss therefore requires fragment-wise threshold
crossing across many fragments.

Taken together, these results describe local continuity properties of the margin-certified region
(for the fixed certification witnesses) in joint-state space. They identify both its interior stability
and the fragment-wise mechanism by which it can be exited. No global distance lower bound is
implied.
Local Structure of the Certified Regime
Certification with strict margin defines a neighborhood in which theδ-record predicate remains
valid with reduced margin. Small subnormalized perturbations of the fragment-conditioned en-
sembles do not immediately destroy accessibility for certified fragments. In this sense, the interior
certification regime is locally stable.
The logical direction is one-sided. The excess disturbance quantityD (k)

No lower bound follows merely from record preservation. Stability and erasure are asymmetric.
These statements concern transformations between joint states within finite-dimensional regimes.
They do not assume collapse, irreversibility, preferred-basis selection, or any geometric rigidity
hypothesis. They establish continuity of certification under controlled perturbation, not static
compatibility geometry.
Relation to Incompatibility Constraints
Section 14 established that incompatibility geometry constrains strong simultaneous accessi-
bility for incompatible observables. Section 16 establishes stability properties of margin-certified
fragment accessibility.
The present results do not prove that the Section 14 incompatibility obstruction itself is stable
under perturbation. They show only that when certified accessibility is present with strict mar-
gin, small fragment-level perturbations cannot immediately eliminate those certificates. Whether
incompatibility-based obstruction to disjoint strong accessibility inherits perturbative stability
requires additional structural bridges between entropic incompatibility bounds and accessible-
information certification. Such bridges are not established here.
Rigidity Program (Open Direction)
The results of§§15–16 establish stability of certification and fragmentwise distributed failure
constraints. They do not establish geometric rigidity of the joint state. The only rigidity hypothesis
adopted in this work appears in§14.3 and is stated as Hypothesis 14.1; it concerns the reduced
system stateρ S(t).
In particular, Hypothesis 14.1 posits trace-distance proximity ofρ S(t) to the compatibility set
CXZ underDSR δ(X,Z;t). No proximity of the full joint stateρ SE(t) to any structural class is
assumed. No argument in§§15–16 depends on joint-state geometric rigidity.

Noise Scaling and Quantitative Dependence
The stability thresholdsε∗,k depend explicitly on the alphabet size|X|, the witness outcome size

witnesses, these thresholds are explicit and finite-dimensional.
This makes the interior certification regime quantitatively analyzable under fragment growth,
increasing alphabet size, structured noise models, and redundancy scaling. Precise scaling laws are
not derived here, but the dependence is explicit.
Conceptual Boundary
Section 16 establishes:
•local stability of margin-certified accessibility,
•a fragment-wise distributed failure constraint,
•continuity properties of the certified interior regime.
The distributed constraint established in Theorem 16.2 is fragment-wise. It does not yield
a single global trace-distance lower bound between joint states without additional structural as-
sumptions. Full geometric rigidity, collapse claims, global compatibility structure, or stability of
incompatibility obstruction are not established here. The rigidity program remains open. What
has been established is precise and limited: margin-certified redundancy is locally stable under
controlled perturbations, and loss of certified redundancy at scale requires fragment-wise threshold
exceedance.

layer: within the margin-certified regime, certificates persist under small conditioned-ensemble
disturbance, and large-scale certificate loss requires many fragments to cross explicit disturbance
thresholds.
Section 17 consolidates these elements into a single regime-theoretic interpretation, clarifying
what is proved unconditionally, what is conditional (Hypothesis 14.1), and what is merely exhibited
by construction.

17.1 Interpretive Synthesis and Regime Closure
Sections 12–16 establish a precise structural separation between (i) incompatibility-driven ob-
struction under admissibility constraints and (ii) engineered coexistence achieved through non-
generic coordination of fragment interactions. We recall that§12 distinguishes the state-level pred-
icateDSR δ(X,Z;t), defined on a single joint stateρ SE(t) through L¨ uders-conditioned fragment
ensembles, from tested-channel redundancy verified across controlled runs with fresh initialization.
The constructive coexistence results developed in§15 concern this tested-channel notion and there-
fore do not by themselves witnessDSR δ(X,Z;t). Once this predicate distinction is kept explicit,
the apparent tension between rigidity and attainability dissolves as a separation of regimes within
a single formal architecture.
The entropic obstruction of§14.1 establishes a quantitative disjoint-predictability constraint
controlled by incompatibility. The step from the operational DSR predicate to trace-distance
proximity of the system marginal to the compatibility setC XZ is treated separately through the
rigidity strengthening (Hypothesis 14.1). The channel-structural analysis of§14.2 motivates that,
absent such strengthening, logical coexistence cannot be excluded in principle. The constructive toy
model of§15 and the finite-operational closure of§16 confirm that tested-channel redundancy (two
tasks) is attainable, but only within fine-tuned parameter windows whose total admissible measure
scales asO(α) as the discrimination threshold tightens. Moreover, the distributed-erasure principle
of§16 shows that eliminating certified records across many fragments requires correspondingly
many fragments to undergo super-threshold disturbance.
The resulting picture is explicitly regime-theoretic. Under generic admissible interactions, si-
multaneous stabilization of incompatible observables is structurally suppressed. Coexistence is
not logically impossible, but it requires coordination beyond typical admissible coupling and be-
comes detectable through the distributed disturbance accounting of§16 (Theorem 16.2). Rigidity
is therefore not a universal prohibition; it is a structural feature of the generic regime. This regime
separation resolves the interpretive concern motivating the earlier development. These results
therefore support a regime-theoretic view of classical persistence: in the generic admissible regime,
incompatible observables cannot simultaneously stabilize redundant records, while coexistence re-
mains possible only through finely coordinated interaction structures. Classical persistence does
not arise from ad hoc collapse, nor from unrestricted dynamical possibility. It emerges instead as
a structural consequence of admissibility, constraint propagation, and incompatibility geometry.
Accordingly, the formal program developed in Sections 12–16 does not overturn the interpretive
framework of Sections 1–11; it refines and sharpens it. Constraint, record stabilization, and shared

17.3 Scope Conditions
The structural results of Sections 13—16 are established under the following controlled assump-
tions.
Finite Dimensionality:All systems and fragments are finite dimensional. No claims are made
regarding infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, continuous spectra, or field-theoretic limits.
Fixed Fragment Decomposition:A definite fragment partitionS:E 1···E N is assumed. The
analysis does not address dynamically emergent or coarse-grained decompositions.
Rank-1 PVM Structure:Incompatibility bounds are derived for rank-1 PVMs. Extensions to
more general POVMs or degenerate observables require separate analysis.
Admissible Interaction Class:The rigidity and suppression results apply within the admissible
fragment-local interaction class defined in Sections 13—14. No universality is claimed beyond this
structural regime.
Trace-Norm Proximity:All closeness statements are formulated in trace distance. No stronger
norm equivalences or operator-topology claims are assumed.
Parameter-Space Typicality:Statements concerning genericity or measure suppression refer to
parameter space under bounded, absolutely continuous coefficient distributions. No global topo-
logical inevitability is asserted.
Within these bounds, the regime separation and structural dichotomy stated above hold. Be-
yond them, the program remains open.
17.4 Non-Claims
The present work does not assert the following.
No Universal No-Go Theorem:The Geometric Rigidity Hypothesis does not establish logical
impossibility of realizing the predicateDSR δ(X,Z;t) for incompatible observables. It postulates
suppression within a specified admissible generic regime.
No Dynamical Inevitability:The analysis does not claim that classicality emerges unavoidably
under all open-system dynamics. It identifies structural conditions under which stabilization is
enforced or suppressed.
No Collapse Mechanism:No dynamical collapse process, hidden-variable model, or modifica-
tion of unitary quantum theory is introduced or required.
No Universality Beyond the Admissible Class:Results are confined to the admissible
fragment-local interaction framework defined earlier. No claims are made regarding arbitrary ar-
chitectures or unconstrained couplings.

17.5 From Diagnostic Framing to Structural Necessity
The preceding sections establish regime separation, admissibility constraints, quantitative in-
compatibility bounds, constructive attainability, and distributed threshold structure within a con-
trolled open-system setting. These results stand independently of any further strengthening. How-
ever, the present framework also exposes several natural upgrade paths by which diagnostic prin-
ciples may be strengthened into structural theorems.
Record Authority as Structural Requirement:Record stabilization has functioned as a
persistence condition within the admissible framework. A structural strengthening would require
demonstrating that, under generic admissible coupling, failure of stabilized correlations to con-
strain successive interactions leads to breakdown of global consistency across multi-step admissible
evolution. Such a result would elevate record authority from interpretive organizing principle to
necessary condition for sustained admissibility. Establishing this would require a multi-time exten-
sion of the present single-time redundancy analysis together with quantitative propagation bounds.
Shared Invariant Necessity:Shared invariants have been introduced diagnostically as chan-
nels enabling mutual constraint participation. A tractable strengthening would prove that, in the
absence of at least one shared invariant channel, constraint propagation between subsystems gener-
ically fails to remain stable under composition. This direction appears feasible within the current
finite-dimensional admissible architecture, as it depends primarily on compatibility geometry and
fragment-channel structure rather than new dynamical assumptions.
Constraint-Enforced Classical Stability:The present results demonstrate suppression of re-
alization of the predicateDSR δ(X,Z;t) in the generic regime. A deeper strengthening would show
that, within admissible open-system interaction, classical pointer structures form the only dynam-
ically stable attractor class compatible with persistent constraint propagation. This extension is
substantially more demanding, as it requires explicit dynamical stability analysis rather than static
incompatibility bounds.
Global Constraint and Nonlocal Correlation:Nonlocal correlations have been treated in-
terpretively as compatibility relations within globally constrained admissibility. A structural re-
formulation would require embedding Bell-type correlation structure directly into the constraint-
compatibility framework. This represents a longer-range objective, as it demands integration of
admissibility geometry with explicit probabilistic and causal structure.

those concerning shared invariants, appear accessible within the current formal apparatus. Others

18 Unified Structural Posture of QCT
The manuscript has proceeded in two complementary movements. Sections 1—11 developed
an interpretive framework centered on constraint, record stabilization, admissibility, and shared
invariants. Sections 12—16 translated those organizing principles into explicit structural analysis,
isolating quantitative incompatibility bounds, regime separation, and constructive attainability
within a controlled interaction class.
These two movements are not independent layers. The interpretive framework anticipated
structural tension; the formal development reframed it as a regime-theoretic problem and bounded
its admissible realizations. What appeared at the outset as a conceptual concern regarding clas-
sical persistence and incompatibility has been recast as a statement about admissible interaction
geometry.
The interpretive principles of constraint propagation, record authority, and invariant participa-
tion are therefore not introduced as metaphysical postulates. Within the formal arc, they function
as organizing hypotheses whose structural content can be bounded, stress-tested, and delimited.
Conversely, the mathematical results do not stand as isolated technical statements; they sharpen
and discipline the interpretive posture by identifying precisely where generic suppression, engineered
coexistence, and structural rigidity arise.
The structural analysis developed in Sections 12—16 does not by itself constitute a catalogue
of empirical predictions. Rather, it establishes the regime constraints within which any predictive
extension of QCT must operate. The formal core functions as a constraint architecture: it delineates
what configurations are generically suppressed, what require fine coordination, and what remain
admissible under controlled interaction. In this sense, QCT is neither a collapse theory, nor a
hidden-variable proposal, nor a modification of unitary quantum dynamics.
It does not legislate ontology, nor does it claim dynamical inevitability. Rather, it is a con-
straint program: a regime-theoretic analysis of the structural conditions under which persistence,
stabilization, and incompatibility geometry cohere. The central contribution of the present work
is therefore not the proclamation of impossibility, but the articulation of structure. Quantitative
incompatibility induces rigidity within admissible generic regimes, while engineered coordination
occupies measure-suppressed regions of parameter space. Classical stability appears, within the
admissible framework analyzed here, as a structural feature of constraint propagation rather than
as an imposed collapse mechanism.
The structural constraints articulated here also isolate a natural rigidity question. In particular,
one may ask whether sufficiently strong margin-certified redundancy forces proximity to classical
broadcast structure at the joint-state level, and whether simultaneous redundancy for incompatible
observables enforces compatibility-set geometry beyond the reduced state. These rigidity directions
are not pursued in the present work. They represent a strengthening of the structural program
rather than a prerequisite for its validity.
At the architectural level, independent of future strengthening directions, the two-part organi-
zation of this manuscript reflects that posture. Interpretation motivates structure; structure disci-
plines interpretation. Neither layer is sufficient in isolation. Together, they define the scope, the
limits, and the identity of the program. The theory thus closes as a bounded structural analysis. Its
claims are precise, its regime distinctions explicit, and its horizons identified. Beyond those bounds,
further strengthening may proceed, but the present architecture stands independently: a coherent
structural account of admissibility, constraint, and incompatibility within finite-dimensional open-
system quantum structure.
