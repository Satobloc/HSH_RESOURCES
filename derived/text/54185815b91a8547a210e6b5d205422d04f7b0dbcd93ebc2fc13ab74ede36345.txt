1.1 The Commodification of Time in Modern Society
In contemporary society, time is widely understood as possessing economic value.
Expressions such as:
 “Time is money”
 time management
 productivity improvement
 efficient use of time
 time as a resource
are commonly used in everyday and economic discourse.
These expressions indicate that time is no longer regarded merely as a natural
phenomenon. Rather, it has become an object that can be organized, allocated, evaluated,
and optimized within human activities.
In economics, human time has long been treated as an important analytical object. For
example, Becker (1965) introduced time into the economic analysis of household
behavior by considering both market goods and time as scarce inputs through which
individuals maximize utility. His contribution was significant because it expanded
economic analysis beyond market transactions and labor supply toward the broader
allocation of human time in everyday life.

===== PAGE 2 =====
Similarly, labor economics has continuously examined the allocation of working time,
leisure time, and the trade-off between labor and non-labor activities. Human beings are
assumed to allocate limited time among various activities, including:
 labor,
 consumption,
 household production,
 leisure,
 and self-development
(Becker, 1965).
However, this conventional understanding contains a fundamental philosophical
question:
Is time truly a resource?
Generally, resources are understood as entities that agents can utilize to achieve
particular purposes. Economic resources such as land, capital, and raw materials possess
several characteristic properties:
 they can be owned,
 they can be stored,
 they can be allocated,
 and they can be accumulated.
Time itself, however, appears fundamentally different.
Time:
 cannot be owned,
 cannot be stored,
 cannot be accumulated,
 and cannot be transferred to others.
For example, people often say that they “have time.” Yet this expression is metaphorical.
Unlike land or money, individuals do not literally possess time as an object. Likewise,
an unused hour today cannot be stored and consumed tomorrow.
In this sense, time itself does not correspond to the conventional concept of a resource.
Nevertheless, modern society continues to treat time as a resource.

===== PAGE 3 =====
This creates a conceptual tension:
Although time does not appear to satisfy the properties of a resource, society
nevertheless uses time as a resource.
This study argues that this contradiction arises because the concepts of “time” and
“resource” have not been sufficiently distinguished.
The fundamental question is therefore not:
Is time itself a resource?
Rather, the more appropriate question is:
Under what conditions does time become a resource?
To answer this question, this study reconstructs the resource value of time not by
treating time itself as a resource, but by focusing on time intervals that are available to
agents for achieving their purposes.
1.2 The Ambiguity of the Concept of
Resource
The previous section identified a fundamental problem: although time is treated as a
resource in modern society, time itself does not fully correspond to the conventional
concept of resources.
To address this problem, it is necessary to clarify what a resource actually means.
However, the concept of resource has never been completely unambiguous.
In classical economics, resources were primarily understood in relation to production
activities.
The traditional factors of production were:
 land,
 labor,
 capital.

===== PAGE 4 =====
Smith (1776) analyzed the sources of wealth through division of labor and productive
activity, identifying land, labor, and capital as fundamental elements supporting
economic production. Ricardo (1817) further examined resource allocation through
differences in land fertility and scarcity conditions. Marx (1867) focused on labor power
within the production process and analyzed how resources are incorporated into value
creation under capitalism.
Within this classical framework, resources were mainly understood as:
inputs that can be introduced into the production process.
Resources referred primarily to concrete entities such as land, raw materials, labor
power, and machinery.
However, modern economics has expanded the concept of resource beyond purely
material objects.
Resources are increasingly understood as scarce means that individuals and societies
can utilize in order to achieve particular objectives.
From this perspective, resources are not limited to physical entities. Information,
knowledge, human abilities, institutions, and social relationships may also function as
resources when they enable goal achievement.
For example, human capital theory treats skills and knowledge not merely as personal
characteristics but as resources generating future productivity and income (Schultz,
1961; Becker, 1964).
Furthermore, Sen’s (1999) capability approach emphasizes that the value of resources
does not depend solely on the quantity possessed, but rather on what individuals are
actually able to achieve through them.
This represents a fundamental transformation of the concept of resource:
From:
Resource = a material object
to:
Resource = an available possibility for achieving purposes

===== PAGE 5 =====
The crucial implication is that the essence of a resource does not reside solely in the
existence of an object itself, but in the relationship between the object and an agent.
For example, the value of the same piece of land changes depending on technology,
institutions, and purposes of use. Similarly, identical information may have different
value depending on the objectives and capabilities of the user.
Therefore, resources should not be understood as objectively fixed objects, but rather as:
relationships through which agents can utilize something to achieve their purposes.
This redefinition allows a new interpretation of time.
Time cannot be owned or stored. However, when an agent possesses a purpose and can
utilize a given time interval toward that purpose, that time may function as a resource.
Therefore, the essential question is not:
Is time a resource?
but:
Under what conditions is time transformed into a resource?
The following section distinguishes between time itself and time that functions as a
resource.

1.4 Research Objective and
Theoretical Approach
The previous sections have demonstrated that the conventional understanding of time as
a resource contains a fundamental conceptual tension. On the one hand, modern
societies routinely treat time as a valuable economic resource. Individuals manage time,
invest time, save time, and allocate time in pursuit of various objectives. On the other
hand, time itself does not possess the essential characteristics traditionally associated
with resources. Unlike land, capital, or material goods, time cannot be owned, stored,
accumulated, or transferred.
This contradiction suggests that the central issue is not whether time itself should be
regarded as a resource, but rather under what conditions time becomes a resource.
Accordingly, this study does not attempt to add time to the conventional category of
economic resources. Instead, it seeks to reconstruct the concept of resource itself by
examining the relationship between time and human agency.
The fundamental argument of this research is that resources are not defined by their
physical properties alone. Rather, a resource emerges when a certain entity becomes
available for a subject to utilize in order to achieve a purpose. From this perspective,
resourcehood is not an inherent property of an object but a relational property
established between a subject, a purpose, and a field of possible action.
Applying this framework to time leads to a crucial distinction:

===== PAGE 9 =====
 Time itself is not a resource.
 Time that becomes available for purposeful action can function as a resource.
This distinction allows us to overcome the conceptual difficulty surrounding time.
Although individuals cannot possess time itself, they can possess opportunities to act
within future temporal intervals. These intervals become meaningful resources when
individuals are able to exercise choice regarding how they are used.
Based on this theoretical approach, this study introduces the concept of
Choice-Possible Time.
Choice-Possible Time refers to the portion of future time during which an individual
can select among multiple possible actions according to personal goals and intentions.
Unlike conventional measures of time quantity, such as total hours available,
Choice-Possible Time emphasizes the qualitative dimension of temporal resources: the
degree to which individuals possess meaningful freedom of action within their available
time.
This concept provides a new analytical perspective on human time. A person may
possess many hours in a day, yet if those hours are completely determined by external
obligations, they contain limited resource value. Conversely, even a shorter period of
time may possess high resource value if it allows meaningful choice and purposeful
action.
Therefore, the value of time does not depend solely on its duration. It depends on the
degree of agency embedded within that duration.
1.5 Research Questions
Based on the theoretical problem described above, this study addresses the following
research questions.
First:
What conditions are necessary for time to become a resource?
Existing discussions often assume that time is inherently a resource because humans
must allocate limited time among competing activities. However, this assumption
overlooks the distinction between the passage of time and the availability of time for

===== PAGE 10 =====
intentional action. This study therefore investigates the conditions under which temporal
intervals acquire resource value.
Second:
How should the concept of resource be reconstructed in order to explain the
resource value of time?
Traditional resource theories have often emphasized scarcity, ownership, and material
availability. However, contemporary economic theories have expanded the concept of
resources toward information, knowledge, human capability, and institutional
arrangements. Building on this development, this study examines whether resources
should be understood not as objects possessed by individuals but as possibilities
available for achieving purposes.
Third:
How does Choice-Possible Time contribute to understanding human well-being
and agency?
Economic systems have traditionally evaluated development through indicators such as
production, income, and consumption. However, these indicators do not necessarily
capture whether individuals possess meaningful freedom over their own time. This
study explores whether Choice-Possible Time can provide an alternative perspective for
evaluating human welfare by focusing on the expansion of individual choice
possibilities.
Fourth:
What implications does the concept of Choice-Possible Time have in the age of
artificial intelligence?
Generative AI technologies have significantly altered the relationship between human
activity and time. Rather than creating additional physical time, AI may reduce
necessary tasks, accelerate cognitive processes, and expand the portion of time available
for human choice. This study examines AI as a technology that transforms the structure
of human temporal resources by increasing Choice-Possible Time.
Through these questions, this research aims not merely to redefine time but to
reconsider the philosophical foundation of the resource concept itself.

Chapter 2
What Is a Resource?
Reconsidering the Concept of Resource
2.1 Resources as Material Objects
Historically, the concept of resources has been primarily understood in terms of material
objects. Particularly in classical economics and resource economics, resources referred
mainly to natural and physical entities that supported human production and survival.
Typical examples include:
 oil
 coal
 natural gas
 minerals
 forests
 water resources
 land
These resources exist independently in the natural world and acquire economic value
through human extraction, transformation, and utilization.
In classical economic thought, land was regarded as one of the fundamental factors of
production alongside labor and capital. For example, Smith (1776) analyzed wealth
creation through production and division of labor, while Ricardo (1817) examined the
economic significance of land scarcity and differences in productive conditions. In both
cases, natural resources were treated as essential foundations of economic activity.
This traditional conception of resources contains several important characteristics.

===== PAGE 13 =====
First, externality.
Resources were understood as entities external to the human subject. They exist
independently of whether a particular individual or society chooses to use them. Oil
deposits, minerals, and forests exist in nature regardless of human recognition or
intention.
Second, ownership.
Traditional resources possess the characteristic of ownership. Land, minerals, and other
natural resources can be legally controlled through property rights. The question of who
owns and manages resources has therefore been a central issue in economic theory and
institutional analysis (Demsetz, 1967).
Third, storability.
Many material resources can be stored and preserved. Oil can be stored in tanks,
minerals can be kept in warehouses, and unused resources can be carried forward into
the future. Their availability can therefore be maintained beyond the moment of
acquisition.
These three characteristics have historically formed the foundation of the conventional
concept of resources.
Characteristic Meaning
Externality Exists independently of the subject
Ownership Can be possessed or controlled institutionally
Storability Can be preserved and carried into the future
Under this traditional framework, resources are primarily understood as objects that
exist in the world and can be utilized by humans.
However, applying this definition directly to time creates a conceptual difficulty.
Although time exists independently as a natural phenomenon, it cannot be owned,
accumulated, or stored. Unused time cannot be transferred into the future, nor can
temporal availability be preserved in the same way as material resources.

===== PAGE 14 =====
Therefore, a resource concept based exclusively on material properties cannot
adequately explain the common economic understanding that "time is a resource."
This problem does not merely reveal the uniqueness of time. Rather, it indicates a
limitation within the traditional concept of resources itself: the concept was historically
constructed around material objects.
Therefore, the following section examines resources from a broader perspective—not as
physical objects, but as forms of availability for purposeful human action.
2.2 Resources in Economic Theory
The previous section examined the historical understanding of resources as material
entities. However, modern economics has gradually expanded the concept of resources
beyond land and physical materials.
Particularly since the twentieth century, economic theory has increasingly incorporated
non-material factors such as time, knowledge, and human capabilities into its analysis
of resource allocation.
A landmark contribution in this development is Becker's (1965) theory of the allocation
of time.
Becker argued that human utility is not generated solely through market goods but
through activities that combine goods and time. For example, eating requires not only
food but also time spent preparing and consuming meals. Similarly, education, childcare,
household production, and leisure activities all require temporal inputs.
Within this framework, time is treated as a fundamental constraint alongside income.
Individuals are understood as allocating their limited daily time among labor, leisure,
household activities, and self-development in order to maximize utility (Becker, 1965).
This approach was theoretically significant because it introduced time as a central
element of economic analysis.
Furthermore, human capital theory expanded this perspective by interpreting time
invested in education and training as an investment that generates future economic
returns (Becker, 1964). Human time became understood as an important factor
contributing to value creation.

===== PAGE 15 =====
Thus, modern economics recognizes time as an economically significant resource
supporting production, consumption, and human capital formation.
However, an important conceptual issue remains unresolved.
Becker's theory explains how individuals allocate time among competing activities.
In this framework, time functions as an input into economic activities.
Yet, the following two statements are not necessarily equivalent:
Time is used as an input in economic activities.
and
Time itself is a resource.
For example, electricity used in a factory is an input into production. However, the fact
that electricity is consumed in production does not by itself explain why electricity
constitutes a resource. Similarly, the fact that time is allocated to production or
consumption does not fully explain the conceptual basis for considering time a resource.
In other words, Becker's theory successfully explains the economic allocation of time,
but it does not directly answer the deeper question:
Under what conditions does time become a resource?
This question lies at the center of the present study.
The fact that humans utilize time in economic activities is undeniable. However, the
resource value of time may not originate from the intrinsic properties of time itself.
Rather, it may emerge from the relationship between human subjects, their purposes,
and their ability to utilize available time.
Therefore, this study accepts time allocation theory as a crucial foundation while
reconsidering the underlying concept of resource itself.
The fundamental question is not:
How should time be allocated?
but rather:
Under what conditions does time become a resource?

2.3 The Essence of Resources:
Availability for Purposeful Action
The previous discussion has shown that resources have historically been understood as
material objects, while modern economics has expanded the concept to include
non-material entities such as time, knowledge, and human capabilities.
However, this expansion raises a fundamental question:
Why can such fundamentally different entities as oil and time both be described as
"resources"?
To answer this question, the essence of resources must be reconsidered beyond material
properties.
This study defines a resource as follows:
A resource is a possibility available to a subject for
achieving a purpose.
This definition emphasizes that resources are not objects possessing value
independently. Rather, resources emerge through the relationship between a subject and
a purpose.
Oil becomes a resource not because it exists physically, but because it can be utilized as
fuel or as a material for industrial production. Knowledge becomes a resource when it
contributes to solving problems. Human abilities become resources when they can be
applied toward meaningful objectives.
Therefore, the existence of a resource requires at least three conditions.
(1) Subject
The first condition is the existence of a subject capable of utilization.

===== PAGE 17 =====
Without a subject, utilization cannot occur. Oil deposits and land may exist in nature,
but without a subject capable of employing them, they remain merely natural entities.
A resource emerges only through its relationship with a subject.
(2) Purpose
The second condition is the existence of a purpose.
Resources always acquire meaning in relation to objectives. The same piece of land may
function as agricultural land for farmers, residential space for urban planners, or a
conservation target for ecologists.
Therefore, resource value is not an objective property independent of human purposes.
It depends on what the subject seeks to achieve.
(3) Availability
The third condition is practical availability.
An object cannot function as a resource unless it is actually available for utilization. Oil
located deep underground may exist, but without extraction technology it cannot serve
as a usable resource. Knowledge that cannot be understood cannot contribute to action.
Human abilities that cannot be exercised under given conditions cannot function as
resources.
Thus, existence and availability are fundamentally different concepts.
Availability refers to the condition in which a subject can select and employ something
for the achievement of a purpose.
These three conditions can be summarized as follows:
Necessary condition Meaning
Subject A user capable of utilization exists

===== PAGE 18 =====
Necessary condition Meaning
Purpose A goal to be achieved exists
Availability The object can be applied toward that goal
None of these conditions can be omitted.
Availability without a subject does not constitute a resource. A subject without a
purpose cannot determine what should be considered valuable. A purpose without
availability cannot be realized through actual utilization.
The theoretical contribution of this study is to redefine resources not as properties
inherent in objects, but as relational phenomena emerging from the interaction
between subject, purpose, and availability.
This reconstruction enables a unified analysis of material resources, knowledge,
capabilities, information, and ultimately time.
The implication for time is fundamental.
Time cannot be owned or stored. Nevertheless, when a subject possesses a purpose and
can meaningfully utilize a temporal interval, that time can function as a resource.
Thus, the question is not whether time itself is a resource.
The essential question is:
Under what conditions does time become a resource?
Proposition 1
A resource is not an inherent property of an object.
It emerges through the relationship in which a subject can utilize something for the
achievement of a purpose.

3.1 Flowing Time and the Concept of Resource
The previous chapter demonstrated that a resource should not be understood as an
intrinsic property of an object, but rather as a possibility that becomes available to a
subject for achieving a purpose. Based on this definition, whether time can function as a
resource cannot be determined by examining the nature of time itself. Instead, it
requires an analysis of the relationship between human beings and time.
For this purpose, it is first necessary to clarify the philosophical understanding of time.
Time has long been a central subject of philosophical inquiry. However, many
philosophers have not regarded time as an independent object existing in the world in
the same manner as physical entities.
Aristotle, in Physics, defined time as “the number of motion in respect of the before and
after” (Aristotle, trans. 1984). According to this view, time is not a substance existing
independently from change and motion. Rather, time emerges through the measurement
and ordering of movement. Without change or motion, time would not become
intelligible as an object of human understanding.
In contrast, Heidegger approached time from an existential perspective. In Being and
Time, he argued that time is not merely a measurable sequence of moments but the
fundamental structure of human existence itself, which he called temporality
(Heidegger, 1927/1962). Human beings exist through their orientation toward future
possibilities, their relationship with the past, and their engagement with the present.
Temporality is therefore not something external to human existence but the very
structure through which human beings understand themselves and the world.
Although Aristotle and Heidegger developed fundamentally different theories of time,
they share an important insight: time is not a self-standing object that simply exists
independently in the world.
For Aristotle, time exists through its relation to movement.
For Heidegger, time exists through the structure of human existence.

===== PAGE 20 =====
In both cases, time is understood relationally rather than as an independent entity.
This philosophical insight has significant implications for the concept of time as a
resource. Resources are generally understood as objects that can be utilized, allocated,
managed, or exchanged. However, time itself does not possess these characteristics.
Time continuously passes; it cannot be stopped, stored, accumulated, or owned.
Human beings do not control the flow of time itself. Rather, they exist within the flow
of time.
Therefore, the proposition that “time is a resource” cannot be accepted without further
theoretical clarification.
However, this study does not attempt to answer the ontological question of “what is
time?” Nor does it deny the obvious fact that time passes. The central question of this
research is different:
Among the continuous flow of time, under what conditions does a portion of time
become available for human purposes and function as a resource?
In other words, a theoretical gap exists between philosophical theories of temporality
and economic theories that treat time as a resource. Philosophy has explained the nature
of time and human existence, while economics has analyzed time allocation and
constraints. Yet the conditions under which time itself becomes a resource have
remained insufficiently examined.
This chapter addresses this missing link.
3.2 The Non-Ownership of Time
The previous section established that time is not a physical entity but a phenomenon
that emerges through the relationship between human beings and the world. This section
examines one of the most important consequences of this characteristic: the
non-ownership of time.
Traditionally, resources have been understood as entities that can be possessed,
controlled, and utilized by subjects according to their purposes. Land can be owned
through property rights. Money can be stored, transferred, and accumulated. Many
physical resources share the common characteristic that they can be retained and
managed for future use.

===== PAGE 21 =====
Time, however, fundamentally differs from such resources.
People commonly use expressions such as:
 “my time”
 “free time”
 “I lost time”
Yet these expressions do not indicate actual ownership of time.
Unlike land or money, time cannot be possessed as an object. A person does not own
time itself; rather, every person experiences the irreversible passage of time.
For example, unused land can remain available for future use. Unused money can be
saved and transferred. However, an unused hour cannot be stored and recovered
tomorrow. A moment that has passed cannot be retrieved or transferred to another
person.
Time is therefore fundamentally non-transferable and non-accumulative.
This understanding is consistent with philosophical theories of time. Heidegger
conceptualized time as the temporal structure of human existence rather than an external
object possessed by individuals (Heidegger, 1927/1962). Similarly, Bergson criticized
the treatment of time as a spatialized object and emphasized lived duration (durée) as
the subjective experience of continuous becoming (Bergson, 1910/2001).
Economic theories also implicitly recognize this distinction. Becker’s time allocation
theory treats time as a finite constraint faced by individuals, not as a commodity that
can be owned like capital or land (Becker, 1965).
Therefore, the everyday statement that “time is a resource” cannot be accepted literally.
Time itself cannot be owned, stored, or transferred.
However, this does not mean that time can never function as a resource.
Rather, the fundamental question is:
Under what conditions can non-ownable time acquire resource characteristics?
This study argues that the answer cannot be found in time itself, but in the relationship
between human purposes and the availability of time for action.

3.3 The Transformation into Time
Intervals
The previous section demonstrated that time itself cannot be owned or accumulated and
therefore differs from conventional resources. However, human beings clearly utilize
time in everyday life.
How can this apparent contradiction be explained?
This study argues that the key lies in the human practice of dividing continuous time
into meaningful intervals.
Natural time flows continuously. The universe itself does not contain categories such as
“9 a.m.,” “working hours,” or “holidays.” These divisions are not properties of time
itself but social constructions created to organize human activities.
Examples include:
 working hours from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m.
 school class periods
 deadlines
 scheduled meetings
 medical appointments
 vacations and holidays
All of these represent the transformation of continuous temporal flow into bounded
units.
At this moment, the object utilized by humans is no longer “time itself.” Rather, it
becomes a defined temporal segment.
This study refers to such segments as:
Resource Intervals
A Resource Interval is defined as:
A socially and institutionally defined temporal segment that
a subject separates from continuous time and treats as an
available unit for achieving a particular purpose.

===== PAGE 23 =====
The introduction of this concept clarifies the relationship between time theory and
resource theory.
Time can therefore be distinguished into two levels:
Concept Characteristics
Flowing Time Continuous temporal passage without boundaries
Resource Interval A bounded temporal unit created for human purposes
This distinction is crucial.
The common statement “time is a resource” does not actually refer to flowing time itself.
What humans manage, schedule, contract, and allocate are not abstract time but socially
defined temporal intervals.
For example, an employment contract does not exchange “time” in general. It specifies
a particular interval, such as working from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. An airline ticket does not
purchase “three hours of time” in the abstract; it provides access to a specific interval
between departure and arrival. Education, healthcare, transportation, and administrative
systems are all organized around structured temporal intervals.
Therefore, human society does not directly utilize time itself. Instead, society
transforms continuous time into manageable units through the process of temporal
segmentation.
However, this study does not define the resource interval itself as a complete resource.
Creating a time interval is a necessary condition for resource formation, but it is not
sufficient.
Consider three hours spent on an airplane. This is certainly a temporal interval. Yet if
the individual has almost no ability to determine how that time is used, its resource
value is limited.
Similarly, compulsory waiting time or highly constrained working time may constitute
temporal intervals, but they do not necessarily represent valuable resources for the
individual.
Thus:
A resource interval is the first stage of transforming time into a resource.

===== PAGE 24 =====
A further condition is required for a temporal interval to become a genuine human
resource.
This study identifies that condition as choice possibility.
The next chapter introduces the concept of Choice-Possible Time, which explains
when a temporal interval becomes a resource through the existence of meaningful
alternatives and individual agency.

4.1 A Time Interval Alone Does Not Constitute a
Resource
The previous chapter introduced the concept of the Resource Interval, demonstrating
that human societies transform continuous temporal flow into bounded units that can be
socially organized and utilized. However, the mere existence of a time interval does not
necessarily mean that time has become a resource.
Consider a possible objection to the argument developed so far.
If being a time interval were sufficient for becoming a resource, then all time intervals
should possess equivalent resource value. Yet this clearly contradicts ordinary human
experience.
For example:
 three hours spent on an airplane,

===== PAGE 25 =====
 two hours spent in a mandatory meeting,
 one hour waiting in a hospital,
 time spent under imprisonment,
are all clearly identifiable temporal intervals. Nevertheless, most people would not
regard these periods as freely available resources.
Of course, individuals may engage in certain activities during these periods. A
passenger may read a book during a flight, or a participant may reflect on ideas during a
meeting. However, the range of possible actions is significantly constrained. Individuals
cannot freely design these periods according to their own purposes, nor can they
flexibly allocate them among alternative activities.
This demonstrates that the concept of a Resource Interval alone cannot fully explain the
resource value of time.
The present study argues that the missing condition is choice possibility.
As defined in Chapter 2, a resource is:
A possibility available to a subject for achieving a purpose.
Accordingly, “availability” does not simply mean that a period of time exists. It means
that a subject possesses the ability to choose among multiple possible actions within that
period.
Consider two periods of equal duration: three hours spent on an airplane and three hours
spent freely at home.
Physically, they are identical in length.
However, their resource values differ substantially because the range of possible actions
available to the individual differs. During a flight, physical and institutional constraints
limit possible activities. In contrast, at home, individuals may choose among various
possibilities, such as working, studying, resting, engaging in hobbies, or spending time
with family.
Therefore, the value of time as a resource is not determined by its quantity but by the
degree of choice available within that time interval.
This perspective shares a certain theoretical affinity with Amartya Sen’s capability
approach. Sen argued that human well-being should not be evaluated merely by the

===== PAGE 26 =====
possession of goods or income but by the substantive freedoms individuals possess:
what they are actually able to do and to be (Sen, 1999).
Similarly, this study argues that the value of time should not be measured by duration
alone but by the extent to which a given period provides meaningful possibilities for
action.
However, this study does not simply apply the capability approach to time.
The central claim is different:
Choice possibility is not merely a criterion for evaluating resources; it is a
necessary condition for something to become a resource in the first place.
Therefore, this study derives the following proposition:
A Resource Interval is a necessary condition for time to become
a resource, but it is not sufficient. A time interval becomes
a resource only when a subject possesses the possibility to
choose actions according to their purposes.
This proposition leads directly to the central concept of this study: Choice-Possible
Time.
The following section defines this concept and identifies the sufficient conditions under
which time becomes a resource.
4.2 Choice Possibility as the
Condition of Resource Formation
The previous section demonstrated that the formation of a Resource Interval does not
automatically create a resource. A flight duration or mandatory meeting period may
constitute a clearly defined interval, yet its resource value remains limited because
individuals cannot freely determine how that time is used.
This indicates that the resource value of time depends not on the existence of a temporal
interval itself, but on the range of actions available to the subject within that interval.

===== PAGE 27 =====
Therefore, this study introduces choice possibility as the fundamental condition through
which time becomes a resource.
The concept of Choice-Possible Time is defined as follows:
Choice-Possible Time refers to a period during which a subject
can select and perform actions among multiple possible
alternatives according to their own purposes.
This definition contains three essential components.
First, the existence of a subject
Choice requires an agent capable of making decisions. Without a subject, there can be
no meaningful selection among alternatives. A period of time without an acting subject
cannot constitute a resource.
Second, the existence of a purpose
Choice is always related to goals. Without some purpose or desired state, alternative
actions have no significance. A temporal interval becomes meaningful as a resource
only when it is connected to what the subject seeks to achieve.
Third, the existence of multiple actionable possibilities
The subject must actually possess alternative possibilities within that time interval.
When physical, institutional, or social constraints eliminate meaningful alternatives, the
resource value of that time declines.
According to this definition, even identical durations may possess radically different
resource values.
For example, three hours during which an individual may freely choose between
reading, researching, resting, or interacting with family represent a high level of
Choice-Possible Time.
Conversely, three hours during compulsory transportation or mandatory participation
may constitute a temporal interval but contain limited choice possibility.
Therefore, this study proposes the following proposition:

===== PAGE 28 =====
A Resource Interval is necessary for time to become a resource,
but sufficient resource formation requires the existence of
choice possibility.
This perspective differs from conventional economic approaches that primarily treat
time as a quantitative input.
In economic analysis, time is often modeled as a limited quantity allocated among
competing activities. However, this study argues that the resource value of time is not
determined by duration alone. Rather, it is determined by the qualitative structure of
possible actions available within that duration.
Thus:
The value of time does not lie in the amount of time possessed, but in the
possibilities that can be realized within that time.
The concept of Choice-Possible Time introduced here becomes the foundation of the
theoretical model developed in the next section.
4.3 The Time Resource Formation Model
The previous sections have demonstrated that time itself is not a resource. Furthermore,
transforming continuous time into bounded intervals does not automatically create a
resource.
This study argues that time becomes a resource only when several conditions are
satisfied sequentially.
The first condition is the transformation of continuous temporal flow into a Resource
Interval.
Human societies divide continuous time through institutions, contracts, and everyday
practices. Working hours, holidays, appointments, and deadlines are examples of
socially constructed temporal intervals.
However, a Resource Interval is only a prerequisite for resource formation. It does not
itself constitute a resource.
The second condition is the existence of a subject who can utilize the interval.

Resources do not exist independently from subjects. A temporal interval acquires
meaning only through an individual or collective actor who seeks to use it.
Furthermore, the subject must possess a purpose.
Only when a purpose exists can a temporal interval represent a possibility for achieving
something.
The most important condition, however, is the existence of choice possibility.
A subject must be able to select among multiple possible actions within the given
interval. When actions are predetermined and no meaningful alternatives exist, the time
interval has little resource value.

Chapter 5
Reconstructing the Concept of Time
through Choice-Possible Time
5.1 What Is Choice-Possible Time?

===== PAGE 31 =====
The previous chapter demonstrated that time itself does not constitute a resource. Rather,
time becomes a resource only when a time interval satisfies three conditions: the
existence of a subject, the presence of a goal, and the availability of meaningful choices
within that interval.
This chapter introduces the central concept of this study: Choice-Possible Time.
In this study, Choice-Possible Time is defined as:
Choice-Possible Time refers to a period in which a subject can choose among
multiple possible actions in accordance with personal goals and can realize those
choices in the future.
The essential point of this definition is that the value of time does not depend primarily
on its quantity, but on the degree of choice available within that time.
For example, three hours may have completely different resource values depending on
the conditions under which they occur. Three hours spent freely reading, conducting
research, resting, or engaging with family members provide a wide range of possible
actions. Such time possesses a high degree of choice availability.
In contrast, three hours spent on an airplane, in a compulsory meeting, or under
institutional confinement may constitute a clear time interval, but the range of possible
actions available to the individual is significantly restricted. Although the person
physically experiences the same duration of time, the resource value of that time is
considerably lower.
Therefore, the relevant object for evaluating time as a resource is not merely the amount
of time available. Rather, it is the extent to which a person maintains an open space of
possible choices toward the future.
In this respect, Choice-Possible Time differs fundamentally from the conventional
concept of free time. Free time is usually defined negatively as time released from
compulsory activities such as labor or household duties. However, the present study
argues that free time does not necessarily correspond to genuine resource value.
A person may formally possess free time but lack meaningful choices because of
exhaustion, financial constraints, social obligations, or psychological limitations.
Conversely, even within a constrained situation, time may possess high resource value
if an individual can make meaningful decisions according to personal purposes.
Thus, Choice-Possible Time is not an intrinsic property of time itself. It is a relational
concept emerging from the interaction among:

===== PAGE 32 =====
 the subject,
 the subject’s goals,
 and the available possibilities for action.
Although time flows uniformly as a physical phenomenon, its value as a resource
differs according to the size of the choice space that remains open to the individual.
Furthermore, Choice-Possible Time is fundamentally a future-oriented concept.
Past time has already been consumed, while present time represents the moment in
which choices are being executed. Future time, however, contains unrealized
possibilities among which choices can still be made.
Therefore, in this study, time as a resource is understood as:
the totality of future-oriented possibilities available for meaningful choice.
The following sections further examine this temporal structure and demonstrate that the
essence of time as a resource lies not in the passage of time itself, but in the possibilities
that remain open toward the future.
5.2 Distinction from Free Time
The previous section defined Choice-Possible Time as a period during which a subject
can select and realize possible actions according to personal goals. This section
distinguishes this concept from the conventional notion of free time.
Free time is generally understood as time free from obligatory activities such as
employment or household responsibilities. In economics and leisure studies, it has often
been treated as the counterpart of working time, and an increase in leisure time has been
regarded as an indicator of improved welfare (Becker, 1965; Gershuny, 2000).
However, this definition does not sufficiently distinguish between being free from
obligations and possessing genuine freedom of action.
This study argues that these two concepts must be separated.
For example, an individual may obtain three hours of free time after work but, due to
extreme fatigue, may realistically have no option other than rest. Similarly, economic

===== PAGE 33 =====
constraints may prevent a person from choosing education, travel, cultural activities, or
other personally meaningful pursuits.
The same issue appears in situations involving childcare, eldercare, or other forms of
responsibility. Although individuals may technically possess free time, their actual
range of possible actions may remain highly restricted.
Such time is categorized statistically as free time, yet its value as a resource is limited
because the individual’s effective choice space is narrow.
Conversely, time subject to certain constraints may possess substantial resource value if
individuals retain meaningful decision-making capacity and can act according to their
purposes.
Therefore, the crucial question is not whether time is free from obligations, but whether
the time provides an individual with meaningful possibilities for action.
Accordingly, this study distinguishes:
Free Time≠Choice-Possible Time\textbf{Free Time} \neq
\textbf{Choice-Possible Time}Free Time =Choice-Possible Time
Free Time represents a social or institutional classification of time, whereas
Choice-Possible Time represents the actual availability of meaningful choices within
that time.
The former concerns the external condition of time; the latter concerns the relational
condition between subject, purpose, and available possibilities.
This distinction has important theoretical implications.
If time resources are understood merely as free time, increasing the quantity of leisure
hours would automatically imply greater well-being. However, actual human experience
demonstrates that additional free hours do not necessarily increase human capability
when individuals remain constrained by exhaustion, poverty, or social limitations.
Therefore, the relevant measure is not the quantity of free time but the amount of
Choice-Possible Time that individuals possess toward their future.
Only through this perspective can the resource value of time be explained in terms of
actual human agency.

Chapter 6
A Temporal Theory of Resources:
Future, Present, and Past Time
6.1 Future Time
This study has argued that time becomes a resource only when three conditions are
satisfied: the existence of a subject, the presence of a goal, and the availability of
meaningful choices. From this perspective, the conventional distinction between past,
present, and future should not be understood merely as a chronological sequence. Rather,
each temporal dimension possesses a different relationship with resource potential.
First, future time is not simply time that has not yet arrived. In this study, future time is
understood as a domain in which a subject retains multiple possible courses of action.
In other words, the future is a space of open possibilities.
Consider an upcoming holiday next week. At that moment, an individual may choose to
travel, read a book, conduct research, or spend time with family. These possibilities
coexist because no final decision has yet been made. The essential characteristic of
future time lies precisely in this openness and indeterminacy.
This understanding directly corresponds to the concept of Choice-Possible Time
proposed in this study. As long as a subject maintains multiple possible choices toward
the future, that time possesses the potential to function as a resource.
Conversely, if future time is completely predetermined and the individual has virtually
no ability to make meaningful choices, the existence of that time interval alone does not
guarantee resource value.
Therefore, this study conceptualizes future time not merely as "time that has not yet
occurred," but as:
a potential resource space in which unrealized possibilities remain available to the
subject.
Future time itself is not yet a resource. However, when a subject retains the ability to
choose and act according to personal goals, future time can become a resource.

===== PAGE 37 =====
From this perspective, the future should not be regarded simply as a portion of time that
lies ahead. Rather, it should be understood as a space of possibilities through which
individuals construct their own actions and identities.
Future time is not merely unused time. It is a field of potential choices that may become
resources through human agency.
6.2 Present Time
The previous section defined future time as a domain in which possibilities remain open.
This section examines the nature of present time.
The present is often understood simply as "the current moment." However, such a
definition describes only the temporal position of an event and does not sufficiently
explain its relationship with human action.
This study defines the present as:
the process through which choices are actualized into action.
Although multiple possibilities exist in the future, they become concrete realities only
through decisions and actions occurring in the present.
At the present moment, a subject selects one possibility among those previously
available and transforms it into an actual activity.
For example, on a holiday morning, an individual may have several possible choices:
reading, going outside, conducting research, or spending time with others. However,
once the individual begins writing a research paper at a desk, one possibility has been
realized, while the others remain unrealized.
Thus, the present is not merely a point located between the past and future. It is a
dynamic process in which possibilities are transformed into reality.
From this perspective, present time serves as the mediating stage connecting future
possibilities and past outcomes.
Possibilities that existed in the future are realized through present decisions and actions,
and these realized actions subsequently become part of the past.

===== PAGE 38 =====
Therefore, this study proposes a three-layer temporal structure of time resources:
 Future time: time as potential choice
 Present time: time as the execution of choice
 Past time: time as the accumulated record of realized choices
Within this structure, present time represents the practical domain where individuals
convert temporal possibilities into actual value.
Accordingly, present time in this study does not refer simply to "the current instant." It
refers to:
the process through which subjects transform future possibilities into meaningful
actions.
6.3 Past Time
The previous section positioned future time as open possibility and present time as the
process of realizing choices. This section examines past time from the perspective of
resource theory.
Past time is commonly understood as time that has already elapsed. However, this study
argues that such an interpretation is insufficient.
Past time should be understood as:
the accumulated history of choices that have already been realized and whose
possibilities have been consumed.
Future time contains multiple possible pathways. Through decisions and actions in the
present, however, these possibilities become fixed as actual events.
At this moment, alternative possibilities that were not selected disappear, while the
chosen action remains as part of personal history.
Therefore, the past is not merely a collection of completed events. It is the historical
record of consumed Choice-Possible Time.
From this perspective, human life is not simply the passage of chronological time.

===== PAGE 39 =====
Rather:
Life is the history of how individuals have allocated and utilized their limited
Choice-Possible Time.
This definition is consistent with the temporal resource model proposed in this study.
Time exists as possibility in the future, becomes action in the present, and becomes
irreversible history in the past. Time resources are not merely consumed; the manner in
which they are consumed constitutes the formation of the individual’s life trajectory.
Furthermore, this perspective is compatible with the view that the subject is not a fixed
entity but a continuously generated process.
Individuals construct themselves by allocating time according to their values and goals.
The past therefore functions not merely as a record of events but as evidence of how
individuals have historically used their temporal resources.
Accordingly, this study defines past time not as "time that has ended," but as:
the accumulated history of Choice-Possible Time that has been transformed into
experience.
In this sense, human life is not measured by the amount of time one possesses, but by
how one has transformed available time into meaningful choices.
The past is no longer a resource itself.
However, it becomes an experiential resource through which individuals revise their
future values, goals, and choices.

7.1 Does AI Increase Human Time?

===== PAGE 40 =====
The recent development of generative artificial intelligence (AI) has brought significant
transformations to human intellectual activities. Tasks such as writing, information
retrieval, programming, translation, and even complex reasoning assistance can now be
performed in substantially shorter periods of time. Consequently, it has become
common to describe AI as a technology that “creates more time” for humans.
Indeed, generative AI can significantly reduce the amount of time required for many
cognitive tasks. It accelerates information search, assists in content creation and analysis,
and enables the exploration of multiple perspectives in reasoning processes. In this
sense, AI represents a transformative technology that fundamentally changes how
humans utilize their limited time.
However, from the perspective of the time resource theory developed in this study, the
expression that “AI increases time” requires careful reconsideration. AI does not create
time itself. The total amount of time available to human beings remains unchanged; a
day still consists of twenty-four hours.
More importantly, AI itself is not the subject of time resources. AI does not possess its
own future as a field of personal possibility, does not establish intrinsic goals, and does
not exist as a finite living being that experiences the limitation of time. Therefore, AI
cannot manage or allocate time as a resource for itself.
This point is crucial because, according to the theoretical framework proposed in this
study, time becomes a resource only when a subject can utilize future possibilities in
accordance with its purposes. A resource is not defined by the mere existence of an
object or quantity, but by the relationship between a subject, its goals, and the
possibilities available for action.
From this perspective, what AI directly expands is not time itself, but rather
Choice-Possible Time—the portion of future time in which individuals can exercise
meaningful choices.
For example, suppose a task that previously required one hour of information gathering
can now be completed within a few minutes through generative AI. The saved time can
then be reallocated toward research, creative activities, learning, family interaction, or
rest. What has increased in this process is not the total quantity of time available to the
individual. Rather, what has expanded is the range of possible actions that the individual
can choose within their remaining time.
Therefore, the essential function of AI is not the replacement or expansion of human
time itself. Instead, AI transforms the structure of human time utilization by expanding

===== PAGE 41 =====
the range of choices available within future time. It enables individuals to allocate their
limited time resources more flexibly according to their purposes and values.
This interpretation is also consistent with the author’s previous argument that large
language models (LLMs) do not possess values of their own but provide a Reasoning
Space in which human reasoning can be expanded and reconstructed. AI is not an
autonomous agent that determines human values or purposes. Rather, it functions as an
intellectual infrastructure that enables humans to reconsider their values, generate new
questions, and reorganize the allocation of their time resources.
In this sense, the fundamental significance of AI in the coming era does not lie in
increasing the amount of time itself. Instead, the essence of AI lies in expanding human
Choice-Possible Time—the capacity of individuals to transform limited temporal
resources into meaningful possibilities.
Chapter 8
Conclusion
This study has critically examined the widely accepted assumption that “time is a
resource” by reconsidering the fundamental concept of resources itself. In contemporary
society, time is routinely treated as a resource through practices such as time
management, productivity improvement, and scheduling. However, when examined
more carefully, time itself does not possess the fundamental characteristics traditionally
associated with resources. Time cannot be owned, stored, accumulated, or transferred to
others. Therefore, there exists a conceptual difficulty in treating time itself as an
intrinsic resource in the same sense as conventional material resources.
This study argues that this difficulty arises because previous discussions have often
failed to distinguish between time itself and the resource potential contained within
time. To address this problem, this research reconstructed the concept of resources from
a relational perspective. Rather than defining resources by their material properties, this
study proposed that resources emerge when something becomes available for a subject
to utilize in pursuit of a goal.
From this perspective, time itself is not a resource. Instead, time becomes a resource
only when a subject can utilize a given temporal interval as a means of achieving its
purposes. Thus, the relevant object of analysis is not abstract, flowing time, but the time
intervals that become available for human action.

===== PAGE 42 =====
Furthermore, this study demonstrated that the existence of a time interval alone is
insufficient for establishing resource value. A period of time may be clearly defined as
an interval while still lacking meaningful resource potential. For example, compulsory
meetings, restricted travel periods, or other forms of constrained time may constitute
recognizable temporal intervals, yet they provide limited opportunities for autonomous
action. Therefore, time becomes a resource only when three conditions are
simultaneously satisfied: the presence of a subject, the existence of a purpose, and the
availability of meaningful choices.
Based on this theoretical framework, this study introduced the concept of
Choice-Possible Time. Choice-Possible Time refers to time during which a subject can
choose among multiple possible actions in accordance with its own purposes and
intentions. This concept extends conventional approaches that evaluate time primarily
through quantity by incorporating the qualitative dimension of time: the degree to which
time enables meaningful choice and action.
Through this concept, the study also reconstructed the relationship between past, present,
and future. Future time represents a domain in which possibilities remain open and
choices have not yet been determined. Present time is the process through which these
possibilities are transformed into actual actions. Past time represents the accumulated
history of previously consumed choices. From this perspective, human life is not merely
the passage of chronological time; rather, it is the historical process of how individuals
have allocated and utilized their Choice-Possible Time.
The implications of this framework extend to the emerging relationship between
humans and artificial intelligence. This study argued that generative AI does not create
additional time itself. The total amount of human time remains unchanged. Instead, AI
expands human Choice-Possible Time by reducing the effort required for certain
cognitive tasks and enabling individuals to redirect saved time toward alternative
purposes, such as learning, creativity, research, relationships, or rest.
AI itself is not a subject of time resources because it does not possess intrinsic purposes,
personal futures, or finite human existence. Nevertheless, it functions as an intellectual
infrastructure that expands the range of possibilities available to human beings. In the
AI era, the significance of technology should therefore not be understood as increasing
the quantity of time, but as enhancing the possibility space within human time.
The central proposition of this study can therefore be summarized as follows:
Time is not inherently a resource. Time becomes a resource when a subject, guided
by purposes, can exercise meaningful choices within it.

===== PAGE 43 =====
The concept of Choice-Possible Time provides a theoretical framework for evaluating
this process of resource formation.
This perspective contributes not only to the reconsideration of time but also to the
broader philosophical and economic question of what constitutes a resource. Resources
are not simply objects that exist independently in the world. Rather, they emerge
through the relationship between subjects, purposes, and available possibilities for
action. By reconstructing the concept of resources through the analysis of time, this
study provides a new theoretical perspective for the fields of time economics, resource
theory, and the understanding of human agency in the age of artificial intelligence.
Ultimately:
Time is not a resource by itself.
Time becomes a resource only when human beings possess the ability to choose
their future.
