Abstract
We develop an analytic framework for jet suppression in radiatively efficient accretion disks 
around spinning black holes, extending the scale-invariant schematic model of Garofalo & Singh 
(2016). In that work, geometrically thin disks were argued to suppress relativistic jets by inhibiting 
the mass loading of the large-scale magnetic field structure associated with the Blandford–Payne 
effect. Here we turn that schematic picture into a spin-dependent analytic model by treating the 
disk atmosphere as a loading reservoir that is depleted by radiatively driven disk winds. The 
Blandford–Payne effect is taken to represent the slower, matter-dominated outer jet component 
required to support, collimate, and stabilize the inner relativistic jet powered by the Blandford–
Znajek mechanism. Because centrifugal loading along inclined magnetic field lines requires 
plasma attachment to the disk atmosphere, the strength of the Blandford–Payne component 
depends sensitively on the density of material available above the disk surface. We show that as 
black hole spin becomes increasingly prograde, the inward shift of the ISCO increases the 
radiative efficiency of the thin disk and enhances radiative mass loss from the disk atmosphere. 
This depletes the plasma reservoir available for Blandford–Payne loading, weakening the outer 
magnetohydrodynamic jet structure even when comparable magnetic flux threads the black hole. 
The resulting suppression is therefore indirect. Radiative winds do not eliminate the Blandford–
Znajek mechanism itself, but prevent the formation of the mass-loaded outer jet required for a 
powerful large-scale relativistic outflow. The model naturally favors strong jets in retrograde 
accretion systems and progressively weaker jets toward high prograde spin, offering a physical 
explanation for the radio loud/radio quiet dichotomy in active galactic nuclei and for jet 
suppression in radiatively efficient black hole accretion states.
1.Introduction
The production of relativistic jets in black hole accretion systems remains one of the central 
unsolved problems in high-energy astrophysics. Observations across the black hole mass scale 
reveal a strong connection between accretion state and jet production, with geometrically thick, 
radiatively inefficient accretion flows generally associated with powerful jets and geometrically 
thin, radiatively efficient disks associated with jet suppression (Fender et al. 2004; Marscher et 
al. 2002; Hardcastle et al. 2007; Körding et al. 2006). In active galactic nuclei (AGN), this 
distinction appears in the radio loud/radio quiet dichotomy, where only a minority of quasars 
produce powerful relativistic jets despite evidence that rapidly spinning black holes may exist in 
both radio loud and radio quiet systems (Sikora et al. 2007; Reynolds 2014). Similar behavior is 
observed in X-ray binaries, where transitions into radiatively efficient soft states are 
accompanied by strong jet suppression (Fender et al. 1999, 2004). These observations suggest 
that jet production depends not only on black hole spin, but also on how the accretion flow 
supplies plasma to the magnetic structures required for jet formation.

Theoretical models for relativistic jet production have historically emphasized large-scale 
magnetic fields threading rotating black holes and accretion disks. The Blandford–Znajek 
mechanism extracts rotational energy electromagnetically from the black hole through magnetic 
flux threading the event horizon, while the Blandford–Payne effect describes centrifugal 
acceleration of matter along inclined magnetic field lines anchored in the accretion disk 
(Blandford & Znajek 1977; Blandford & Payne 1982). General relativistic magnetohydrodynamic 
simulations have demonstrated the ability of magnetized accretion flows to produce relativistic 
jets, particularly in magnetically arrested disk states characterized by strong vertical magnetic 
flux accumulation near the black hole (Tchekhovskoy et al. 2011; McKinney et al. 2012; Narayan 
et al. 2022). However, despite major progress in numerical modeling, the physical origin of jet 
suppression in radiatively efficient thin disks remains poorly understood.
One common explanation for the radio loud/radio quiet dichotomy invokes differences in black 
hole spin, with radio loud AGN associated preferentially with rapidly spinning black holes (Sikora 
et al. 2007). However, X-ray reflection spectroscopy indicates that many radio quiet Seyferts and 
quasars may also host rapidly spinning black holes (Reynolds 2014; Walton et al. 2013; 
Brenneman 2013). If rapidly spinning black holes exist in both radio loud and radio quiet systems, 
then spin alone cannot determine whether a powerful large-scale jet forms. A second class of 
models emphasizes differences in magnetic flux accumulation, especially in magnetically arrested 
disk scenarios where powerful jets require large concentrations of vertical magnetic flux near the 
event horizon (Narayan et al. 2003; Tchekhovskoy et al. 2011). Yet differences in magnetic flux in 
otherwise similar accretion systems remain unclear. 
In the present work, we adopt a different starting point. We assume that the large-scale magnetic 
flux threading the black hole and inner disk can be broadly comparable across the spin range. 
Therefore, the relevant spin-dependent control parameter is not primarily the amount of 
magnetic flux, nor the mere ability of field lines to bend into a Blandford–Payne favorable 
configuration. Field-line inclination remains a necessary condition for centrifugal launching, but 
it is not by itself sufficient. The field lines must also acquire plasma from the disk atmosphere. 
The central issue addressed here is therefore the availability of matter for mass loading, not the 
existence of magnetic flux or field-line bending alone.
Garofalo & Singh (2016) proposed a scale-invariant framework in which jet suppression emerges 
naturally in radiatively efficient accretion states through the interaction between thin-disk 
accretion and jet-supporting outflows. In that schematic picture, thin disks suppress jets not 
because of insufficient black hole spin or reduced magnetic flux, but because efficient radiative 
disks inhibit the mass loading required for the large-scale magnetohydrodynamic outflow 
associated with the Blandford–Payne effect. The present work builds directly on that framework 
by developing the underlying physics analytically and by explicitly modeling the competition 
between radiative atmospheric depletion and centrifugal mass loading as a function of black hole 
spin. 
A key ingredient in the model is the dependence of radiative efficiency on black hole spin. As the 
ISCO radius decreases toward high prograde spin, the inner disk becomes increasingly radiatively 
efficient, enhancing radiatively driven mass loss from the disk atmosphere. This depletes the 
plasma reservoir available for Blandford–Payne loading. By contrast, high-spin retrograde

2. The Jet–Disk Connection and Blandford–Payne Mass Loading
The production of large-scale relativistic jets in black hole accretion systems is generally 
understood to require the combined action of rotating black holes, large-scale magnetic fields, 
and plasma capable of loading those magnetic structures. In the standard picture, the Blandford–
Znajek mechanism (Blandford & Znajek 1977) extracts rotational energy electromagnetically 
from a spinning black hole through magnetic flux threading the event horizon, while the 
Blandford–Payne effect (Blandford & Payne 1982) centrifugally accelerates matter outward along 
inclined magnetic field lines anchored in the accretion disk. In the schematic developed in 
Garofalo & Singh (2016), the Blandford–Payne component is identified with the slower, matter-
dominated outer jet structure that supports, collimates, and stabilizes the inner relativistic 
Blandford–Znajek jet. In this interpretation, efficient relativistic jet production requires not only 
electromagnetic extraction from the black hole, but also successful mass loading of the outer 
magnetohydrodynamic flow.
The essential condition for centrifugal launching in the Blandford–Payne effect is that the 
magnetic field lines emerging from the disk surface possess sufficient inclination relative to the 
vertical direction. For cold magnetocentrifugal acceleration, the poloidal magnetic field line must 
bend away from the vertical by more than 30∘, or equivalently make an angle smaller than 60∘
relative to the disk plane (Blandford & Payne 1982). In cylindrical coordinates, this condition may 
be written approximately as

In many jet models, differences between radio loud and radio quiet systems are attributed

the black hole remains broadly comparable across the spin range. The dominant difference 
instead emerges from the ability of the accretion disk atmosphere to supply plasma to the outer 
magnetohydrodynamic jet structure. This shifts the focus of the jet problem away from magnetic 
flux accumulation alone and toward the interaction between the disk atmosphere and radiative 
disk physics.
Radiatively efficient thin disks produce substantial radiation pressure in their inner regions, 
particularly at high prograde black hole spin where the ISCO radius becomes small and the 
radiative efficiency increases. The enhanced radiation field naturally drives strong disk winds and 
atmospheric mass loss. In the context of the present model, these radiative winds compete 
directly with the centrifugal mass-loading process required for the Blandford–Payne component. 
Plasma removed from the disk atmosphere by radiative driving is no longer available to load the 
inclined magnetic field lines responsible for supporting the outer jet.
The competition between these two processes forms the basis of the jet suppression mechanism 
explored here. The essential idea is not that radiative winds dynamically destroy an already-
formed relativistic jet. Rather, radiative atmospheric depletion suppresses the formation of the 
mass-loaded outer MHD jet structure required to sustain the large-scale Blandford–Znajek 
outflow. As radiative efficiency increases toward high prograde spin, progressively less plasma 
remains available for centrifugal loading, weakening the outer jet and indirectly suppressing the 
large-scale relativistic jet despite continued electromagnetic extraction near the black hole 
horizon.
This picture naturally explains why rapidly spinning black holes need not always produce 
powerful relativistic jets. Even if magnetic flux threading the black hole remains substantial, 
insufficient plasma loading of the outer magnetohydrodynamic component may prevent the 
formation of a stable, well-collimated large-scale outflow. In the following section, we develop a

5. Discussion and Conclusions
The framework developed in this paper has important implications for the interpretation of jet 
production across black hole accretion systems. Because the suppression mechanism is tied 
fundamentally to the interaction between radiative disk physics and centrifugal mass loading, the 
model naturally connects the radio loud/radio quiet dichotomy in AGN with accretion state 
transitions in X-ray binaries within a unified scale-invariant picture. In both cases, the essential 
physics depends not primarily on black hole mass, but on the ability of the accretion flow to 
maintain a sufficiently dense disk atmosphere capable of loading the outer

This interpretation is consistent with the growing observational evidence that rapidly spinning 
black holes exist in both radio loud and radio quiet systems (Reynolds 2014; Brenneman 2013). 
In conventional spin paradigms, the existence of high-spin radio quiet AGN is difficult to reconcile 
with the expectation that large spin should produce strong relativistic jets through efficient 
electromagnetic extraction. In the present framework, however, high spin simultaneously 
increases radiative atmospheric stripping, thereby reducing the efficiency of centrifugal loading 
in the outer jet. Large black hole spin therefore becomes compatible with jet suppression once 
the role of disk atmosphere depletion is included.
The model also provides a natural explanation for the observed anti-correlation between strong 
disk winds and large-scale relativistic jets in some AGN populations. Radiatively efficient systems 
exhibiting strong winds are precisely those expected to experience substantial atmospheric 
depletion. In such systems, plasma removal from the disk surface suppresses the formation of 
the outer magnetohydrodynamic jet component required to support the relativistic outflow. 
Conversely, systems with weaker radiative winds retain sufficient atmospheric plasma for 
efficient centrifugal loading and stronger jet production. The jet–wind connection therefore 
emerges naturally from the competition between radiative depletion and Blandford–Payne 
loading.
The same physical framework extends naturally to X-ray binaries. During hard states, accretion 
flows are geometrically thick and radiatively inefficient, producing relatively weak radiative 
atmospheric depletion and strong steady jets. During transitions into soft states, however, the 
accretion disk becomes geometrically thin and radiatively efficient. The resulting increase in 
radiative atmospheric stripping suppresses centrifugal loading of the outer 
magnetohydrodynamic jet component, leading to the well-known disappearance of steady 
relativistic jets in soft states (Fender et al. 1999, 2004). The observed scale invariance between 
AGN and X-ray binaries therefore follows directly from the underlying disk atmosphere physics 
rather than from black hole mass itself.
An important implication of the present model concerns the interpretation of GRMHD 
simulations. Modern simulations have successfully demonstrated the ability of magnetized 
accretion flows to generate relativistic jets, particularly in magnetically arrested disk 
configurations characterized by strong vertical magnetic flux accumulation near the event 
horizon (Tchekhovskoy et al. 2011; McKinney et al. 2012). However, many numerical studies 
focus primarily on the production of relativistic jets rather than on the physical origin of jet 
suppression in radiatively efficient thin disks. The present framework suggests that this limitation 
may arise because the key suppression physics depends sensitively on the thermodynamic 
structure of the disk atmosphere and on the competition between radiative winds and centrifugal 
mass loading.

radiation physics, radiative transfer, opacity-dependent acceleration, and disk surface 
thermodynamics. These processes remain difficult to model in current GRMHD calculations,
