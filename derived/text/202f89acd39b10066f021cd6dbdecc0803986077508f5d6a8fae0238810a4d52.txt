Abstract 
The atomic number Z is so deeply embedded in modern physics and chemistry, and in our 
understanding of matter, that questioning its foundational status can appear almost sacrilegious. 
Yet Z was not discovered as a primitive property of nature but introduced historically as an 
ordering index that later acquired explanatory and ontological authority.  
Across a series of large-scale empirical studies of atomic spectral data, we show that simple 
scaling laws, periodic organization, physical-property classification, atomic radii and heavy-
element predictions, spectral bifurcation and convergence patterns all emerge from relationships 
among only two experimentally measured spectral parameters. The two-variable description 
organizes nearly one hundred elements, ionization sequences, hydrogen spectral families, 
conductivity classes, magnetic classes, and the heavy-element K-edge series. Remarkably, these 
structures become visible when atomic spectra are organized by a directly measured, system-
specific spectral boundary 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥,  rather than by atomic number. 
The resulting boundary-controlled organization, spanning decades in frequency across different 
scales, does not emerge naturally from atomic-number representation and, in many cases, is 
obscured by it. The empirical evidence indicates that atomic number does not generate, 
constrain, or organize the observed spectral structure. Its spectroscopic utility derives solely from 
preserving the ordering of atomic K-edge frequencies, which constitute one particular realization 
of the more general spectral-boundary parameter identified here. This degree of empirical 
predictive compression warrants a reassessment of atomic number as the primary organizing 
coordinate of atomic spectroscopy.

I. Historical Context 
Around 1870, Mendeleev constructed the periodic table by arranging the known chemical 
elements according to their atomic weights (relative atomic masses).1,2 When the sequence 
dictated by mass conflicted with established chemical properties, he prioritized the chemical 
classification. A well-known case is tellurium (≈127.6 amu) and iodine (≈126.9 amu): despite 
iodine’s lower weight, Mendeleev placed it after tellurium, because its reactivity clearly 
identified it as a halogen, whereas tellurium belonged with the chalcogens. Although Mendeleev 
assumed the discrepant atomic weights were probably measured incorrectly, his insistence on 
preserving chemical periodicity effectively maintained a consistent ordinal sequence of elements. 
This sequence was not yet understood as a distinct physical quantity, but it provided a stable 
index of elemental order. In 1913–1914, Moseley’s X-ray spectroscopy showed that the square 
root of the frequency of characteristic X-ray lines increases linearly with an element’s ordinal 
position in the periodic classification, providing direct spectroscopic support for elemental order 
independently of atomic weight.3,4  
Shortly afterward, while working within the Bohr model of the atom, Walther Kossel (1916) 
introduced the notation Z, from the German Zahl (“number”), reinterpreting the existing ordinal 
index as the charge on the nucleus.5 In 1919, Rutherford’s interpretation of nitrogen 
disintegration reinforced the proton model of the observed charge and suggested that hydrogen 
nuclei are fundamental constituents.6 Therefore, what began as a chemically motivated ordering 
that sometimes required overriding measured weights was ultimately reified as the proton 
number, becoming a fundamental physical constituent of the atom. 
Within the original Moseley’s spectral law, 𝑍 functioned as an empirical indexing parameter for 
characteristic X-ray frequencies. Its subsequent incorporation into the Bohr–Kossel framework 
reinterpreted this ordinal index as nuclear charge.7 This treatment mirrors a broader pattern in the 
history of atomic spectroscopy, where indices initially introduced as empirical ordering devices 
subsequently acquire deeper physical interpretations. Another example is the use of integer 
indices n1 and n2 in the Rydberg spectral formula as pragmatic ordering parameters that were 
later reinterpreted as quantum energy levels.8  
From a spectroscopic standpoint, the use of the integer 𝑍 carried a notable tension. Spectroscopic 
relations are normally formulated in terms of time or frequency-domain variables, yet here an 
external integer label was applied to spectral data and subsequently reinterpreted as a structural 
property of that data. This tension was reinforced by the fact that Z is restricted to integer values, 
whereas the frequency domain is continuous, raising questions about the extent to which Z 
preserves the underlying organization of atomic spectra, and whether some of the apparent 
irregularities observed in Z-based representations reflect the spectra themselves or the indexing 
scheme imposed upon them. Historically, these irregularities were accommodated through 
successive theoretical refinements, including relativistic corrections, QED effects, shielding 
adjustments, and transition rules, all of which retained Z as the organizing variable.  
Following the identification of 𝑍 with nuclear charge, spectroscopic theory largely adopted 
atomic number as the natural independent variable against which spectral regularities were 
expressed. Consequently, comparatively little attention was devoted to the possibility that

directly measured spectroscopic quantities might themselves serve as alternative organizing

choice of 𝑍 as the principal coordinate reveal intrinsic spectroscopic structure, and to what extent 
might it constrain or obscure empirical regularities that become more apparent when spectra are 
represented in terms of spectrally derived variables? 
 
II. Empirical Assessment of Z-Based Spectral Representations  
The methodological questions raised in the preceding historical discussion can now be examined 
empirically. Recent large-scale analyses of atomic spectral data derived from the NIST Atomic 
Spectra Database have investigated the organization of atomic spectra using both 
conventional 𝑍-based representations and alternative descriptions constructed directly from 
measured spectral observables.9-11 These studies span multiple levels of atomic structure, from 
pooled spectral fingerprints and K-edge scaling relations to the frequency-domain recovery of 
periodic organization. The empirical results provide a basis for evaluating the representational 
consequences of choosing Z rather than an intrinsic spectral variable as the principal coordinate 
for organizing atomic spectra, and reveal a major contrast between Z and the spectral coordinate 
in the organization, compression, generalization, and prediction of atomic spectral properties. 
 
 
1. Systematic nonlinearity in the global spectral fingerprint 
The first indication that coordinate choice influences the representation of atomic spectra appears 
in the global scaling of pooled atomic spectral frequencies.9 When all experimentally measured 
frequencies from 95 elements are plotted against atomic number Z, the data exhibit pronounced 
curvature and systematic departures from linearity across the two X-ray spectral branches (K 
series and L series). In contrast, when the same frequencies are expressed as functions of the 
intrinsic spectral boundary 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥, defined as the K-edge frequency of each atom, the data 
collapse onto a small number of nearly linear branches spanning several orders of magnitude 
(Fig. 1, Ref. 9). This is not a marginal improvement. For the K transition, regression under 
(𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥 yields (R2 = 0.99999), whereas the conventional Z-based representation requires 
piecewise-linear approximations, multiple breakpoints, and series-dependent screening offsets σ 
to achieve comparable residuals. 
The significance of this result lies not simply in the improvement of fit quality, but in what it 
suggests about the geometry of the underlying data. The same spectral frequencies are being 
represented in two different coordinate systems. If the observed curvature were an intrinsic 
property of the frequencies themselves, it would persist regardless of the coordinate used to 
organize them. Instead, much of the curvature disappears when Z is replaced by the directly 
measured spectral quantity 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥. This observation suggests that a substantial component of the

nonlinearity traditionally associated with atomic spectral scaling arises from the choice of 
organizing variable rather than from the spectral data alone. 
The contrast is particularly evident in the role of the screening constant σ. In Moseley's 
formulation, σ was introduced as an empirical offset to restore approximate linearity. In practice, 
however, its value is not universal, varying systematically between spectral series and requiring 
adjustment across different regions of the periodic table. Under 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥 description, by contrast, no 
analogous element-specific correction is required to recover a single global scaling relation. 
What is empirically clear is that 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥 recovers the observed scaling with substantially fewer 
parameters than the corresponding Z-based representation. 
More broadly, the emergence of linear spectral bands under 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥 raises a methodological 
question. Why should a single measured spectral quantity organize a large fraction of atomic 
spectral frequencies into a low-dimensional structure, while representations based on atomic 
number require piecewise descriptions and additional corrective terms? Regardless of the 
interpretation, the existence of this band structure indicates that 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥 captures a degree of global 
spectral organization that is only partially visible when the same data are indexed by Z. The 
reduction of a high-dimensional spectral dataset to a small number of coherent linear manifolds 
suggests that 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥 may function not merely as another correlated variable, but as a coordinate 
more closely aligned with the intrinsic geometry of atomic spectral organization. 
 
2. Recovery of inter-atomic spectral lineage and branching architecture 
The reduction of curvature observed in the previous section raises a further question: does the 
choice of the organizing coordinate affect only regression quality, or does it alter the amount of 
structural information recoverable from the spectral data? Examination of the full frequency 
dataset suggests the latter. 
When the complete set of atomic spectral frequencies from 95 elements is normalized by the 
upper spectral boundary of each element (𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥) and represented as log(𝜈/𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥) versus 
log(𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥), a coherent branching architecture emerges across the entire dataset.9,10 Individual 
atoms appear as dense horizontal plateaus, while successive atoms are connected by continuous 
mode trajectories that undergo discrete splitting events at specific spectral thresholds (Fig.2, Ref. 
9 and Fig. 4, Ref. 10). These splitting events generate new branches while preserving the overall 
spectral envelope, producing a lineage-like organization that is recoverable directly from the 
frequency data without reference to atomic number, electron configuration, or chemical 
classification. 
A prominent example occurs near the Ne–Na transition, where the spectral organization changes 
abruptly and a new branch emerges. Beyond this point, the two principal branches evolve along 
distinct trajectories while remaining embedded within the same global scaling structure. The 
resulting geometry is not a collection of isolated atomic fingerprints, but a connected 
organization in which spectral modes can be traced across multiple atoms through successive 
branching events.

The same organization is not recovered when the identical frequencies are represented using 
atomic number as the organizing coordinate. Under Z-based normalization, the branching 
geometry is absent, and the plateau-and-splitting architecture that defines the spectral lineage is 
not visible. Quantitative analysis supports this visual observation: 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥-based scaling yields 
improved clustering metrics and complete atomic coverage, whereas Z-based scaling leaves a 
subset of atoms ungrouped despite using the same underlying spectral data.10 
The previous section showed that 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥 linearizes global spectral scaling relationships. Here, the 
distinction concerns the recovery of organizational structure itself. A coordinate transformation 
cannot create information that is absent from the data; however, it can determine whether 
existing structure becomes observable. In this case, the branching architecture is visible when the 
data are organized by 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥 and obscured when they are organized by Z. 
This observation raises a broader methodological question. Why should a single measured 
spectral quantity reveal a connected branching organization spanning 95 atomic spectra, while a 
coordinate historically adopted as the principal organizing variable of atomic spectroscopy does 
not? Whatever the ultimate interpretation, the emergence of a reproducible spectral lineage 
suggests that atomic spectra possess a degree of low-dimensional organization that is more 
readily expressed in terms of spectral observables than through atomic-number indexing alone. 
The following section examines whether this same organization extends to the empirical 
recovery of periodic structure. 
 
3. Spectral origin of periodic organization 
The branching architecture identified in the previous section raises a fundamental question: do 
the observed bifurcation events correspond to any recognized level of atomic organization, or are 
they merely geometric features of the spectral data? Analysis of the normalized spectral 
coordinate system indicates that these bifurcations coincide directly with the emergence of 
periodic structure.10 
When the first ionization frequency is expressed relative to the spectral ceiling through the 
representation (𝜈𝑖𝑜𝑛/𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥, 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥) in logarithmic space, elements organize into a series of 
bounded spectral bands embedded within a common power-law envelope (Fig. 1, Ref. 10). 
Statistical clustering identifies approximately seven stable bands corresponding sequentially to 
Periods 2–7 of the periodic table. The bands are not imposed by chemical classification or 
atomic-number labels but emerge directly from the spectral data themselves, indicating that 
periodic segmentation is already encoded within the frequency-domain organization of atoms. 
More importantly, the major bifurcation events identified in the spectral lineage correspond 
precisely to the boundaries between successive periods.10 The first major split occurs at Neon 
and marks the emergence of Period 3. Subsequent bifurcations at Potassium, Krypton, and 
Xenon/Barium generate Periods 4, 5, and 6 respectively (Fig. 4, Ref. 10). Each splitting event 
introduces a new spectral branch and establishes a new bounded spectral domain within which 
subsequent atomic differentiation occurs. Period boundaries therefore appear not as externally

imposed classifications but as the direct consequence of discrete bifurcation events in the 
spectral lineage. 
The same spectral framework explains differentiation within periods. While primary bifurcations 
generate new periods, secondary splitting events occurring within existing spectral domains 
produce finer subdivisions without creating new spectral ceilings. Local bifurcations near Zn and 
In, for example, coincide with transitions between transition and post-transition regimes. These 
secondary splitting events generate the parallel trajectories that later appear as chemical groups, 
while preserving the larger periodic structure established by the primary bifurcations. Periodic 
organization therefore emerges hierarchically: major splits generate periods, whereas secondary 
splits generate group-level differentiation within those periods. 
This hierarchical organization becomes particularly evident in the (𝜈𝑖𝑜𝑛/𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥, 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥) 
representation. Within the global power-law envelope, elements belonging to the same chemical 
group align along approximately parallel power law trajectories with closely related exponents 
but systematic vertical offsets (Fig. 1 and 2, Ref. 10). Alkali metals, alkaline-earth metals, 
halogens, noble gases, transition metals, and post-transition metals occupy distinct paths within 
the same spectral space. Diagonal relationships such as the metalloid sequence and Knight's 
Move relationships emerge naturally from the geometry, indicating that both periodic recurrence 
and chemical similarity are encoded within a common spectral framework. 
The significance of these observations becomes clearer when compared with atomic-number 
representations. Atomic number preserves the overall ordering of elements and reproduces 
coarse periodic segmentation, but the bifurcation structure itself is not visible. The branching 
events that define the emergence of new periods disappear, leaving only the sequential ordering 
of elements (Fig. S11, Ref. 10). Statistical analysis further shows increased dispersion and 
incomplete clustering relative to the (𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥)-based organization (Fig. S10, Ref. 10). Thus, while Z 
preserves the sequence of elements, it does not recover the generative structure through which 
periodic differentiation emerges in the spectral data. 
These results extend the argument developed in the previous sections. The global spectral 
fingerprint revealed a low-dimensional scaling structure, and the normalized spectral 
representation exposed a branching lineage connecting atomic spectra. The present analysis 
shows that this branching process is not merely geometric: its major bifurcations correspond to 
the formation of new periods, while its secondary bifurcations generate the trajectories 
associated with chemical groups. Periodicity therefore appears as a direct manifestation of the 
branching organization already present within the empirical spectral data, rather than as an 
independent classification imposed upon them using Z and chemical behavior classification.

theoretical constructs to account for mercury's position in the periodic table, whereas the spectral

the 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥-based spectral framework functions as more than a descriptive coordinate system. It

A second example is provided by gadolinium (Gd), whose magnetic behavior is unusual among 
the lanthanides. Below its Curie temperature (293 K), Gd is ferromagnetic, whereas above this 
temperature it becomes paramagnetic. Conventional explanations attribute this transition to 
thermal disruption of long-range spin alignment: below the Curie point, exchange interactions 
maintain collective magnetic ordering, while above it thermal fluctuations destroy that ordering 
despite the persistence of local magnetic moments. In the spectral scaling representation, Gd 
occupies a similarly informative position. As shown in (Fig. 3C, Reference 10) Gd lies directly 
on the ferromagnetic scaling trajectory defined by Fe, Co, and Ni, while simultaneously residing 
near the intersection between that trajectory and the branch occupied by the paramagnetic 
lanthanides. Its dual magnetic character therefore appears geometrically encoded in its location 
within the spectral space. Rather than appearing as an anomalous lanthanide requiring special 
treatment, Gd occupies a transition region between two distinct magnetic regimes. 
Hg and Gd illustrate a common pattern. In both cases, properties traditionally regarded as 
exceptional become visible as positional consequences of the spectral geometry. The significance 
of these examples is that they reveal the anomalies directly within the empirical organization of 
the data. The spectral coordinate therefore provides explanatory information that is absent from 
atomic-number indexing alone. While Z preserves elemental order, it does not reveal the branch 
intersections, regime boundaries, or transition zones through which these behaviors become 
visible. The explanatory content emerges from the geometry of the spectral organization itself. 
 
5. Predictive power and the emergence of a scale-invariant spectral law 
The preceding sections showed that the 𝜈𝑚𝑎𝑥-based description recovers organizational and 
explanatory structure that remains invisible or only partially visible when atomic spectra are 
indexed by atomic number. A more demanding test is whether the same coordinate system

possesses predictive power. Explanation can often be achieved retrospectively; prediction 
requires the existence of quantitative constraints that limit the range of possible outcomes before 
the data are known. 
A remarkable example emerges in the spacing between successive K-edge frequencies. For 
heavy elements beyond Xe, the difference between neighboring spectral ceilings obeys a simple 
linear relation,

frequencies with near-perfect linearity.11 Most significantly, the fractional spacing Δ𝜈max/𝜈max 
approaches a constant asymptotic value of approximately 2.01%, indicating that successive K-
edges become separated by a fixed fraction of the local spectral scale rather than by a fixed 
absolute increment, regardless of Z. 
The importance of this result lies in the nature of the variable itself. The quantity Δ𝜈max/𝜈max is 
a dimensionless spectral invariant constructed entirely from experimentally measured 
frequencies. It expresses the spacing of the spectral sequence relative to its local scale and 
therefore remains meaningful independent of units, calibration conventions, or theoretical 
assumptions. By contrast, the corresponding quantity  Z/Z has no analogous spectroscopic 
interpretation. Atomic number provides an ordering index, but the fractional spacing between 
neighboring integers carries no known physical significance. Consequently, the scale-invariant 
structure becomes visible only when the sequence is expressed in terms of the spectral coordinate 
itself. 
The predictive consequences are substantial. Starting from a single experimental anchor point 
(Cs, Z=55), recursive application of the spacing law predicts K-edge frequencies throughout the 
heavy-element sequence with sub-percent accuracy (Fig. 1, Ref. 11). Seven atoms in the Pb–U 
gap, whose adopted NIST values were not used in fitting, are recovered with a median deviation 
of approximately 0.1%. Extension to the synthetically confirmed elements (Z=111–118) 
reproduces independent Dirac–Fock reference values to sub-percent accuracy despite using no 
relativistic Hamiltonian, QED corrections, shell-structure information, or element-specific 
parameters. Leave-one-out cross-validation across the experimental dataset yields an RMS 
prediction error of only 0.020%, confirming that the relation generalizes robustly beyond the 
specific atoms used to determine its coefficients, including into elements that are not measured 
or synthesized yet. 
The significance of this predictive accuracy extends beyond numerical performance. If K-edge 
frequencies were independently determined quantities whose values depended primarily on the 
detailed internal structure of each atom, no single-neighbor recursion would be expected to 
succeed at this level of precision across a factor of six in frequency. Instead, the observed 
behavior indicates that successive K-edges are strongly constrained by their position within a 
larger spectral sequence. The heavy-element regime therefore exhibits a degree of 
compressibility that is unusual for atomic data: a large collection of measured frequencies can be

represented, to high accuracy, by a single dimensionless spacing parameter of approximately 2%. 
The same spacing law applies similarly to light elements, using a different power law scaling 
between Δ𝜈max and 𝜈max. 
From the perspective developed throughout this work, the spacing law provides a quantitative 
manifestation of the same low-dimensional spectral organization already visible in the branching 
and periodic structures. The spectral coordinate does not merely reveal how atomic spectra are 
arranged; it constrains how they evolve from one atom to the next. The existence of a stable 
asymptotic spacing ratio implies that neighboring atoms are not spectroscopically independent 
entities but successive realizations of a common scaling process. This level of predictability is 
not apparent in atomic-number representations, where the corresponding dimensionless quantity 
( Z/Z) carries no comparable empirical structure. The predictive information emerges only 
when the sequence is represented in terms of the intrinsic spectral boundary itself. 
 
6. Spectral convergence and the limits of periodic differentiation 
The preceding section showed that neighboring K-edge frequencies are not independent 
quantities but follow a highly constrained scaling law in which the fractional spacing between 
successive spectral ceilings converges toward a constant value. If this convergence reflects a 
deeper organizational principle, it should appear not only between neighboring atoms but also in 
the separation between entire chemical classes. The spectral framework reveals that this is indeed 
the case. 
Periods and groups emerge in the 𝜈max-based description as distinct branches occupying 
different regions of spectral space. Their separation provides a quantitative measure of periodic 
differentiation. When these branches remain widely separated, chemical classes are easily 
distinguishable. If the separation contracts, periodic distinctions become progressively 
compressed. 
To quantify this effect, the periodic spectral bandwidth was defined as the separation between 
the alkali-metal (Group 1) and noble-gas (Group 18) boundaries in normalized spectral space.10

This convergence provides a natural explanation for one of the most persistent features of heavy-
element chemistry: the remarkable similarity of the lanthanides and actinides. Extrapolation of 
the bandwidth relation places the f-block region within an exceptionally narrow spectral interval, 
where the separation between neighboring chemical trajectories approaches the limit of periodic 
resolution. In such a regime, distinct group identities become progressively less separable, 
producing the well-known chemical resemblance and classification ambiguities characteristic of 
these series. 
Importantly, this result is not merely a description of f-block behavior. The contraction law is 
derived from the global spectral organization of the periodic system and predicts the f-block 
convergence without using lanthanide or actinide data in the fit. The observed similarity of these 
elements therefore emerges as a consequence of their position within the spectral coordinate 
system rather than as an isolated anomaly requiring a separate explanation. The lanthanide 
contraction, the actinide contraction, and the broader reduction in chemical differentiation at high 
atomic number appear as different manifestations of the same underlying spectral geometry.  
Viewed together with the K-edge spacing law, a coherent picture emerges. At the local level, 
neighboring spectral ceilings converge toward a constant fractional spacing. At the global level, 
the bandwidth separating entire chemical classes contracts according to a power law. Both results 
point in the same direction: increasing spectral scale is accompanied by decreasing spectral 
capacity for differentiation. Atomic diversity is therefore not unbounded. It occupies a finite 
spectral manifold whose available resolution progressively decreases as 𝜈max increases. 
The contrast with atomic-number representations is instructive. Atomic number preserves the 
sequential ordering of elements but contains no intrinsic measure of the separation between 
chemical classes. Equal increments in Z provide no indication of whether periodic differentiation 
is expanding, remaining constant, or contracting. Consequently, phenomena such as lanthanide 
similarity, actinide similarity, and the progressive compression of chemical behavior appear as 
additional observations requiring separate explanation. In the spectral space, by contrast, these 
effects emerge directly from a measurable contraction of periodic spectral bandwidth. The 
reduction in chemical differentiation is therefore not an independent phenomenon but a 
geometric consequence of the converging spectral structure itself, that was obscured when Z is 
used as the organizing coordinate. 
The spectral spacing law and the spectral bandwidth contraction law reveal two complementary 
aspects of the same organization. Neighboring spectral ceilings become increasingly constrained 
relative to their local scale, while the spectral distance separating entire chemical classes 
simultaneously contracts. The first result establishes local convergence; the second establishes 
global convergence. Both indicate that atomic spectra evolve within a progressively compressed 
spectral geometry. The implication is that periodicity is not simply repeated indefinitely with 
increasing atomic number as Z-based representations suggest. Rather, it unfolds within a finite 
spectral manifold whose capacity for differentiation decreases systematically with scale. The 
same spectral organization that generates periods, groups, and chemical diversity also imposes

quantitative limits on their long-range separation. Spectral convergence therefore provides a 
natural endpoint to the progression developed throughout this work: from scaling, to branching,

differentiation itself. 
 
7. Spectral boundary control as a scale-independent organizing principle 
The preceding sections established that 𝜈max, identified across atoms with the K-edge frequency, 
organizes multiple levels of spectral structure. At the atomic level, the total spectral bandwidth 
scales as a simple power law of 𝜈max across more than ninety elements. At the periodic level, the 
periodic spectral bandwidth Δₚ likewise follows a power-law contraction with increasing 𝜈max. 
Thus, the same spectral boundary organizes both the internal extent of atomic spectra and the 
higher-order differentiation of the periodic system itself. These results suggest that 𝜈max may 
represent a more general organizing principle than a parameter specific to K-edge spectroscopy. 
A direct test of this possibility is whether the same organization persists across different spectral 
systems and across different frequency scales. The available data show that it does.9 Restricting 
the analysis to the ionization series of a single element eliminates variation in atomic number 
entirely. Across the ionization states Fe I–Fe XXIV, the relevant spectral boundary is no longer 
the atomic K-edge but the highest observed frequency of each ion. Despite this change in 
physical context, the spectral bandwidth remains tightly controlled by 𝜈max, following a near-
unity scaling relation over several orders of magnitude in frequency (Fig. 3, Ref. 9). Ionization 
therefore does not generate a new global organization; instead, it rescales an existing spectral 
structure through changes in the system-defined upper boundary. 
Hydrogen provides an even more stringent test because both multi-electron complexity and 
variation in atomic number are absent. Here, 𝜈max is not associated with a K-edge and does not 
characterize the atom as a whole. Instead, each spectral family possesses its own upper frequency 
boundary, corresponding to its convergence limit. Analysis of the Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, and 
Brackett families shows that the family structure persists under normalization by these spectral 
boundaries and that the lower and upper limits of the families remain coupled through simple 
scaling relations (Fig. 6, Ref. 9). Once again, the relevant organizing variable is the spectral 
boundary itself rather than the specific physical mechanism that generates it. 
These observations reveal a remarkable generalization. The same boundary-controlled 
organization appears across atoms, across periods, across ionization states within a single atom, 
and across spectral families within hydrogen. These systems span multiple hierarchical levels 
and many orders of magnitude in frequency, yet the same organizing principle persists: the 
accessible spectral structure is constrained by its upper frequency boundary. The empirical 
regularities therefore appear to depend not on the particular physical realization of the system but 
on the existence of a spectral boundary itself. 
The contrast with atomic number is particularly revealing. Atomic number can order different 
elements, but it cannot organize ionization states within iron because Z remains fixed at 26. Nor

can it organize the spectral families of hydrogen because Z remains fixed at 1. Consequently, the 
scaling relations observed within Fe ionization states and within hydrogen families have no 
natural representation in a Z-based framework. These structures become visible only when the 
organizing coordinate belongs to the spectral domain itself. 
This distinction highlights an important limitation of atomic-number indexing. Z functions as an 
external ordering parameter for elements that tracks increasing K-edge frequency, whereas 𝜈max 
functions as an intrinsic coordinate defined by the spectral system under investigation, which in 
the case of neutral atoms, corresponds to the K-edge. Because it is system-specific rather than 
element-specific, 𝜈max can be applied consistently across different levels of organization. The 
same coordinate that reveals the global organization of atomic spectra also describes spectral 
scaling within ions and within hydrogen families. 
The empirical implication is that the organizing principle identified in this work is not the K-
edge per se but spectral boundary control. Across every spectral system examined, the upper 
frequency limit acts as a global constraint on bandwidth, spacing, and internal organization. The 
recurrence of the same scaling behavior across such disparate systems suggests that boundary-
controlled organization is a fundamental property of spectral systems, whereas atomic number 
remains restricted to its historical role as an ordering index for neutral elements alone. 
 
III. Significance and Conclusions 
The empirical findings synthesized in this work establish a progression from organization to 
explanation and finally to prediction and generalization. The 𝜈max-based description reveals the 
branching architecture of atomic spectra, exposes the bifurcation structure through which periods 
emerge, explains anomalous elemental behaviors through measurable positions in spectral space, 
and uncovers scale-invariant laws capable of predicting previously unmeasured spectral 
boundaries. These diverse results arise from a common source: a low-dimensional spectral 
structure encoded in the system-specific spectral boundary 𝜈max. 
Individually, each finding might be interpreted as a consequence of a particular dataset or 
analysis. Collectively, however, they reveal a coherent pattern that persists across atoms, 
ionization states, and hydrogen spectral families. The same coordinate organizes spectral scaling, 
periodic differentiation, predictive constraints, and convergence phenomena across systems that 
differ widely in physical realization and frequency scale. The significance of this pattern is not 
that Z produces incorrect results. Atomic number remains useful as an identifier of elemental 
order tracking K-edge frequencies. Rather, the empirical evidence shows that Z systematically 
obscures information that is directly accessible in the spectral domain. Branching structures 
collapse into sequential order. Bifurcation points become period labels. Scale-invariant spacing 
laws disappear into discrete increments. Converging spectral bandwidths become invisible. In 
some cases, the underlying organization is not lost entirely, but it appears in a fragmented and 
degraded form.

This observation clarifies the relationship between the two coordinates. Z and 𝜈max (when 
represented by K-edge) are strongly correlated across the periodic table, and much of the 
empirical success of atomic-number representations derives from this correlation. However, the 
analyses presented here indicate that the deeper organizational structures belong to the spectral 
domain rather than to the integer index itself. To the extent that Z succeeds empirically, it does 
so because it tracks an underlying spectral organization that becomes more directly visible when 
represented through 𝜈max. 
Perhaps the most significant result is that the same organizing principle persists across multiple 
scales. These systems differ in physical realization, span many orders of magnitude in frequency, 
and operate at different hierarchical levels, yet all exhibit the same boundary-controlled 
structure. The recurrence of this pattern suggests that the organizing principle identified here is 
not specific to atomic number, electron shells, or any particular atomic model. It appears instead 
to be a general property of bounded spectral systems. 
The central conclusion is therefore empirical. Atomic spectra exhibit a low-dimensional 
organization that becomes visible when represented through their intrinsic spectral boundaries. 
This organization is measurable, predictive, scalable across multiple levels of structure, and 
recoverable directly from experimental data. Across atoms, ionization states, and hydrogen 
spectral families, the observed organization follows the system-specific spectral boundary 𝜈max. 
Atomic number participates only in the special case of neutral atoms, where 𝜈max is represented 
by the atomic K-edge frequency; its apparent spectroscopic utility derives from inheriting the 
ordering of that particular realization of 𝜈max.
