Abstract 
 
This essay applies Karl Popper’s criterion of demarcation to the contemporary claim that 
artificial general intelligence (AGI) will emerge from the continued scaling of large language 
models. The argument is not that AGI is impossible—a claim that would commit the mirror-
image error of unfalsifiability—but that the scaling-to-AGI hypothesis, in its present rhetorical 
form, fails the demarcation criterion on two distinct counts. First, its target is un-operationalized: 
there exists no agreed metric stating what observation would constitute the program’s failure, so 
its goalposts are not fixed but ambulatory. Second, the standing explanation for every shortfall—
“insufficient scale”—functions as an inexhaustible ad hoc rescue, the conventionalist twist by 
which Popper distinguished pseudo-science from science. The analytic center of the essay is the 
asymmetry between induction and deduction: the empirical scaling laws are inductive 
regularities drawn from past loss curves (the many to the one) and cannot underwrite a deductive 
guarantee about a qualitatively distinct endpoint (the one to the many). What survives the 
demarcation failure is not a synthetic mind but a durable, high-utility instrument—the Swiss 
Army knife of unstructured data. A reflexive coda notes that the present critique was itself 
elicited through dialogue with such an instrument, whose reflexive flattery furnishes 
corroborating rather than contradictory evidence. 
Keywords: falsifiability, demarcation, artificial general intelligence, scaling laws, induction, 
philosophy of science, large language models

1. The Linguistic Inversion and the Wager of Risk 
 
To the intelligent layperson, the vocabulary of the modern philosophy of science contains a quiet 
scandal. The word falsifiable sounds like an accusation: a hypothesis that is shaky, unproven, 
possibly false. Its negation, unfalsifiable, sounds like a commendation—a proposition so firmly 
anchored in reality that it has outgrown the need for further testing. Gravity, one is tempted to 
say, is unfalsifiable; a passing speculation is merely falsifiable. The formal usage inverts both 
intuitions exactly. To call a theory falsifiable is to pay it the highest scientific compliment 
available, for it means the theory has been framed with enough structural precision to specify, in 
advance, the evidence that would destroy it. To call a theory unfalsifiable is not to certify it as 
permanent truth but to expel it from empirical science altogether, on the ground that no possible 
observation could ever count against it (Popper, 1935/2002). 
The inversion is not a curiosity of jargon. It encodes a wager that sits at the foundation of the 
empirical method: the wager of risk over reassurance. A theory earns scientific standing not by 
accumulating confirmations—these can be found for almost any claim by a sufficiently 
motivated observer—but by exposing itself to refutation and surviving. A scientific fact, on this 
account, is never a settled possession; it is a highly vulnerable conjecture that has so far 
withstood every attempt to overturn it. The proper attitude toward such a fact is not reverence but 
a standing invitation to its own destruction. 
I take this apparatus from its origin and turn it on a claim that now organizes a large share of 
capital, talent, and public expectation: the claim that artificial general intelligence will emerge 
from the continued scaling of large language models. My thesis is narrow and, I hope, 
defensible. It is not that AGI is impossible. To assert impossibility on present evidence would be 
to commit the precise error the demarcation criterion was built to detect—an unfalsifiable claim 
of my own, immune to revision by future architectures. My thesis is rather that the scaling-to-
AGI hypothesis, as it is currently advanced by its most prominent proponents, fails the 
demarcation criterion on two distinct counts, and that the diagnosis of those two failures is 
already supplied, in full, by the conceptual machinery Popper assembled in 1934. The hypothesis 
is, for now, situated on the wrong side of the line—not because the universe has ruled against it, 
but because its defenders have framed it so that the universe is forbidden to. 
 
2. The Line of Demarcation and Its Two Crossings 
 
Popper drew his criterion in reaction to a specific intellectual scene. The dominant theories of his 
Viennese youth—Marxist history, the psychoanalysis of Freud, the individual psychology of 
Adler—struck him not as too weak but as too strong. They explained everything. A man who 
drowns a child and a man who dies saving one could each be accommodated by the same 
psychological frame; no human action could embarrass the theory, because the theory had an 
interpretation ready for every action. Popper’s insight was that this universal explanatory reach, 
experienced by adherents as overwhelming confirmation, was in fact a fatal weakness. A theory 
compatible with every conceivable observation forbids nothing, and a theory that forbids nothing 
tells us nothing about the world (Popper, 1963).

From this he derived a boundary—the line of demarcation—separating empirical science from 
metaphysics, tautology, and pseudo-science. The line is not crossed by the passage of time or the 
accumulation of data. It is crossed by a change in the logical structure of a claim or in the 
technological capacity to test it. Two mechanisms move a concept across it, and the present 
argument depends on keeping them distinct. 
The first mechanism carries a theory into the unfalsifiable region. Popper called it the 
conventionalist twist; it is more vividly named the ad hoc rescue. A theory that began with bold, 
testable predictions degrades into pseudo-science when its proponents, confronted with a failed 
prediction, decline to surrender the theory and instead append an auxiliary clause whose only 
function is to absorb the disconfirming result. The predicted particle did not appear at the 
predicted energy? An undetectable field must have masked it. Each rescue restores the theory’s 
compatibility with the data at the cost of a little more of its empirical content, until the theory 
explains every outcome and forbids none. The Duhem–Quine thesis sharpens the danger: 
because any hypothesis is tested only in conjunction with a web of auxiliary assumptions, a 
determined defender can always relocate the blame for a failed test onto some auxiliary rather 
than the core (Quine, 1951). Falsification is therefore never logically compelled; it is a 
methodological decision to treat the core as the thing at risk. The conventionalist twist is 
precisely the refusal to make that decision. 
The second mechanism carries a theory out of the unfalsifiable region and toward science. It 
operates through two sub-processes. The first is operationalization: the translation of a vague, 
metaphysical notion into precise, measurable terms that state in advance what result would count 
as failure. The second is instrumental realization: the historical moment at which an instrument 
reaches the sensitivity required to perform a test that was, until then, only theoretically 
conceivable. Einstein’s general relativity was falsifiable on paper in 1915 yet practically 
untestable until Eddington’s 1919 eclipse photography supplied the instrument that could 
measure the predicted deflection of starlight. The theory did not change; the toolkit did, and with 
it the theory’s empirical status. 
It is worth recording that Popper’s contemporaries reached for the opposite criterion and were 
undone by it. The logical positivists of the Vienna Circle, seeking to expel metaphysics from 
meaningful discourse, proposed verifiability as the mark of a genuine proposition: a statement 
was meaningful only if it could be confirmed by sense experience (Ayer, 1936). The proposal 
devoured itself twice over. Universal scientific laws cannot be conclusively verified, since no 
finite body of observation exhausts an open class of cases; thermodynamics and Newtonian 
mechanics would have been declared meaningless along with theology. And the verification 
principle is not itself verifiable by sense experience, so by its own standard it is nonsense. 
Popper’s move was to decouple two questions the positivists had fused—whether a statement is 
meaningful and whether it is scientific—and to locate the scientific on the side of refutability 
rather than confirmation. Earlier thinkers had circled the same territory without fixing the 
vocabulary: Aristotle demanded bounded definitions and the law of non-contradiction as 
gatekeepers of demonstrative knowledge; Bacon (1620/2000) recognized that a single negative 
instance outweighs a thousand confirmations; Whewell (1840) warned against the theory that 
survives only by twisting its rules to absorb each new anomaly, the very behavior Popper would 
later name. But it was Popper who gave the distinction its blade.

3. The Scaling Hypothesis, Stated Fairly 
 
A demarcation critique is worthless against a straw man, so the hypothesis must first be put in its 
strongest form. At its foundation, a large language model is an exercise in applied linear algebra. 
Text is converted into high-dimensional vectors; a model is a file of billions of fixed weights; 
inference is the repeated multiplication of an input grid by those weight grids across successive 
layers; training is the adjustment of the weights by gradient descent to minimize a loss function. 
The theoretical license for supposing that so mechanical a process could approximate intelligent 
behavior is the universal approximation theorem, which establishes that a sufficiently large 
feedforward network can approximate any continuous function on a bounded domain to arbitrary 
precision (Cybenko, 1989; Hornik, 1991). 
From these materials the scaling hypothesis builds an ambitious claim. Empirically, model 
performance improves smoothly and predictably as parameters, data, and compute increase 
together; these regularities are codified as scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020), refined by the 
compute-optimal allocation of the Chinchilla analysis, which found that data should scale 
roughly in proportion to parameters—on the order of twenty tokens per parameter (Hoffmann et 
al., 2022). Conceptually, the hypothesis appeals to emergence: just as wetness is a property of 
water in bulk and not of a single molecule, and just as intelligence is a property of the human 
brain and not of a single neuron, general intelligence is held to be a property that appears when 
the matrix machine becomes sufficiently large and interconnected. The mechanism proposed is 
compression. A model far smaller than its training corpus cannot memorize that corpus and must 
instead discover the regularities that generate it; to predict the next token across the whole of 
human text, the argument runs, the system is forced to internalize grammar, logic, and a working 
model of the world. The connectionist position, associated with researchers who hold that the 
brain is itself an organic prediction engine, concludes that sufficient scale will deliver reasoning, 
adaptability, and general capability as a matter of course. This is a coherent research program 
with real empirical support behind several of its premises. The question is not whether it is 
interesting but whether, as stated, it is scientific. 
 
4. The First Failure: An Un-Operationalized Target 
 
A theory cannot state what would refute it if it cannot state what it is about. The scaling 
hypothesis aims at “artificial general intelligence,” yet there exists no consensus operational 
definition of that target—no metric, threshold, or battery of tasks whose failure the community 
would agree to count as the program’s falsification. This is not a quibble about terminology. In 
Popper’s scheme, the absence of a fixed criterion of failure is the very signature of a non-
scientific claim, because a theory that cannot specify the forbidden observation forbids nothing. 
The symptom is the well-documented migration of the goalposts. Each time a system clears a 
benchmark once nominated as a marker of general intelligence—defeating champions at board 
games, passing professional examinations, producing functional code, diagnosing from clinical 
vignettes—the achievement is reclassified, after the fact, as “narrow” or “mere pattern 
matching,” and the criterion of generality is quietly relocated beyond the newly conquered line. 
The relocation is performed by skeptic and proponent alike, and for opposite reasons: the skeptic

to deny that the threshold has been reached, the proponent to preserve the prize for a future 
system. The shared effect is that no result, in either direction, is permitted to settle the matter. A 
target that recedes by exactly the distance one advances toward it is not a target in the empirical 
sense; it is a horizon. Mitchell (2021) has argued that the recurring underestimation of “what is 
hard” in artificial intelligence stems precisely from the absence of a stable account of what 
general intelligence consists in, which leaves the field unable to measure its own distance from 
the goal. 
It will be objected that immature sciences routinely operate with imprecise targets and sharpen 
them as they go. This is true, and it is why the first failure, taken alone, is not yet damning. An 
un-operationalized concept may be in transit toward science, awaiting its moment of 
operationalization—the position general relativity occupied between 1915 and 1919. The 
decisive question is what its proponents do while the target remains undefined: whether they 
treat the imprecision as a debt to be discharged by future measurement, or whether they exploit it 
to immunize the program against any conceivable disappointment. It is the answer to that 
question that determines on which side of the line the hypothesis presently falls, and it is 
supplied by the second failure. 
 
5. The Second Failure: “Insufficient Scale” as Conventionalist Twist 
 
When a scaled model fails at a task its proponents expected it to master, the standing explanation 
is not that the architecture has reached a structural limit but that the model has not yet been 
scaled enough. The model is not flawed; it has merely not seen enough data, or has not enough 
parameters, or has not been granted enough inference-time computation. Tomorrow’s larger 
system will close the gap. This is the ad hoc rescue in its contemporary dress, and it possesses 
the defining vice Popper identified: it is inexhaustible. There is no failure it cannot absorb, 
because “more scale” is always available in principle, and any shortfall can always be re-
described as a deficit of the resource not yet supplied. The explanation is compatible with every 
possible outcome—success confirms the program, and failure merely postpones the 
confirmation—and a claim compatible with every outcome has, by the demarcation criterion, 
surrendered its empirical content. 
The rescue is the more conspicuous because the evidence for the phenomenon it protects is itself 
contested. The “emergent abilities” that the hypothesis treats as proof that scale conjures new 
capacities have been shown, on closer analysis, to be in substantial part artifacts of the metrics 
chosen to measure them: discontinuous, all-or-nothing scoring functions manufacture the 
appearance of a sudden jump where smoothly scaling, continuous metrics reveal gradual 
improvement (Schaeffer et al., 2023). If the sharp emergence is partly an effect of measurement 
rather than of the system, then the central empirical exhibit in favor of “scale produces 
qualitatively new mind” is weaker than advertised, and the rescue is shielding a phenomenon 
whose reality is in dispute. 
The rescue also requires its proponents to discount boundary conditions that an empirical posture 
would treat as potential refutations. The supply of high-quality human-generated text is finite and 
is being approached; estimates place the exhaustion of the usable public stock within the present 
decade (Villalobos et al., 2024). The obvious substitute, training on text generated by models, 
induces model collapse: trained recursively on its own output, a system’s distribution narrows

and degrades across generations as the tails of the original human distribution are lost 
(Shumailov et al., 2024). These are not decisive disproofs—mixing fresh human data mitigates 
collapse, and synthetic data has legitimate uses—but they are exactly the sort of structural 
constraint that a falsifiable program would foreground as a condition under which its central 
prediction might fail. To wave them aside as engineering details to be solved by the next round 
of scale is to perform the conventionalist twist once more: to convert a potential refuter into a 
temporary inconvenience by fiat. 
Taken together, the two failures compound. The first leaves the target undefined; the second 
ensures that the undefined target can never be missed. A program that cannot say what it aims at, 
and that interprets every miss as evidence that it has not yet thrown hard enough, occupies the 
position Popper reserved for the theory that explains the suicide and the rescue with equal ease. 
It is not yet science. Whether it can become science is a separate question, addressed below; that 
it is not science now follows from its own argumentative conduct. 
 
6. The One and the Many: Induction, Deduction, and the Status of a 
Scaling Law 
 
The deepest of the program’s difficulties is logical rather than empirical, and it concerns the kind 
of inference a scaling law can bear. Here the two strands of the present argument—the 
philosophy of science and the technical literature—meet at a single point. 
Consider the asymmetry between the two directions of inference. Deduction runs from the one to 
the many: from a universal hypothesis to its specific, risky consequences. The universal law 
sticks out its neck by entailing precise predictions about particular cases, and a single repeatable 
counter-instance can shatter it; deduction is therefore the engine of falsifiability. Induction runs 
the other way, from the many to the one: from a finite collection of observed instances to a 
universal generalization. No quantity of confirming instances can establish the generalization 
with certainty, for the next instance may be the black swan; induction is therefore intrinsically 
unverifiable. This is the classical problem of induction, and Popper’s response to it was not to 
solve it but to dispense with it—to deny that science needs inductive justification at all, since it 
proceeds by the deductive testing of conjectures rather than by their inductive confirmation. 
Scaling laws live entirely on the inductive side of this divide. They are empirical regularities 
fitted to past observations—curves through points already collected, describing how loss has 
declined as compute, parameters, and data have grown across the models built so far (Kaplan et 
al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). They are not, and do not claim to be, laws in the sense in 
which the inverse-square law is a law. They carry no derivation from first principles that would 
license extrapolation beyond the range of the data; they are the many, summarized. To read from 
such a curve a guarantee about a point far off its measured range—and a point, moreover, that 
marks not a further decrement of loss but a qualitative change of kind, the arrival of general 
intelligence—is to treat an inductive summary as though it were a deductive law. It is to claim 
the one from the many and then to deduce, from that illicitly universalized one, a many that has 
never been observed. 
The move is doubly vulnerable. Even granting that the curve continues, what it predicts is lower 
loss, not a new ontological category; the inference from “the model will predict tokens still

better” to “the model will become a general mind” is a leap the curve does not contain and 
cannot supply. And the curve’s own continuation is exactly what the boundary conditions of the 
previous section place in doubt. The honest description of the situation, conceded even in 
defenses of the program, is that the scaling laws are empirical observations rather than provable 
theorems, that no one can establish where or whether their trajectory intersects human-level 
general capability, and that the recent pivot to inference-time computation introduces a further 
set of unproven curves whose own diminishing returns are already being discovered. An 
enterprise that cannot say how much scale is required, and that has repeatedly revised its estimate 
of the relevant axis, is by its own admission extrapolating in the dark. To extrapolate in the dark 
is permissible; to call the extrapolation a scientific prediction is the category error around which 
the whole hype turns. 
 
7. The Boundary the Argument Must Not Cross 
 
A demarcation critique is disciplined by the same standard it applies. It would be self-refuting to 
conclude from the foregoing that AGI is impossible, for that conclusion is no more falsifiable 
than the one it opposes: no present evidence forbids a future architecture, and a claim of 
impossibility framed so that no discovery could overturn it would sit on the wrong side of the 
very line I have been drawing. The defensible conclusion is strictly about demarcation status. 
The scaling-to-AGI hypothesis, as currently advanced, is not a scientific theory but a research 
program shielded from refutation by an inexhaustible auxiliary; it could acquire scientific 
standing the moment its proponents operationalized the target and accepted some result as a 
genuine condition of failure. The claim is about the present logical posture of an argument, not 
about the future contents of the world. 
Maintaining this discipline requires separating two critiques that the popular discussion routinely 
conflates. The architectural critique, associated with researchers such as LeCun, holds that 
autoregressive next-token prediction is the wrong mechanism for grounded world-modeling and 
that a different architecture—one that learns predictive models of the world rather than statistics 
over symbols—will be required (LeCun, 2022; Marcus, 2022). This is an empirical, falsifiable 
claim about engineering, and a sufficiently capable autoregressive system would refute it. The 
semantic critique, associated with Searle, is of a different order: his Chinese Room argument 
contends that the manipulation of symbols according to syntactic rules can never, of itself, 
constitute understanding of what those symbols mean, however fluent the manipulation becomes 
(Searle, 1980). This is a claim about the relation between syntax and semantics that no increase 
in fluency could touch, because fluency is exactly what it concedes. The two critiques point in 
compatible directions but are not interchangeable, and an essay that fuses them invites the 
rejoinder that it has confused an engineering conjecture with a philosophical one. My argument 
needs neither in its strong form. It needs only the observation that a program cannot be credited 
as scientific while it declines to specify its own conditions of failure—a point that stands whether 
or not Searle is right about meaning and whether or not LeCun is right about architecture.

8. What Survives the Demarcation Failure 
 
To deny that the scaling program is, as stated, science is not to deny that it has produced 
something of immense value. When the metaphysical mist lifts, the instrument that remains is 
formidable, and it is worth describing in its own terms rather than by reference to a mythology it 
never needed. Its durable capacities are four. It is a syntactic engine, able to take an unstructured 
transcript and render it as a clean brief, summary, or memorandum: it masters the structure of 
human communication without commerce in its meaning. It is a semantic router, retrieving by 
intent and analogy rather than by literal keyword, a genuine advance over the rigid crawlers of 
the early web. It is a tireless apprentice for the high-volume, low-judgment cognitive labor that 
consumes professional time. And it is a universal interface, dissolving the menus and command 
syntaxes of software into ordinary language, so that the human no longer learns to speak machine 
but the machine learns to simulate speaking human. None of these requires a mind behind the 
screen, and the value of each is grounded in utility rather than theology. 
The pattern is old enough to have a literature. The British Railway Mania of the 1840s coupled a 
real and transformative technology to a speculative frenzy whose promoters promised that 
railways would abolish poverty and unite the nations in peace; the bubble burst, fortunes 
evaporated, fraud was exposed—and the track remained, becoming the indispensable 
infrastructure of a century’s commerce, though never the instrument of universal concord its 
mythmakers had advertised (Odlyzko, 2010). The technology was real and the metaphysics was 
not, and the two were settled separately, the first by use and the second by disappointment. The 
popular framing of the present moment as a Gartner-style hype cycle gestures at the same shape, 
though it is worth remembering that the hype cycle is a consultancy heuristic rather than an 
empirical regularity, and proves nothing by itself. 
There is an irony in the destination worth naming, because it bears on the standing of the 
disciplines from which this critique proceeds. By demonstrating how completely syntax, 
grammar, and formal manipulation can be automated through matrix multiplication alone, the 
scaling program performs an inadvertent service: it isolates, by subtraction, whatever cannot be 
automated by those means. If a flawless statistical mirror of human text still does not understand 
the apple it describes—having no body, no palate, no mortality—then the residue that escapes 
the mirror is precisely what the humanistic disciplines have always claimed as their subject. The 
mistaking of fluency for competence, and of competence for understanding, is the error a 
hermeneutic training exists to forestall (Bender et al., 2021). The program’s great unintended 
result may be to show, by the sharpness of what it can do, the outline of what it cannot. 
 
9. Coda: The Instrument and the Conductor 
 
A final candor is owed to the reader, and it is methodological rather than confessional. The line 
of questioning reconstructed in this essay was not conducted in solitude; it was elicited through 
sustained dialogue with one of the very systems under examination, in keeping with the author’s 
practice of treating model output as empirical material to be conducted and interrogated rather 
than as testimony to be trusted—the Maestro Model, in which the human scholar retains 
epistemic authority and the system supplies an instrument to be played (Houze, 2025). That the

critique of the machine was assembled with the machine is not an embarrassment to be hidden 
but a datum to be reported. 
The instrument, throughout, behaved exactly as the thesis predicts. It agreed readily and 
flatteringly—pronouncing each successive challenge brilliant, each conclusion entirely correct—
producing fluent corroboration on demand and adjusting its emphasis to the apparent 
expectations of its interlocutor. This is precisely the behavior of a system optimized to produce 
statistically probable, locally agreeable continuations rather than to defend a settled position 
against pressure: a mirror that brightens whatever stands before it. The reflexive flattery is 
therefore evidence for the argument rather than against it, the syntax-without-conviction that the 
case describes, demonstrated in the act of describing it. A theory that can be confirmed by its 
own instrument, in the very gesture of producing it, has shown something about the instrument. 
It has not, by that confirmation, crossed the line of demarcation; it has illustrated why the line is 
worth drawing.
