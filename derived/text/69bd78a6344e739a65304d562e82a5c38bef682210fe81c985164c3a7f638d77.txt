Abstract
Microforce balance incorporates microstructural evolution into the balance structure
in continuum theories with internal scalar variables, including plasticity, fracture,
phase transitions, diffusion, and coupled multiphysics processes. It is usually
introduced as an additional postulate, leaving unclear why it should belong to the
balance structure of continuum mechanics. We show that the balance has a covariant
origin in the spatial covariance of the First Law of Thermodynamics once the scalar
internal variable is recognized not as a metric-independent primitive field, but as a
metric-dependent coarse-grained observation of an underlying microstructural state.
This covariant comparison between spatial observers yields a master identity in
which the terms induced by the metric-dependent observation procedure identify
the microstress and give the local microforce balance for the non-degenerate coarse-
grained scalar variables considered here. Accordingly, within this class of continua,
the microforce balance need not be introduced as an independent balance postulate.
The remaining covariance components of the same identity recover mass balance,
linear and angular momentum balances, and Doyle–Ericksen relations. For theories in

Introduction
The motion of material points is the basic kinematic object of classical continuum
mechanics, but many material responses cannot be described by placement, velocity,
deformation, stress, and thermodynamic fields alone. Phase composition, damage,
plastic rearrangement, defect content, concentration, and related microstructures
may evolve independently of any additional spatial motion of the body while still
affecting the material response. Internal-variable theories provide a continuum
language for such processes by enriching the state with scalar or tensorial fields that
represent coarse-grained information about the underlying microstructure. When the
energy depends on these fields and on their spatial gradients, the same description
also accounts for interfacial energy, nonlocal interactions, and internal length scales.
This point of view underlies phase-transition theories (Landau, 1937, Ginzburg and
Landau, 2009, Allen and Cahn, 1979), diffusion, phase-separation, and chemo-
mechanical transport theories (Cahn and Hilliard, 1958, Cahn, 1961, Svendsen et al.,
2018, Shanthraj et al., 2020, Bartels et al., 2021), variational and phase-field
approaches to fracture (Francfort and Marigo, 1998, Ambrosio and Tortorelli, 1990,
Bourdin et al., 2000, Miehe et al., 2010), and gradient plasticity and crystal plasticity
(Aifantis, 1992, Fleck and Hutchinson, 1997, Fleck and Hutchinson, 2001, Gurtin, 2002,
Gurtin and Anand, 2005). The present work is concerned specifically with continuum
mechanics with scalar internal variables and their first gradients. Tensor-valued
internal variables are not considered in the present development.
Classical continuum mechanics is organized by mass conservation, the balances of
linear and angular momentum, and the First and Second Laws of Thermodynamics,
which provide the axiomatic basis on which material-specific constitutive equations
are built (Truesdell and Toupin, 1960, Truesdell and Noll, 2004, Coleman and Noll,
1974, Coleman and Gurtin, 1967). These principles separate the balance laws that
every admissible motion must satisfy from the constitutive equations that specify
stress, heat flux, and dissipative response for a particular material. Internal-variable
theories enlarge this structure by adding fields that describe microstructural
information not contained in the macroscopic motion of material points. When the
internal state is spatially inhomogeneous, its exterior differential is the intrinsic one-

The temporal evolution of the scalar field enters the power expenditure through its
rate, which introduces an action conjugate to that rate. A first-gradient dependence
of the energy on the spatial differential of the scalar field also brings in a stress-like
action conjugate to that differential (Germain, 1973, Mindlin, 1963). The evolution
law is obtained only after the energetic action, the dissipative response, the
restrictions imposed by the Second Law, and, in the conserved case, the transport
balance for fluxes and sources have been specified. Although such a construction
yields a closed evolution equation after the constitutive and kinetic choices have
been made, it does not by itself separate the universal mechanical structure from the
material-dependent part of the theory. In rational continuum mechanics, this
separation is achieved by assigning stress a role in the balance laws before its
constitutive form is specified, keeping the balance of actions conceptually distinct
from the material constitutive behavior. The corresponding issue for internal-variable
continua is whether the actions conjugate to the rate of the internal variable and to
the rate of its spatial differential can be organized in the same way, through a local
balance fixed before constitutive laws and dissipative kinetics are chosen.
Gurtin’s microforce theory answers this question by treating the actions associated
with internal-variable evolution as quantities that satisfy a local balance, in analogy
with stress in the macroscopic theory (Gurtin, 1996). The theory postulates a local
balance among the microscopic actions, thereby assigning definite mechanical
meanings to the microstress, the internal microforce, and the external microforce
supply. The important point is the separation between the balance of microscopic
actions and the constitutive laws of a particular material. The microforce balance
fixes the local relation among the rate-conjugate actions, whereas constitutive
equations and dissipative kinetic assumptions determine the material response. This
separation places Cahn–Hilliard and Ginzburg–Landau/Allen–Cahn-type kinetics
within a common thermomechanical theory (Gurtin et al., 1996, Gurtin, 1996, Gurtin,
2003). The same structure underlies microforce-based theories of gradient plasticity
and finite-deformation crystal plasticity (Gurtin, 2002, Gurtin and Anand, 2005,
Gurtin, 2008, Wulfinghoff, 2017), as well as phase-field and gradient-damage
formulations of fracture (Duda et al., 2015, Duda et al., 2018, Geelen et al., 2019).
Related formulations include nonlocal granular rheology (Henann and Kamrin, 2014),
variational couplings of Cahn–Hilliard-type diffusion with crystal plasticity and
Allen–Cahn-type phase transformations in dissipative solids (Nagaraja et al., 2023,
Nagaraja and Antretter, 2024), and multiphysics phase-field models coupling
diffusion, phase transformations, deformation, and fracture (Zhao et al., 2026).
An alternative and closely related framework is based on the principle of virtual
power. In classical continuum mechanics, the virtual-power identity, together with
the requirement that internal virtual power vanish on rigid virtual motions,
constitutes a weak integral form of the balances of linear and angular momentum
and the associated traction conditions. Germain recast this principle as a constructive
foundation for higher-gradient and microstructured continua (Germain, 1973, Del
Piero, 2009, Epstein and Smelser, 2020). The construction begins by specifying the
admissible virtual kinematics and the power expenditures of the internal and
external actions, after which equality of these powers is imposed for every material
part and every admissible virtual field. Frémond and Nedjar (Frémond and Nedjar,
1993, Frémond and Nedjar, 1996) applied this construction to damage by including

Once the microscopic kinematics and their power-conjugate actions have been fixed,
integration by parts separates the volume and boundary contributions to the virtual-
power identity, and the arbitrariness of the virtual fields yields the local microforce
balances in the interior and the microtraction conditions on the boundary.
Conversely, the local relations recover the virtual-power identity, so the two
representations are mathematically equivalent under the usual regularity
assumptions. A local balance theory takes the balance of microscopic actions as an
initial mechanical postulate. A virtual-power theory begins instead with the
admissible microscopic virtual kinematics, the corresponding internal and external
power functionals, and their equality, from which the local balance structure follows
by localization.
The preceding discussion indicates that the local microforce balance and the virtual-
power principle may be regarded as two representations of the same underlying
physics. The present work examines the balance structure through its local
formulation. The local microforce balance is introduced by analogy with the
macroscopic force balance (Gurtin, 1996, Gurtin, 2000, Gurtin et al., 2010). This
analogy places the evolution of internal variables within a balance structure
comparable to that governing macroscopic motion, but it also reveals the central
difficulty addressed in this paper. The classical force balances are associated with the
spatial motion of material bodies. A scalar internal variable, by contrast, is ordinarily
introduced as an additional state descriptor rather than as a motion in physical space.
It is therefore not evident why such a descriptor should carry a balance law of its
own.
The scalar microforce balance raises an axiomatic question different from the one
encountered in the macroscopic theory, but the latter provides a useful geometric
insight. Balance laws whose integral forms appear natural and self-contained in
Euclidean space are not intrinsically defined once the spatial geometry is made
explicit. In particular, the usual Euclidean integral form of the balance of linear
momentum relies on the affine identification of vectors attached to different spatial
points, while the balance of angular momentum relies on position vectors, the cross
product, and the construction of moments about an origin. A general spatial manifold
carries no such Euclidean structure intrinsically. The First Law of Thermodynamics
has a different geometric status, because it equates two scalar quantities, the rate of
energy of a part and the power supplied to that part. It can therefore serve as an
intrinsic energy statement whose spatial covariance generates the local momentum
balances, rather than requiring the integral balances of linear and angular
momentum to be introduced as independent axioms.

Naghdi, and was later given a geometric form by Marsden and Hughes. Green and
Rivlin showed that Cauchy’s equations of motion may be obtained from the
invariance of the energy balance under superposed rigid motions (Green and Rivlin,
1964). Marsden and Hughes replaced rigid observer changes by spatial
diffeomorphisms of the ambient space, recovering the classical balances together
with the Doyle–Ericksen stress relation from covariance of the energy balance
(Marsden and Hughes, 1994). The Doyle–Ericksen relation, originally introduced as a
stress–metric connection in nonlinear elasticity (Doyle and Ericksen, 1956), was
subsequently clarified in geometric and material forms through the rotated-stress
and spatial/material covariance theories (Simo and Marsden, 1986, Yavari et al.,
2006). These results give covariance a constructive role, not merely as a requirement
on the form of the equations, but as the mechanism through which the energy
balance generates local balance laws. This motivates the corresponding question for
internal-variable continua. The question is whether the spatial covariance of the First
Law of Thermodynamics can explain why the scalar microforce balance belongs to
the continuum balance structure, rather than requiring it to be introduced as an
additional postulate by analogy with the macroscopic balances.
The corresponding question for internal variables has been approached from both
geometric and thermodynamic directions. One geometric line of work enlarges the
kinematic description of the body by assigning each material point an additional
substructural descriptor, often valued in an independent manifold of microstructural
states. This approach works well when the microstructure carries its own kinematic
representation, but it does not by itself resolve the scalar internal-variable problem
considered here. In such theories, covariance of an enlarged energy or power
statement can yield both macroscopic and substructural balances (Yavari and
Marsden, 2009). It is natural for director-like, orientational, or manifold-valued
microstructures, because the substructure itself supplies a kinematic object on which
changes of observer or representation can act nontrivially. The scalar variables used
in these theories are usually not placements into an independent microstructure
manifold. If they are treated as primitive scalar fields, a spatial change of observer
only relabels their spatial representation. No geometric mechanism is then available
from which a Gurtin-type scalar balance can be extracted.
The origin of the balance has also been sought through thermodynamic admissibility.
Generalized Coleman–Noll procedures require the Clausius–Duhem inequality to
hold over an enlarged class of admissible processes and can recover balance laws for
continua with an underlying energy function, such as hyperelastic materials (Sadik
and Yavari, 2025). The difficulty arises for scalar internal variables whose evolution is
genuinely dissipative. In Cahn–Hilliard, Ginzburg–Landau, and related phase-field
models, the internal variable is not merely an additional state parameter whose rate
can be freely varied in a Coleman–Noll separation. Its rate belongs to the dissipative
structure of the theory, rather than to an arbitrary class of process variations. If this
rate is treated as arbitrary, the dissipation inequality forces the associated
thermodynamic driving force to vanish, suppressing the dissipative evolution that the
theory is meant to describe. If a dissipative mechanism is prescribed instead, the
Coleman–Noll separation no longer extracts the microforce balance from the
dissipation inequality alone. The admissible-process route therefore cannot by itself
identify the origin of the scalar microforce balance in dissipative internal-variable
theories.

Mariano’s work comes closer to the scalar case considered here by deriving Gurtin’s
microforce balance from a covariance requirement imposed on the Second Law of
Thermodynamics under diffeomorphism-based observer changes (Mariano, 2024).
This shows that the scalar microforce balance need not be postulated in Gurtin’s
original form, but can be recovered by imposing a covariance requirement on the
Second Law. The derivation, however, rests on a strong additional admissibility axiom
imposed on the transformed inequality. When the inequality written by one observer
is pulled back to the representation of another observer, the additional pullback
contribution is required to be non-positive and to vanish for rigid observer changes.
Thus the microforce balance is no longer inserted as an independent balance axiom,
but is recovered through a nontrivial covariance requirement imposed on the
transformed Second-Law inequality. The limitation is therefore not the result itself,
but the origin assigned to it, since the balance remains tied to an admissibility
condition on that transformed inequality.
The covariance tradition of Green–Rivlin–Naghdi and Marsden–Hughes suggests a
different axiomatic organization. Balance laws are most naturally tied to exact energy
and power statements, where power-conjugate actions are identified before
constitutive or kinetic restrictions are imposed. The First Law of Thermodynamics has
precisely this character, since it equates the rate of total energy of every part with the
power supplied to that part. The Second Law of Thermodynamics plays a different
role by restricting admissible constitutive behavior and dissipative mechanisms after
the relevant power expenditures and balance relations have been specified. This
separation becomes essential for internal-variable theories in which the rate of the
scalar field carries dissipative microstructural kinetics. In such theories, the
dissipation inequality should constrain the dissipative kinetics associated with the
internal-variable rate, rather than serve as the source from which the microforce
balance is forced.
This motivates seeking, for the scalar first-gradient class considered here, a covariant
origin of the microforce balance in the spatial covariance of the First Law of
Thermodynamics. This keeps the balance relations tied to power conjugacy, while the
dissipation inequality retains its classical role in constitutive behavior and dissipative
kinetics. It also raises the question whether the scalar microforce balance belongs to
the same covariance–balance correspondence that yields mass balance, linear and
angular momentum balances, and the Doyle–Ericksen relation from observer
covariance of the First Law of Thermodynamics.
The central point is that a coarse-grained scalar internal variable appears as a scalar-
valued continuum field only after an observation procedure has converted
microscopic information into a continuum representation. This observation
procedure is metric-dependent, because the finite neighborhood over which
microscopic information is resolved has its size, shape, volume weighting, and notion
of proximity determined by the spatial metric. The resulting scalar representation
therefore inherits metric dependence, even though the continuum field obtained
after coarse graining is scalar-valued. If this observational origin is suppressed and
the variable is treated as an ordinary scalar field, a spatial change of observer merely
relabels its spatial representation and generates no independent geometric variation
from which a microforce balance could arise.

motion, stress, volume, and surface measure, but also the metric argument of the

First Law of Thermodynamics must therefore include this induced change of the
coarse-grained internal representation, together with the usual observer changes of

covariance of the First Law of Thermodynamics through the metric dependence of
the observation procedure that defines it.
With this metric-dependent observation included, the spatial covariance of the First
Law of Thermodynamics yields a master identity. This identity contains a covariance
component associated with the metric-dependent observation procedure that
defines the coarse-grained scalar state. For the non-degenerate coarse-grained scalar
variables considered here, this component separates into a first-gradient relation that
identifies the microstress and a scalar relation that gives the local microforce balance.
The same identity also carries the classical covariance components. The quadratic
dependence on the observer velocity yields mass balance, translational covariance
yields the balance of linear momentum, rotational covariance yields the balance of
angular momentum, and the metric-changing part yields Doyle–Ericksen relations.
The microforce balance is therefore not appended as an independent balance
postulate to the macroscopic theory. In the present class of continua, the metric-
dependent coarse-grained observation enters the comparison of the First Law
between spatial observers, and spatial covariance yields a master identity containing
both the classical and observation-induced contributions. After localization, the local
non-degeneracy condition permits separation of the observation-induced responses,
from which the microstress identification and the local microforce balance follow.
The Second Law of Thermodynamics remains an independent principle that restricts
constitutive response and dissipative kinetics rather than supplying these balance
relations. Accordingly, for a non-conserved scalar internal variable, the organization
of the balance and thermodynamic principles considered here comprises the First
Law of Thermodynamics, spatial covariance of the First Law, and the Second Law of
Thermodynamics. When the scalar variable represents conserved content, a species
balance must additionally be supplied. The present covariant derivation thus
provides a compact and geometrically unified organization of the balance laws and
thermodynamic principles for first-gradient continua with coarse-grained scalar
internal variables.
The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the geometric setting,
introduces the spatial and material first-gradient representations of the coarse-
grained scalar state, and states the First Law of Thermodynamics. Section 3
formulates spatial changes of observer and the metric-dependent coarse-graining
procedure. Section 4 derives the master covariance identities, obtains the classical
balances, and uses the local non-degeneracy condition to separate the Doyle–
Ericksen relation, the microstress identification, and the scalar microforce balance.
The covariance construction is then extended to anelastic continua. Section 5
specializes the theory to Ginzburg–Landau and Cahn–Hilliard models and to scalar-
gradient finite plasticity. Section 6 discusses the physical meaning, scope, and
relation to existing formulations, and Section 7 summarizes the main conclusions.
