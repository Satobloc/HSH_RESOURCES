Life as Boundary-Condition Updateability: Topological Connectivity as a 
Candidate Physical Signature of the Living State
A Companion Framework to the Volume Phase Transition Hypothesis of Chronic Disease
Tsugumi Fukushi, MD, PhD; Tadashi Hoshikawa, MD, PhD
Miyama Hospital, Oshu City, Iwate Prefecture, Japan
-----------------------------------------------------------
Abstract
We recently proposed that the volume phase transition (VPT) of interstitial polyanionic gel 
constitutes the mesoscale physical mechanism underlying Prigogine's internal time, and that chronic 
disease represents a thermodynamic state in which gel collapse has attenuated a system's capacity for 
dissipative self-organization. Here we generalize this account into a more fundamental physical 
criterion for the living state itself: boundary-condition updateability. We define life as a physical 
condition in which the junction between past structure and future possibility remains open through the 
continuous, internal modifiability of the boundary conditions governing energy flow, transport 
pathways, and dissipative degrees of freedom. We argue that this updateability is supported by 
nontrivial topological connectivity within the organism-wide mesoscopic transport field -- the 
interstitial network -- and propose the first Betti number (b1 >= 1) as one candidate, measurable 
structural signature of such connectivity, ensuring continuous loops and alternative pathways through 
which entropy can be distributed rather than locally trapped. We distinguish geometric connectivity, 
which describes anatomical arrangement, from effective connectivity, which describes the transport 
actually available; gel collapse can reduce the latter toward zero while leaving the former intact, which 
may explain why the collapsed state resists detection by conventional structural imaging. This account 
converges with, and extends, Igamberdiev's recent re-evaluation of Bauer's principle of sustained non-
equilibrium, situating topological connectivity as a candidate structural precondition through which 
thermodynamic buffering becomes architecturally possible. We further note that recent anatomical 
work has independently demonstrated the body-wide continuity of interstitial spaces -- across organ 
boundaries and along the adventitia of the vasculature and the perineurium of nerves -- providing 
empirical support for a connected transport field consistent with this framework. On this account the 
interstitium is not a specialized tissue that happens to matter, but the particular form taken, in 
multicellular organisms, by a transport field that all life requires: in unicellular organisms the external 
medium itself, at the origin of life the ocean, serves the same function. We formulate this criterion in

1. Introduction: From Gel Collapse to a General Physical Criterion for Life
In a companion paper, we proposed that the interstitial matrix of multicellular organisms 
undergoes volume phase transitions (VPT) between a swollen and a collapsed phase, and that this 
transition constitutes the mesoscale physical mechanism by which thermodynamic closure occurs in 
living tissue -- a mechanism operating at a scale that Prigogine's framework of internal time and 
dissipative structures did not address [1]. That account was developed specifically around the 
interstitial gel and its clinical consequences for chronic disease.
The present essay generalizes the underlying physical intuition into a broader criterion, applicable 
in principle beyond the interstitial gel of the specific case: life as the continuous updateability of 
internal boundary conditions. Where the companion paper asked what physical state of the interstitium 
permits or forecloses dissipative self-organization, this essay asks a prior question: what general 
physical property must any open system possess in order to remain, in a meaningful sense, alive -- 
capable of converting the structure inherited from its past into coherent activity in its future, without 
being suffocated by the entropic residue of its present?
We propose that a key part of the answer is topological, not merely thermodynamic: a living 
system must maintain a connected transport field through which the boundary conditions governing its

2. Boundary Conditions as Internally Modifiable Physical Parameters
In classical physics, a system is typically defined by boundary conditions imposed externally and 
held fixed: the walls of a container, the temperature of a reservoir, the geometry of a domain. Living 
systems violate this picture in a specific and physically consequential way. We define life as a physical 
state in which the junction between past structure and future possibility remains open through the 
continuous updateability of internal boundary conditions.
Here, boundary conditions are not treated as static external constraints but as internally modifiable 
physical parameters governing energy flow, transport pathways, and dissipative degrees of freedom. 
This updateability enables a biological system to remain globally non-equilibrated despite ongoing 
entropy production. By dynamically reshaping its own internal constraints, a living system avoids 
entrapment in local equilibrium states. Boundary-condition updateability thus functions as the physical 
bridge that allows structure inherited from the past to manifest as coherent activity in the future, without 
being suffocated by the entropic byproducts of the present.
This account does not depart from the companion paper; it asks what the interstitial gel's phase 
state -- the specific physical substrate examined there -- is an instance of. There, the swollen phase 
permitted continued modification of the thermal, osmotic, and ionic boundary conditions surrounding 
embedded cells, while the collapsed phase foreclosed it. Here, we treat that specific case as one instance 
of a more general physical criterion for the living state.
This criterion admits a more precise formulation in terms of Markovian dynamics. A Markov 
process is one in which the future state depends only on the present state, with no dependence on the 
trajectory by which that state was reached; the system, in this sense, has no memory. Living systems 
are characteristically history-dependent and may therefore require a non-Markovian description at 
appropriate coarse-grained scales: their present state carries the trace of the path taken to reach it -- in 
healed tissue, in immunological memory, in the mechanical history retained by extracellular matrix -- 
and their behaviour is shaped not only by where they are but by the range of states still reachable from 
there. We note that history dependence is not unique to living systems; glasses, viscoelastic materials,

and many other physical systems also require non-Markovian description. The distinction we draw is 
not the mere presence of memory but what accompanies it: in a glass, history is retained passively, as 
residual structure left by a trajectory the system can no longer influence, and the accessible future state 
space contracts monotonically toward the relaxed state. In a living system, history is retained while the 
accessible future state space is actively maintained -- and, crucially, can be enlarged, as in wound 
healing, immunological adaptation, or tissue remodelling. What we propose is therefore narrower than 
an appeal to non-Markovianity as such: that at an appropriate coarse-grained level, boundary-condition 
updateability can be viewed as the maintenance of a non-Markovian dynamical structure in which 
memory and future accessibility are jointly and actively sustained, rather than memory being a passive 
residue of an already-foreclosed trajectory.
On this formulation, the loss of updateability is Markovianization. As hysteretic memory is erased 
and the set of reachable states contracts, the system's dynamics collapse toward dependence on the 
present state alone. This is, we suggest, a more tractable statement of what is lost in chronic disease 
and in death than the language of energy flux permits: what fails is not the supply of energy but the 
system's capacity to carry its past forward and to keep its future open. We note, to forestall a natural 
objection, that this is not a claim that beginning from new conditions constitutes Markovianization. A 
plant transplanted to new soil begins from a new boundary condition while retaining its internal history 
and its capacity to respond to a new environment; it remains non-Markovian. Markovianization refers 
specifically to the loss of that capacity -- the erasure of history and the closure of possibility -- not to a 
change of circumstance.
A recent independent theoretical treatment has reached a structurally similar conclusion from a 
different starting point, formulating organismal aging and death within a non-Markovian stochastic 
thermodynamics framework and identifying the loss of dynamical stability, rather than the cessation 
of energy flux, as the physical signature of death [8]. That treatment develops the non-Markovian 
formalism in considerably greater mathematical depth than we attempt here, but remains at the level 
of an abstract state-space description; it does not specify what physical substrate, in any particular 
organism, carries the system's non-Markovian memory or constitutes the reachable future state space 
whose contraction it identifies with aging. This is precisely the question the interstitial framework 
developed here is intended to address, and we regard the convergence of two independent approaches 
on a non-Markovian characterization of the living state as suggestive rather than coincidental.
3. Relation to Prior Definitions of the Living State

The proposal that life consists in the maintenance of its own conditions is not new, and it is worth 
stating precisely what the present account adds. The closest antecedent is the theory of autopoiesis 
developed by Maturana and Varela, in which a living system is defined as one that continuously 
produces the boundary that defines it: the boundary generates the interior, and the interior in turn 
regenerates the boundary, in a closed loop of self-production [9]. Cessation of that self-production is, 
on their account, death.
The correspondence with boundary-condition updateability is close enough that the two may be 
read as the same insight expressed in different registers. Where autopoiesis speaks of a system 
producing its own boundary, we speak of a system modifying the boundary conditions governing its 
own dynamics; where autopoiesis identifies death with the cessation of self-production, we identify it 
with the loss of updateability. We regard this convergence as a point in favour of both accounts rather 
than a redundancy.
Two features of the subsequent literature make this correspondence more than incidental. The first 
concerns multicellularity. Maturana and Varela characterized multicellular organisms as second-order 
autopoietic systems -- systems composed of autopoietic units -- but explicitly declined to determine 
whether multicellular organisms are themselves first-order autopoietic systems, on the grounds that the 
molecular processes that would constitute them as autopoietic unities comparable to cells were 
unknown [10]. Weber and Varela subsequently argued that multicellular organisms are not autopoietic 
units in their own right. Razeto-Barry, reformulating the definition, reaches the opposite conclusion: 
on his reformulation, multicellular organisms are clearly first-order autopoietic systems [10]. Notably, 
he observes that while multicellular organisms do produce their own boundary -- skin in vertebrates, 
cuticle in plants -- the production of that boundary is not what physically maintains the system as a 
unit, in contrast to the unicellular case; what maintains multicellular cohesion, he suggests, are the 
chemical products that hold cells together. The question of what physically sustains a multicellular 
organism as a single spatially discrete unit is therefore, within this literature, explicitly open.
The second, and for present purposes more significant, concerns what autopoiesis does not 
specify. Razeto-Barry identifies as the principal shortcoming of autopoiesis as a definition of life that 
it is descriptive rather than mechanistic-causal: the property it names does not refer to causes or forces 
analyzable within physical, chemical, or thermodynamic theory, and in particular does not specify the 
thermodynamic conditions that allow the system to function as an autonomous machine, nor define its 
energetic relation to the environment [10]. This is not an external criticism imposed upon autopoiesis

from a rival framework; it is a limitation acknowledged from within the literature that defends the

What we believe the present framework adds is a candidate answer to precisely this open question: 
what physically permits the boundary to be produced, and what thermodynamic conditions permit the 
organization to be sustained. Maturana and Varela's formulation is deliberately organizational rather 
than material -- it specifies the logical structure of self-production without specifying the physical 
substrate through which that structure is realized in any particular organism. The framework developed 
here proposes one such substrate for the multicellular case: the interstitial matrix in its swollen phase 
may provide candidate physical conditions -- diffusive, osmotic, mechanical -- under which the cellular 
boundary can continue to be produced and modified, and under which cells remain in the physical 
proximity that multicellular cohesion requires, while collapse of that matrix forecloses both. On this 
reading, the volume phase transition described in our companion paper is a candidate physical 
mechanism by which autopoiesis is locally suspended in tissue, and interstitial phase state is a 
candidate material determinant of whether autopoietic organization can be sustained.
4. The Dynamic Signature: Topological Connectivity Versus Passive Diffusion
The observable physical signature of boundary-condition updateability is sustained, non-diffusive 
persistence within the biological transport field. In the absence of active maintenance, transport fields 
generally tend toward diffusive relaxation and passive homogenization; we do not claim this tendency 
is unique to inert matter, since non-equilibrium self-organization -- convection, reaction-diffusion 
patterning, and dissipative structures more generally -- is well documented in inorganic systems as 
well. What distinguishes the living case, on our account, is not the mere presence of non-equilibrium 
organization, but its active, ongoing maintenance through internally modifiable boundary conditions: 
living systems preserve directed flow across scales within what we term the organism-wide mesoscopic 
transport field, an internal medium that remains dynamically connected rather than diffusively 
homogenized.
Such persistence requires nontrivial topological connectivity, characterized by a first Betti number 
b1 >= 1 -- ensuring the existence of continuous loops and alternative pathways for flow, rather than a 
tree-like or disconnected structure with no redundancy. We focus on b1 specifically because it is the 
invariant that counts independent cycles: connectedness alone (b0 = 1) permits a tree-like network with 
no redundancy at all, whereas b1 >= 1 guarantees at least one closed alternative pathway within the

network. We note that this is weaker than a guarantee of full fault-tolerance: a single loop attached to 
an otherwise tree-like structure still has b1 = 1, yet a failure at the junction connecting that loop to the 
rest of the network can still disconnect it. b1 >= 1 is therefore best understood as a minimal, necessary 
structural signature of redundancy, not a sufficient guarantee against any single point of failure. Higher 
Betti numbers, which count higher-dimensional voids, may also be relevant to the three-dimensional 
geometry of the interstitial network, but we do not develop that possibility here. Topologically, b1-
connectivity functions as an architectural radiator: it allows entropy to be distributed and dissipated 
more broadly across the network rather than trapped and accumulated at isolated nodes. This function 
is not confined to internal redistribution. Because the transport field is, as discussed below, continuous 
with the exchange the organism maintains with its external environment, the loss of connectivity we 
describe threatens not only the internal circulation of entropy but the field's openness to that external 
exchange, on which dissipative organization ultimately depends.
We emphasize that b1 >= 1 is proposed here as a candidate necessary structural signature of 
updateability, not as a sufficient criterion for life. Many inert physical networks -- a looped irrigation 
system, an electrical grid with redundant circuits -- also satisfy b1 >= 1 without being alive. What we 
propose is narrower: that among the further conditions required for a system to sustain boundary-
condition updateability, nontrivial topological connectivity of its transport field is one necessary, 
measurable structural precondition. The full criterion for life, on this account, remains updateability 
itself; connectivity is a candidate physical substrate through which it becomes measurable, not a 
restatement of the definition.
This requirement is not a mathematical abstraction imposed upon biological tissue from outside. 
Recent anatomical work provides direct, independent empirical support for exactly this kind of 
connectivity in the human interstitium. Building on the initial characterization of the interstitium as a 
fluid-filled, body-wide connective tissue network [5], subsequent studies have demonstrated that 
interstitial spaces are continuous not merely within single organs but across organ and tissue 
boundaries: continuity has been shown between the pancreas and all adjoining tissues, along the 
adventitia of the entire cardiovascular system, and along the perineurium of the entire nervous system 
[3]. A parallel study demonstrated equivalent continuity of interstitial spaces within and beyond the 
human lung [4]. Together, these findings indicate that the interstitial network forms a single, 
anatomically continuous transport field spanning organ boundaries -- an architecture consistent with, 
though not a direct measurement of, the topologically nontrivial (b1 >= 1) connectivity that boundary-

condition updateability, as we define it, requires. The anatomical studies cited demonstrate spatial 
continuity of tissue; they do not themselves report a Betti number, and we do not claim they do.
An important distinction must be drawn here between geometric and functional connectivity. A 
tissue region may retain its anatomical architecture -- the channels remain structurally present, and a 
purely geometric analysis of the fixed tissue would still yield b1 >= 1 -- while the gel occupying those 
channels has collapsed to a state in which flux no longer passes. Geometrically the loop persists; 
functionally it does not. We therefore distinguish geometric topology, which describes the anatomical 
arrangement of the network, from effective topology, which describes the connectivity actually 
available to transport. It is effective topology that our framework concerns.
This distinction clarifies what we take the volume phase transition to accomplish. Gel collapse 
need not destroy the anatomical channel in order to abolish its function: by dehydrating and contracting 
the medium within the channel, the transition can reduce effective connectivity toward zero while 
leaving geometric connectivity intact. This is, we suggest, why the collapsed state is so difficult to 
detect by conventional structural imaging, which reports geometry rather than effective transport. 
Within this connected field, then, the reduction of effective b1 toward zero in a region of tissue 
constitutes a local foreclosure of updateability: interstitial gel collapse does not merely dehydrate 
tissue, it functionally severs loops in the transport network, converting a connected, radiator-like 
architecture into an isolated, diffusion-limited pocket, without necessarily altering what an anatomical 
section would show.
A structurally analogous phenomenon has been independently demonstrated at a smaller spatial 
scale, within the cytoplasm of individual living cells. The crowded, glass-like cytoplasm is fluidized 
by mechanical energy derived from metabolism, converting a jammed, diffusion-limited intracellular 
medium into a more mobile, non-equilibrium state; this metabolically driven fluidization has been 
directly quantified through the violation of the fluctuation-dissipation theorem in living cells [6,7]. We 
do not claim that intracellular cytoplasmic fluidization and mesoscale interstitial VPT are the same 
phenomenon, nor that they share a common molecular mechanism; the spatial scales, constituent 
materials, and driving processes differ substantially. We note the parallel because it suggests that 
metabolically or mechanically driven transitions between a jammed, transport-limited state and a 
connected, transport-permissive state may be a recurring physical motif across the scales of living 
organization, from the single cell to the organism-wide interstitial field -- a motif for which boundary-
condition updateability offers one candidate unifying description.

This raises the question of what plays the role of the transport field in organisms that have no 
interstitium at all. In unicellular organisms, the external environment itself serves this function: at the 
origin of life, that environment was the ocean. Multicellularity may on this view be understood as the 
internalization of the transport field -- the function once performed by the surrounding medium being 
taken over by an internal one. If this reading is correct, the interstitium is not a specialized tissue that 
happens to matter, but the particular form taken, in multicellular organisms, by a transport field that all 
life has required. This formulation is consistent with the thermodynamic truism that living systems 
require, by definition, exchange across their boundary: no system is open without an interface with 
something external to it. What differs across scales and modes of biological organization is what serves 
as the transport field, not whether one is necessary.
5. Convergence with Bauer's Principle and Igamberdiev's Thermodynamic 
Buffering
This account converges, from a different starting point, with a research program recently re-
articulated by Igamberdiev [2]. Igamberdiev has argued that living systems are organized around a 
stable non-equilibrium state -- the principle first formulated by Ervin Bauer -- maintained through 
thermodynamic buffering: the coupling of near-equilibrium buffering reactions with far-from-
equilibrium driving reactions, such that the system as a whole is stabilized in a regime of sustained 
non-equilibrium rather than relaxing toward thermodynamic death.
Boundary-condition updateability, as developed here, proposes a candidate physical substrate for 
how such buffering might be realized architecturally. Thermodynamic buffering requires that the 
system possess alternative pathways -- routes through which flux can be redistributed when one 
channel becomes obstructed or saturated. Topological connectivity (b1 >= 1) is one plausible structural 
precondition for this kind of redistribution: a connected, loop-containing transport field provides an 
architecture through which buffering reactions could redistribute flux, whereas its absence would 
confine near-equilibrium and far-from-equilibrium components to a single, unbuffered pathway. We 
do not claim this connectivity is strictly necessary for thermodynamic buffering as Igamberdiev 
describes it, nor that thermodynamic buffering and topological connectivity are the same object: 
buffering is a description of reaction kinetics, and connectivity is a description of network geometry. 
The two frameworks may be complementary rather than mutually reducible; what we suggest is that 
they are compatible, and that the kind of connected transport field we describe here offers one structural 
setting in which the kinetic buffering Igamberdiev describes could plausibly operate.

This convergence is, we believe, mutually clarifying rather than redundant. Igamberdiev's 
framework specifies the dynamical and biochemical mechanism -- near-equilibrium enzymatic 
buffering -- by which sustained non-equilibrium is achieved at the metabolic scale. The present 
framework specifies a mesoscale architectural condition -- topological connectivity of the interstitial 
transport field -- that may provide the structural space in which such buffering mechanisms can operate. 
The two frameworks may therefore be complementary rather than mutually reducible; each operates 
at a scale the other does not directly address.
A further, more direct link is worth noting. The buffering reactions Igamberdiev describes are 
themselves enzymatic processes, and enzymatic function of this kind depends on the diffusive 
availability of substrates, ions, and water within the reaction milieu. The interstitial gel's swollen phase 
is precisely the physical condition under which such diffusion proceeds freely; the collapsed phase, by 
reducing water activity and constraining ionic and molecular mobility, may impair the same buffering 
chemistry Igamberdiev describes, independent of whether the relevant enzymes and substrates remain 
present. On this view, gel collapse does not merely sever topological loops in the transport network, 
as discussed above; it may also directly compromise the hydrated milieu on which near-equilibrium 
buffering chemistry itself depends. We offer this as a further candidate link between the two 
frameworks, not as an established mechanism.
6. Clinical Implications: Interventions as Topology Restoration
This framework reframes the goal of therapeutic intervention in cases of chronic disease. On the 
account developed in our companion paper, interventions such as interstitial hydromechanical 
modulation (IHM) -- the delivery of a localized osmotic, thermal, and mechanical perturbation into 
loose connective tissue spaces -- act by supplying the energy required to overcome the hysteresis 
barrier separating the collapsed and swollen phases of interstitial gel [1].
The present framework adds a topological interpretation of the same intervention: because the 
interstitial network is anatomically continuous across organ and tissue boundaries [3,4], a local 
restoration of the swollen phase at one anatomical site may, in principle, increase the effective 
connectivity of the network at that site and across the portion of the network to which it is topologically 
joined. We emphasize that what such an intervention would restore is effective rather than geometric 
connectivity: the anatomical channels were present throughout, and what changes is whether flux can 
pass through them. If this framework is correct, this change should in principle be reflected in an 
increase in measures of effective transport connectivity, though no such measurement has yet been

effects appearing at sites remote from the point of intervention and following vascular-anatomical 
rather than neural-segmental distribution [1]. On this account, such interventions are best understood 
not as delivering a local repair, but as reopening a dissipative stage that had been topologically severed 
-- restoring the connected loops through which entropy can once again be managed more broadly rather 
than locally.
Avascular tissue compartments offer a theoretically useful setting for future investigation of this 
account, precisely because direct vascular explanations for a tissue-maintenance effect are less readily 
available there than in perfused tissue. Articular cartilage is a well-characterized example: direct 
vascular supply to cartilage is absent, and chondrocyte nutrition is conventionally attributed to 
diffusion from synovial fluid and, to a limited extent, from subchondral bone, rather than to direct 
capillary perfusion within the cartilage itself. Any tissue-maintenance effect observed in such a 
compartment would necessarily occur independent of vascular supply, which is consistent with, though 
it would not by itself establish, the possibility that restored interstitial connectivity -- rather than 
restored blood flow -- can support tissue maintenance in diffusion-dependent compartments. We flag 
this as a candidate direction for future clinical investigation of the connectivity account proposed here, 
rather than as a claim supported by data reported in this essay.
7. Limitations
This framework carries limitations that parallel those of the companion paper from which it 
extends. First, the identification of b1 >= 1 as the relevant topological invariant is, at present, a 
theoretical proposal rather than a directly measured property of living tissue; no in vivo measurement 
of the interstitial network's Betti number has yet been performed. The distinction between geometric 
and effective connectivity introduced in Section 4 compounds this difficulty: measuring effective 
connectivity requires assessing transport, not merely resolving structure, and we are not aware of an 
established modality that does so at the relevant mesoscale across large tissue volumes. Structural 
imaging alone would report geometric connectivity and would therefore be expected to under-detect 
precisely the state we describe.
Second, the anatomical evidence we cite for interstitial continuity [3,4,5] demonstrates 
connectivity in ex vivo tissue sections under specific staining protocols; it does not, by itself, establish 
that this connectivity is dynamically maintained, actively regulated, or subject to the phase-transition-

Third, the relationship between boundary-condition updateability as defined here and Bauer's 
principle as re-articulated by Igamberdiev is proposed as a structural convergence; we do not claim 
mathematical equivalence between the two formalisms, nor that either account is reducible to the other.
8. Conclusion
We have argued that the physical distinction between a living system and an inert one may involve 
a topological component that is not captured by thermodynamics alone: what must be maintained is 
not merely a flux of energy, but a connected transport field through which that flux can be redistributed, 
and through which the boundary conditions governing the system's own dynamics remain open to 
revision. This reframes the volume-phase-transition account of chronic disease developed in a 
companion paper as a specific instance of a more general criterion, gives that criterion a formulation 
in terms of non-Markovian dynamics, brings it into structural dialogue with both the theory of 
autopoiesis and Igamberdiev's re-evaluation of Bauer's principle, and grounds it in independent 
anatomical evidence for body-wide interstitial continuity that none of these accounts, on its own, had 
previously invoked. Where autopoiesis specifies the organizational form of self-production and Bauer's 
principle specifies the thermodynamic condition of its persistence, the present framework proposes a 
candidate material substrate through which both may be physically realized in multicellular tissue.
What remains open is precisely what a topological criterion should leave open: whether the first 
Betti number is the correct invariant, whether it can be measured in living tissue, and whether the 
connectivity we describe is actively maintained or merely structurally present. These are, in our view, 
tractable empirical questions rather than merely philosophical ones, and we hope this essay's chief 
contribution is to have posed them in a form that future imaging and biophysical work can address 
directly.
