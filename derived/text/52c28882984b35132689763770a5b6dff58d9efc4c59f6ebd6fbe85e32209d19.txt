Temporal Nihilism: The Ontological Decomposition of Time Preference 
and the Emergent Catallactic Order 
 
BRILHANTE, Gerson 
 
Abstract 
 
The persistent and systematic suppression of interest rates by central banks 
across the developed world represents far more than a mere technical failure of 
monetary policy. It constitutes a form of temporal nihilism ; the ontological 
decomposition of authentic time preference and the erosion of the existential 
structure through which human beings relate to scarcity, finitude, and the future. 
This article develops a sustained interdisciplinary analysis that integrates M artin 
Heidegger’s phenomenology of temporality in Being and Time with the 
praxeological framework of the Austrian School of economics, particularly the 
contributions of Eugen von Böhm -Bawerk, Ludwig von Mises, and Friedrich A. 
Hayek. Natural positive interest rates, when allowed to emerg e freely in the 
market, serve as the honest price signal of human finitude, voluntary saving, and 
care (Sorge).  
By contrast, artificial credit expansion and the forced lowering of rates below their 
natural level falsify these signals, generating widespread malinvestment, illusory 
booms followed by painful busts, and a collective slide into inauthentic modes of 
existence. Drawing on empirical patterns observed since the 1980s and 
especially after the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, the article demonstrates how  
decades of ultra-low and even negative real interest rates have produced serial 
asset bubbles, corporate zombies, widening inequality, and chronic financial 
instability (Chancellor, 2022; Laubach & Williams, 2003).  
Ontologically, this process denies the reality of scarcity and colonizes the future 
through unsustainable debt accumulation, weakening the resoluteness and 
authentic projection that Heidegger identifies as essential to Dasein. In opposition 
to this nihilistic interventionism, the article defends Hayek’s concept of catallaxy; 
the spontaneous, emergent order of the market , as the only institutional 
arrangement capable of preserving genuine intertemporal coordination without 
coercion. Catallaxy respects dispersed knowledge, honors voluntary time 
preference, and allows complex social cooperation to arise naturally from the 
mutual adjustment of individual plans. Ultimately, the defense of the catallactic 
order transcends narrow economic considerations. It represents an ontological 
and ethical impera tive: the restoration of authentic Being -in-time against the 
nihilistic tendencies of modern monetary centralization. By reclaiming the real 
price of time, societies can move toward responsible projection into the future,

sustainable capital formation, and  a mode of existence that accepts rather than 
denies human finitude. This article contributes to the growing interdisciplinary 
dialogue between phenomenology and heterodox economics, offering both a 
rigorous diagnosis of contemporary monetary pathologies a nd a philosophically 
grounded path toward renewal. 
Introduction 
Modern economies have entered an era defined by the systematic falsification of 
time. For more than three decades, and with unprecedented intensity following 
the 2008 Global Financial Crisis and the monetary response to the 2020 
pandemic, central banks in advanced economies have maintained real interest 
rates at historically low or negative levels. Models developed by Laubach and 
Williams estimate that the natural rate of interest in the United States has declined 
dramatically, often approaching zero or fal ling below it, with similar trends 
observed internationally (Laubach & Williams, 2003; Holston et al., 2017).  
What mainstream macroeconomics interprets as a puzzle of secular stagnation 
or a necessary response to weak demand appears, from a deeper Austro -
ontological perspective, as a profound institutional assault on the temporal 
foundations of human action and social order. This falsification operates through 
the artificial expansion of credit and the suppression of market interest rates 
below the level that would emerge from voluntary saving and investment 
decisions.  
The consequences extend well beyond short -term business cycles. They 
reshape the entire structure of production, consumption patterns, and even the 
collective understanding of scarcity and the future. When the price of time is 
politically manipulated, the signals that coordinate intertemporal choices become 
distorted, leading to systematic malinvestment and recurrent crises.  Time 
preference, understood as the universal human inclination to value present goods 
more highly than identical future goods, forms the bedrock of capital theory within 
the Austrian tradition (Mises, 1998). Far from being a mere psychological trait, it 
reflects the praxeological reality that all action involves choices between sooner 
and later satisfaction.  
Martin Heidegger’s phenomenological analysis in Being and Time provides a 
complementary ontological foundation, revealing temporality as the ecstatic 
horizon of Dasein ; the being for whom its own existence is always at stake 
(Heidegger, 1962). In this light, the suppression of natural interest rates does not 
constitute neutral policy; it represents temporal nihilism, a decomposition of the 
authentic relationship between present restraint and future possibility.  
The present article offers a comprehensive defense of the Austrian School’s 
perspective while enriching it with Heideggerian phenomenology. It argues that 
only the spontaneous catallactic order, as conceptualized by Hayek, can restore

genuine time preference signals and enable responsible economic coordination. 
The following sections examine the ontological structure of time preference, 
analyze the mechanism of its decomposition through central banking, review 
historical and empirical evidence, elaborate the restorative potential of catallaxy, 
explore broader philosophical and ethical implications, address major objections, 
and conclude with the civilizational stakes of reclaiming authentic temporality.  
This interdisciplinary approach reveals that the monetary debates of our time are 
not merely technical but touch upon the deepest questions of human existence: 
how finite beings negotiate scarcity, project themselves into an uncertain future, 
and build sustainable forms of social cooperation. 
 
Time Preference as Ontological Structure 
Eugen von Böhm -Bawerk laid the essential groundwork for understanding time 
preference by demonstrating that more productive production processes are 
typically more “roundabout,” requiring greater sacrifices of present consumption 
in favor of future yields (Böhm-Bawerk, 1890). This insight established that capital 
accumulation depends fundamentally on the willingness to forgo immediate 
gratification. Without positive time preference operating through voluntary saving, 
societies cannot extend their production  structures toward higher stages of 
complexity and efficiency. Böhm -Bawerk’s analysis remains foundational 
because it grounds interest not in monetary phenomena or exploitation but in the 
real temporal choices of acting individuals.  Ludwig von Mises radicalized this 
framework by treating time preference as an a priori category of human action 
itself (Mises, 1998).  
In Human Action , Mises argues that every purposeful behavior necessarily 
involves ranking satisfactions according to their temporal proximity. The 
phenomenon of interest emerges organically as the market price that equilibrates 
the supply of savings with the demand for i nvestable resources. This 
praxeological approach avoids the psychologism common in mainstream 
economics and instead derives time preference from the logical structure of 
choice under scarcity.  
Heidegger’s phenomenology offers a powerful ontological deepening of these 
economic insights (Heidegger, 1962). Temporality is not a neutral container for 
events but the very structure of Dasein — stretched ecstatically between having-
been, making-present, and coming-toward. Care (Sorge) constitutes the unifying 
phenomenon of this temporal existence, binding Dasein to its ownmost 
possibilities in the face of finitude and death. Authentic existence demands 
resoluteness, while inauthentic existence flees into the tranquilizing ambiguity of 
das Man.

When central banks suppress natural interest rates, they deform this ontological 
structure at a societal level. The future appears artificially inexpensive, weakening 
the existential tension that makes present sacrifice meaningful. Individuals and 
institutions are encouraged to live as though time were abundant rather than 
scarce, leading to distorted consumption patterns and speculative rather than 
productive investment.  
The integration of Austrian praxeology with Heideggerian ontology thus reveals 
time preference as both an economic category and an existential structure. Its 
artificial suppression constitutes not only inefficiency but a form of nihilism that 
undermines th e conditions for authentic Being -in-time. This dual perspective 
provides a richer understanding of why monetary interventions produce such 
persistent and damaging consequences. 
The Mechanism of Ontological Decomposition: Central Banking and the 
Austrian Business Cycle 
Mises and Hayek developed the Austrian Business Cycle Theory to explain how 
monetary intervention inevitably produces intertemporal discoordination (Mises, 
1998; Hayek, 1976). The process begins when a central bank expands credit and 
pushes market interest rates below the natural rate that would reflect voluntary 
time preferences. Entrepreneurs interpret this lower rate as a signal of abundant 
real savings available for long -term projects. This false signal induces a shift of 
resources toward higher -order c apital goods and excessively roundabout 
production methods that cannot ultimately be sustained by actual voluntary 
saving.  
During the artificial boom phase, economic activity appears robust. Asset prices 
rise, employment in capital -intensive sectors increases, and widespread 
optimism prevails. However, this prosperity rests on a foundation of 
malinvestment rather than genuine productivity gains. Consumption plans and 
production plans gradually diverge as the underlying scarcity of real resources 
becomes evident. The boom contains the seeds of its own reversal.  
The inevitable bust phase arrives when the mismatch can no longer be 
concealed. Resource shortages emerge, consumer goods prices rise relative to 
capital goods, and many long-term projects prove unprofitable. The recession or 
depression that follows serves  the painful but necessary function of liquidating 
malinvestments and realigning the structure of production with actual time 
preferences (Garrison, 2001).  
This corrective process, though disruptive, restores intertemporal order. From a 
Heideggerian viewpoint, the entire cycle represents ontological decomposition 
(Heidegger, 1962). Scarcity is institutionally denied during the boom, encouraging 
a collective flight from finitude. Debt is piled upon future generations, colonizing 
tomorrow to sustain today’s illusions. Dasein loses itself in the “they-self” of easy

credit and speculative euphoria, postponing authentic resoluteness. Edward 
Chancellor’s extensive historical documentation confirms these dynamics across 
multiple episodes (Chancellor, 2022).  
The repeated pattern of boom, bust, and increasingly desperate monetary easing 
illustrates how central banking has institutionalized temporal nihilism rather than 
resolved economic instability. 
Historical and International Evidence 
Empirical research on the natural rate of interest reveals a clear secular decline 
across advanced economies, influenced substantially by prolonged monetary 
accommodation rather than purely technological or demographic factors 
(Laubach & Williams, 2003; Holston et al., 2017). During the classical gold 
standard period from roughly 1870 to 1914, when interest rates were largely 
determined by market forces, economies experienced relatively stable capital 
formation and more moderate cyclical f luctuations. This historical benchmark 
demonstrates the superiority of monetary arrangements that respect genuine 
time preferences.  
The post-Bretton Woods fiat era, particularly after the full abandonment of gold 
convertibility in 1971, ushered in greater volatility. Central banks gained 
unprecedented power to manipulate rates, leading to the inflationary crises of the 
1970s followed b y the low -rate experiments of subsequent decades. Japan’s 
experience after the late 1980s bubble collapse stands as a cautionary tale of 
how persistent monetary easing can produce decades of stagnation while 
preventing necessary structural adjustments (Chancellor, 2022).  
The 2001–2008 period in the United States provides another clear illustration. 
Extremely low rates after the dot -com bust fueled the housing bubble, massive 
malinvestment in real estate, and eventual financial collapse. Similar patterns 
emerged globally in the 2010s “everything bubble,” where ultra-low and negative 
rates drove asset prices to unsustainable levels across equities, bonds, and real 
estate. Post-2020 pandemic policies intensified these distortions, with massive 
credit expansion and near -zero rates producing the highest inflation in decades 
in many countries.  
These episodes consistently validate the core predictions of Austrian Business 
Cycle Theory (Mises, 1998; Hayek, 1945). Taken together, the historical and 
international record strongly supports the view that central bank interventions 
amplify rather than dampen economic cycles while generating profound 
ontological costs through the decomposition of authentic temporality. 
Catallaxy as Ontological Restoration 
Hayek introduced the concept of catallaxy to describe the spontaneous order that 
emerges from free exchange (Hayek, 1945, 1976). Derived from the Greek

===== PAGE 6 =====
katallassō (to exchange and reconcile), catallaxy refers to the dynamic process 
through which countless individuals pursuing their own ends coordinate their 
plans via the price system. Unlike a centrally directed “economy” with a unified 
hierarchy of goals, catallaxy  constitutes an open, polycentric order that 
accommodates diverse and often conflicting purposes. In a genuine catallaxy, 
interest rates function as truthful signals of aggregated time preferences.  
Prices serve as discovery procedures that communicate dispersed knowledge 
across society far more effectively than any central authority could achieve 
(Hayek, 1945). This emergent coordination respects scarcity and allows 
individuals to project themselves authentically into the future based on genuine 
signals rather than distorted ones. Catallaxy operates through abstract rules of 
property, contract, and sound money rather than specific commands. It requires 
no omniscient planner because it leverages the knowledge problem in a creative 
rather than paralyzing way (Mises, 1998; Garrison, 2001).  
The resulting order is evolutionary, constantly adapting to new circumstances 
through entrepreneurial discovery and competitive selection. By restoring 
authentic time preference, catallaxy counters the nihilistic tendencies of central 
banking. It encourages responsible saving, productive investment, and 
sustainable capital formation while honoring human finitude. Individuals regain 
the existe ntial tension necessary for meaningful projection and resoluteness. 
Ultimately, catallaxy represents the institutional expression of ontological humility. 
It accepts that no single authority can master time or abolish uncertainty, thereby 
creating space for free human beings to build their futures cooperatively through 
voluntary exchange. 
Philosophical, Ethical, and Policy Implications 
Heidegger’s critique of modernity provides a powerful lens for understanding the 
deeper stakes of monetary intervention (Heidegger, 1962). The enframing 
(Gestell) characteristic of technological thinking reduces all entities, including 
time itself, to standing -reserve available for manipulation and optimization. 
Central banking embodies this enframing in the monetary sphere by treating 
temporality as a policy variable to be engineered for macroeconomic ends. This 
reduction has profound ethical consequences. Temporal nihilism fosters 
widespread moral hazard by privatizing the gains of artificial booms while 
socializing the losses of busts (Mises, 1998; Hayek, 1976). It undermines virtues 
such as thrift, foresight, and personal responsibility that sustain healthy societies 
across generations.  
Future generations inherit not only financial burdens but also a degraded 
temporal horizon. Restoring catallaxy therefore demands institutional humility 
and a rejection of the hubris that any authority can abolish scarcity or uncertainty. 
Policy recommendations consistent with this view include the gradual introduction 
of currency competition, st rict rules limiting high -powered money creation, and

===== PAGE 7 =====
potential movement toward commodity -based or decentralized monetary 
systems. Such reforms would allow interest rates to once again reflect genuine 
time preferences while supporting the re-emergence of a healthy catallactic order.  
The philosophical and ethical renewal involved goes beyond economics to touch 
upon the conditions for authentic existence in a finite world. In an age threatened 
by technological and monetary enframing, the defense of catallaxy becomes a 
civilizational task of reclaiming human freedom and responsibility in relation to 
time. 
 
Objections and Replies 
A frequent criticism leveled against the Austrian framework is that it supposedly 
lacks sufficient empirical validation or fails to account for demand -side 
deficiencies during downturns. Careful empirical investigations, however, have 
found substantial consistency between the predictions of Austrian Business Cycle 
Theory and observed patterns across different historical periods and countries 
(Keeler, 2001; Schaffer, 2017).  
The methodological divide between Austrian process analysis and mainstream 
equilibrium modeling explains much of the persistent resistance.  Another 
common objection concerns the practicality of limiting or abolishing central banks 
in complex modern economies. Critics argue that some form of monetary 
stabilization is indispensable. Historical evidence from freer banking periods and 
commodity money standards, however, demonstrates superior performance on 
key stability and growth metrics compared to the fiat era (Chancellor, 2022). The 
knowledge problem articulated by Hayek further undermines claims of effective 
central planning (Hayek, 1945).  
No authority possesses the dispersed, tacit knowledge necessary for successful 
fine-tuning of interest rates or credit conditions. Attempts to do so inevitably 
produce the distortions analyzed throughout this article. Ontological objections 
sometimes arise from those who view time preference as culturally variable rather 
than praxeologically universal.  
While cultural and institutional factors certainly influence the intensity of time 
preference, the underlying category remains rooted in the logic of action and 
human finitude (Mises, 1998; Heidegger, 1962). These replies reinforce the 
robustness of the Austrian position and highlight the practical and philosophical 
superiority of catallactic coordination over centralized monetary control.

Conclusion 
Heidegger, Mises, and Hayek collectively illuminate one of the most significant 
ontological pathologies of modernity: the deliberate decomposition of authentic 
time preference by central monetary authorities (Heidegger, 1962; Mises, 1998; 
Hayek, 1945). Through the systematic falsification of the price of time, central 
banks erode the existential foundations of care, resoluteness, and responsible 
projection into the future.  
The Austrian tradition, enriched by phenomenological insight, provides both a 
penetrating diagnosis of this condition and a coherent pathway toward renewal. 
Reclaiming the real price of time requires accepting the reality of scarcity, 
honoring voluntary saving and investment, and permitting the emergent 
catallactic order to coordinate individual plans without coercive distortion. This 
restoration carries profound implications that extend beyond economics. It 
represents a philosophical commitment to authentic existence in a finite world 
and an ethical stance against the institutionalization of nihilism.  
Contemporary societies face a clear choice between continued temporal 
nihilism and the difficult but liberating path of catallactic renewal. The evidence 
from theory, history, and ontology strongly favors the latter. Only by moving 
beyond the illusions of monetary mastery can humanity recover a healthy 
relationship with time — one that respects finitude while enabling genuine 
creativity, cooperation, and projection into a future we build rather than falsely 
colonize. This transition stands as one of the central civilizational tasks of the 
twenty-first century.
