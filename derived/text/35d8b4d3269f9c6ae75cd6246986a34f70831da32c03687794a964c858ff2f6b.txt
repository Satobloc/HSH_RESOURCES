Referee #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Ghareeb et al. presents Robin, a multi-agent system designed to automate the scientific discovery
process by integrating literature synthesis with experimental data analysis. The system architecture consists of several
specialized agents: Crow and Falcon for literature review and Finch for autonomous analysis of experimental data, such as
flow cytometry and RNA-seq. The core workflow involves Robin identifying a therapeutic strategy for a given disease,
proposing testable drug candidates, delegating experiments to human scientists, and then analyzing the resulting data to
refine its hypotheses in an iterative "lab-in-the-loop" framework. 
The system's capabilities are demonstrated through a real-world use case: identifying a novel treatment for dry age-related
macular degeneration (dAMD). Starting from the broad disease prompt, Robin identified impaired retinal pigment epithelium
(RPE) phagocytosis as a key mechanism. After reviewing approximately 400 publications, it nominated 30 repurposable
drugs. Experimental validation of the top candidates confirmed that the ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 enhanced phagocytosis. In
a subsequent iteration informed by these results, Robin proposed ripasudil—a 
ROCK inhibitor approved in Japan —which
was then experimentally shown to be even more effective. Finch, the data analysis agent, autonomously analyzed the raw
flow cytometry and RNA-seq data, identifying the upregulation of ABCA1 as a potential underlying mechanism. 
Overall, this work represents one of the first published examples of an AI system driving a complete discovery cycle, from
hypothesis to an experimentally validated therapeutic candidate. 
Strengths 
1. The system's end-to-end architecture moves beyond "idea generators" by creating a closed-loop system where the data
analysis agent, Finch, writes and executes Jupyter notebooks to inform the next round of hypothesis generation. 
2. A successful lab-in-the-loop demonstration validates the approach by closing the loop with original flow cytometry and
RNA-seq data and showing that both autonomous and human analyses reach the same qualitative conclusion. 
3. The system's focus on drug repurposing demonstrates a pragmatic approach. Identifying ripasudil, a drug with an
established clinical safety profile, highlights how this method could accelerate development timelines and address the clear
unmet need for dAMD treatments. 
4. The workflow is presented with sufficient detail, as the authors provide information on the prompts, agent architecture, and
ranking schemes used, which is crucial for allowing others to replicate or critique the pipeline. 
5. The use of LLM-based judging to infer a global ranking from many individual pairwise contests is a useful mechanism for
automating decision-making. Its implementation here is strengthened by benchmarking against human experts, which
shows strong concordance. 
Weaknesses and opportunities for improvement: 
1. The claim of being the "first system to fully automate..." is overstated. Several sophisticated multi-agent frameworks have
been described recently, including Google's "AI Co-Scientist," Boiko et al.'s "Coscientist" (Nature, 2023), and "TxGemma"
(Wang et al., 2025, arXiv). Robin's specific contribution is its focus on biological discovery and its integration of an
autonomous data analysis agent (Finch). The claim of primacy should be narrowed to this specific achievement 
2. The novelty of ROCK inhibition is clinically actionable but mechanistically incremental. While repurposing an existing

===== PAGE 2 =====
drug is a valuable outcome, the paper would benefit from framing its contribution carefully. The core finding is not the
discovery of a new mechanism but rather the identification of a new therapeutic application for a known pathway, given that
Y-27632 was previously shown to restore phagocytic efficiency (Mao & Finnemann, 2021). 
3. The reported magnitude of the ripasudil effect requires reconciliation. The manuscript should address the discrepancy
between the AI's reported 7.5-fold increase in MFI and the human analysis showing a 1.75-fold increase, as this points to
potential instability in the autonomous analysis pipeline. 
4. The paper lacks benchmarking and ablation studies, which are needed to rigorously validate the architecture. The authors
should provide: (a) A comparison against a single-agent baseline (e.g., a state-of-the-art model like o3/Gemini 2.5
pro/Claude 4 given the same high-level prompt) (b) Ablation studies that quantify the marginal contribution of each agent
(Crow, Falcon, Finch). (c) A head-to-head comparison on an open benchmark eg GPQA to contextualize Robin's
performance against other systems. (d) A runtime and cost analysis (e.g., GPU hours or dollar cost per iteration) to assess
scalability. 
5. The biological validation would benefit from more translationally relevant models. The work relies solely on immortalized
ARPE-19 cells. Because dry-AMD pathogenesis is still poorly understood, the proposed mechanism may ultimately fall short
in a clinical setting. Including validation in primary RPE cells, retinal organoids, or an in-vivo model would strengthen the
findings. 
6. The generalization claims are illustrative rather than validated. The paper lists 10 further diseases with candidate tables,
but provides no experimental follow-up, making them unproven examples of the system's ideation capability. 
7. The statistical reporting should be more detailed. To clarify the experimental findings, the authors should report the
number of biological and technical replicates for each assay, include raw effect sizes, and provide adjusted P-values. 
8. The reproducibility of the Finch agent is a concern, and its stochastic nature would benefit from further characterization
with run-to-run variance metrics. 
9. The human effort involved is not quantified. To substantiate claims of automation, the authors should provide a time-on-
task table detailing the minutes of scientist involvement required for each stage of the discovery loop. 
10. The data hand-off between agents is opaque. The manuscript does not provide the precise, machine-readable artifact
that passes Finch’s analysis to Crow/Falcon. Providing a concrete example of that prompt or data structure is necessary to
assess the degree of automation versus hidden human curation. 
11. A literature quality-control filter is needed. To mitigate the "garbage-in, garbage-out" risk, the system would be improved
by having the Crow agent assign a confidence score to sources (e.g., based on citation count or journal impact) before they
are consumed by other agents. 
12. The manuscript should ideally nclude a discussion of ethics and safety, noting that automated discovery pipelines
should incorporate guardrails to address potential dual-use concerns or off-target effects. 
Reviewer: Olivier Elemento 
(Remarks on code availability) 
The authors provide a GitHub repository for the Robin system. After getting a FutureHouse API key, resolving several
Python version and package dependency issues (including for the aviary framework), I was able to run the demonstration
notebook. The system successfully executed the initial literature search and assay generation steps. However, the workflow
failed during the subsequent detailed investigation step due to a 429 Too Many Requests API rate-limiting error from the
backend service. This suggests that while the code is available, successfully reproducing the full discovery loop may require
direct support from the authors or modifications to handle API limits. I also note that the Finch agent is not available via
github and is only accessible upon request to FutureHouse.

11. Reliance on existing literature: The range and potential of Robin’s hypotheses are 
constrained by the existing literature.
This naturally limits the ability of Robin to generate truly novel insights. In addition, its reliance on existing literature presents
a risk of overfitting analysis to accommodate existing biases and gaps in the current literature. Finch suffers from similar
weaknesses in being constrained by established bioinformatic workflows and standardized knowledge and data filtering.
This reliance on standardized pipelines may limit its applicability in emerging contexts that are driven by novel analytical
strategies used to uncover previously unrecognized biological insights. The authors should clarify the extent to which their
current application can address these concerns. 
12. Opaque reasoning in pairwise comparison to rank hypotheses: 
The inherent complexity of LLMs makes it difficult to interpret or understand the underlying logic used in generating ranked
hypotheses from pairwise comparisons. The authors must clarify to what degree end users have flexibility in understanding
or adjusting the hypothesis ranking criteria provided by Robin. 
In addition, the authors should clarify how their pairwise comparison methodology addresses potential systemic biases,
given the limited description of the underlying statistical framework. In particular, prior studies have shown that pairwise
comparisons using LLMs may inadvertently favor publications with a more assertive or authoritative tone over those
grounded in greater scientific rigor. The authors should explain how the current approach mitigates such biases, or consider
alternative comparison methods (i.e. would pointwise comparison make more sense?). 
13. Robin’s reliance on LLMs introduces sensitivity to prompt phrasing. This renders its candidate ranking system potentially
variable and limits the reproducibility of hypothesis prioritization, reducing user confidence in the consistency and
robustness of the system’s output. For example, the experimental assays recommended by the robin_full.ipynb and
robin_demo.ipynb scripts for dry AMD vary significantly due to changes in search criteria. The authors should provide user
guidelines on recommended settings that effectively mitigate the influence of prompt sensitivity on generated results. 
14. Bias towards larger effect sizes: The DGE analysis performed by Finch focuses on the identification of a novel risk gene
with a 3-fold upregulation. However, it remains unclear whether its standardized bioinformatic pipelines can effectively
analyze more complex, multifactorial diseases involving several genes with small individual effect sizes. As an example,
demonstrating Finch’s accuracy in polygenic risk score analysis could help address this concern.

Referee #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have made a substantial effort to address the concerns raised in the first round of review. The addition of
primary RPE-SC validation, ablation studies, time-on-task analysis, and agent comparisons represent meaningful
improvements. I acknowledge the quality and quantity of new experimental work. However, several significant issues remain
that require attention before this manuscript can be recommended for publication. 
1. The Finch comparison undermines, rather than supports, the claim that a specialized data analysis agent is necessary. 
The manuscript states that Finch demonstrated "equivocal performance" compared to Claude Code and Biomni on both
tasks (Section 2.3), and Supplementary Figure S18 visually confirms that Finch and Claude Code achieved near-identical
scores on both the RNA-seq and flow cytometry rubrics. The rebuttal reports Finch scores of 85.7% (RNA-seq) and 100%
(flow cytometry), but the manuscript does not discuss what "equivocal performance" implies for the system architecture. This
is the most important result in the ablation section. If a general-purpose coding agent already matches a purpose-built,
containerized bioinformatics agent on the authors' own rubrics — designed by the authors themselves — the justification for
Finch as a distinct architectural component is substantially weakened. Furthermore, the comparison is based on only two
tasks (RNA-seq differential expression and flow cytometry gating), each with three replicate runs — an evaluation too narrow
to draw architectural conclusions. Critically, the flow cytometry task shows a ceiling effect: all three agents achieved 100%
on the rubric, meaning the task is too simple to discriminate between a specialized and a general-purpose agent. When
benchmark tasks are solved perfectly by all contestants, they cannot inform architectural decisions. The RNA-seq task does
not reach a ceiling but presents a separate problem: overlapping standard deviations across agents (Figure S18) make the
results statistically indistinguishable. Together, these two issues — one task too easy, the other too noisy to differentiate —
mean that neither benchmark supports the claim that Finch offers an advantage. A convincing case for a specialized agent
would require evaluation across a broader range of bioinformatics tasks of varying complexity (e.g., single-cell analysis,
proteomics, imaging quantification, variant calling) — tasks difficult enough to avoid ceiling effects yet well-defined enough
to yield discriminative comparisons. The authors should expand the evaluation to additional task types and directly address
what value Finch adds over simply using a general-purpose coding agent within the Robin pipeline. 
2. The model comparisons are obsolete for a 2026 submission and must be updated. 
The paper uses OpenAI o4-mini as Robin's backbone, Claude Sonnet 3.7 as the LLM judge, and compares against Claude
Code (model version unspecified) and OpenAI Deep Research (June 2025). As of the date of this review, the frontier has
advanced considerably (Claude Opus 4.5/4.6, GPT-5 class models, Gemini 3.1 Pro). Given that Claude Code already
matched Finch with a mid-2025 model (point 1 above), it is reasonable to expect that current-generation general-purpose
agents would equal or exceed Finch's performance. Of course, the Robin backbone itself (o4-mini) is now several
generations behind — the system's own performance would likely improve substantially with updated models. 
For a paper submitted in 2026 to Nature, I consider it essential that the authors update the Finch comparison to include at
least one current-generation coding agent (e.g., Claude Code with Opus 4.5 or later). The step-change in coding and
analytical capabilities introduced by models such as Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT-5 class systems is well documented and
represents a qualitative, not merely incremental, advance over the mid-2025 models used in this study. Given that Claude
Code already matched Finch with a weaker model (Figure S18), the outcome with current-generation models bears directly
on whether the specialized-agent architecture remains justified. This comparison is straightforward to execute — it requires
only providing the same dataset and prompt to an updated agent — and should not be deferred to a Discussion caveat. 
3. The biological validation is convincing as a proof-of-concept but should be framed accordingly. 
The RPE-SC experiments are a strong addition. The use of primary cells from an aged donor, fluorescently labeled rod outer
segments (replacing beads), dose-response curves for both Y-27632 and ripasudil, immunocytochemistry validation of RPE
markers, and the identification of KL001 as an additional hit collectively make a compelling in vitro case. The ABCA1
upregulation finding and its connection to AMD genetics via ApoE is an interesting mechanistic lead. 
However, the authors should be careful not to overstate these findings. This remains a single in vitro assay system
(phagocytosis in cultured RPE cells), and as Reviewer 2 correctly noted, enhanced phagocytosis without concomitant
increases in autophagy could theoretically worsen debris accumulation. The Discussion now appropriately mentions the
need for in vivo validation, and I appreciate this addition. 
4. The time-on-task analysis (Table 1) is a useful addition but contains estimates that should be clearly flagged. 
Several entries in Table 1 are derived from published surveys (von Hippel et al. 2015 for hypothesis ideation; Sboner et al.
2011 and the Anaconda Data Science Report for bioinformatics setup) rather than measured directly in the context of this
study. While these are reasonable estimates, they should be more prominently acknowledged as approximations to avoid
giving the impression that the 200-fold speedup was empirically measured end-to-end. 
Overall, the paper presents a genuinely interesting system and the RPE-SC biological validation is solid work. The core
architectural idea — connecting literature-grounded hypothesis generation with autonomous experimental data analysis in
an iterative loop — is sound and timely. However, the paper's own data (Figure S18) shows no performance advantage of
the specialized Finch agent over general-purpose Claude Code, and all model comparisons predate the current generation

of foundation models by a significant margin. These issues bear directly on the claimed contribution of the system
architecture and should be addressed in the next revision.

Referee #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In their responses and revisions, the authors have addressed several key points raised in the initial review, including
reframing Robin as a semi-autonomous tool and more clearly acknowledging the role of human input in iterative literature-
based discovery. The addition of validation experiments using RPE-SC cells further strengthens the practical utility of the
approach. However, several methodological aspects would benefit from additional clarification: 
Reviewer pt 9. Opaque reasoning in pairwise comparison to rank hypotheses: The inherent complexity of LLMs makes it
difficult to interpret or understand the underlying logic used in generating ranked hypotheses from pairwise comparisons.
The authors must clarify to what degree end users have flexibility in understanding or adjusting the hypothesis ranking
criteria provided by Robin. In addition, the authors should clarify how their pairwise comparison methodology addresses
potential systemic biases, given the limited description of the underlying statistical framework. In particular, prior studies
have shown that pairwise comparisons using LLMs may inadvertently favor publications with a more assertive or
authoritative tone over those grounded in greater scientific rigor. The authors should explain how the current approach
mitigates such biases, or consider alternative comparison methods (i.e. would pointwise comparison make more sense?). 
Authors Response to point 9: We thank the reviewer for highlighting the importance of bias mitigation in LLM-based
rankings. We are aware of the ’verbosity’ and ’tone’ biases established in recent literature (e.g., Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et
al., 2023), where LLM judges may favor assertive or longer text over factually rigorous content. To mitigate this, our pairwise
comparison prompts do not simply ask ’which is better?’ but instead utilize a structured rubric that explicitly instructs the
model to evaluate specific criteria (e.g., ’Proposed Mechanism Plausibility,’ ’Experimental Feasibility’) and to penalize
vague or purely rhetorical assertiveness. Furthermore, by using our agents (Crow/Falcon) to retrieve the underlying
evidence before the ranking step, we ground the comparison in retrieved facts rather than the linguistic style of the
hypothesis itself. Importantly, our agents utilize PaperQA2 (Skarlinski et al. 2024), a retrieval augmented generation (RAG)
approach which ensures that the claims used to build assay and drug candidate hypotheses are strictly lined to evidence
based sources. 
Regarding the use of pointwise (single answer) scoring, the same literature (Zheng et al.) shows that pairwise comparisons
are generally more robust than pointwise scoring because LLMs lack a stable internal rubric for absolute grading (e.g., a
”7/10” means different things in different contexts), whereas they are quite good at relative ranking (”A is better than B”). In
general, while pointwise scores are advantageous when criterion-level interpretability or calibrated absolute scores are
needed, the literature favors pairwise comparisons for relative ranking of long-form responses under non-adversarial
conditions4. 
Regarding the transparency of the LLM judgments, the LLM judge’s reasoning is presented to the user in the “reasoning”
column of the CSV files (experimental_assay_ranking_results.csv and therapeutic_candidate_ranking_results.csv).
Transparency is noted as one of the advantages of LLM judges in the Zheng et al. paper: 
“LLM-as-a-judge offers two key benefits: scalability and explainability. It reduces the need for human involvement, enabling
scalable benchmarks and fast iterations. Additionally, LLM judges provide not only scores but also explanations, making
their outputs interpretable...” 
Furthermore, the user can read and adjust the rubric used by the LLM judge in prompts.py. The rubrics used in our
manuscript are presented in Supplementary Figures S5 and S11. 
Reviewer reply to the authors response to point 9: 
It remains unclear whether alternative approaches for hypothesis ranking were considered. This is important to clarify as
there is no clear consensus in the literature regarding optimal strategies for LLM-based relative rankings. Of particular
concern is position bias within pairwise comparison frameworks, and multiple variants exist that incorporate aspects of
pointwise comparison to improve robustness (e.g., Choo et al., 2025; Bouchard et al., 2025). Further detail on the authors’
choice of methodology would therefore be valuable. Therefore changes should be made to the text. 
10. Robin’s reliance on LLMs introduces sensitivity to prompt phrasing. This renders its candidate ranking system potentially
variable and limits the reproducibility of hypothesis prioritization, reducing user confidence in the consistency and
robustness of the system’s output. For example, the experimental assays recommended by the robin full.ipynb and robin
demo.ipynb scripts for dry AMD vary significantly due to changes in search criteria. The authors should provide user
guidelines on recommended settings that effectively mitigate the influence of prompt sensitivity on generated results. 
Authors Response to point 10: We agree that stochasticity is a inherent to LLM based agents. In the context of scientific
discovery, we believe that some degree of stochasticity is beneficial, as it allows the system to explore a broader solution
space across multiple runs rather than deterministically converging on the most obvious answers. Additionally, as we argue

===== PAGE 7 =====
in the case of Finch (see point 14 below) it allows the agent to flexibly adapt to a new problem space. We view Robin as a
generator of high-quality possibilities that requires expert adjudication, rather than a deterministic, predictable system. It is
also worth noting that variability is not unique to AI systems. Human scientific analysis is subject to significant inter- and
intra-rater variability; different experts (or the same expert at different times) frequently prioritize different hypotheses when
synthesizing vast bodies of literature. We see Robin’s variability as a parallel to the diversity of thought found in human
collaboration. 
We have provided all of the system and user prompts used in this manuscript (Supplementary Figures S2-S11).
Furthermore, these are the default prompts used in robin full.ipynb and robin demo.ipynb and do not contain any instructions
specific to dry AMD or the phagocytosis assay. 
Reviewer Reply to the authors response to point 10: 
While stochasticity is an inherent feature of LLMs, the explanation of whether this variability meaningfully parallels human
reasoning within the Robin framework remains inadequate. Further prompt-based analyses that distinguishes variability
driven by underlying evidence from that arising due to semantic sensitivity would improve user confidence in recommended
experiments and hypotheses. Therefore changes should be made to the text.

The authors have made a substantial revision within the scope defined by the editorial team. The new BixBench evaluation
broadens the Finch assessment beyond the prior two-task comparison and avoids the ceiling effects that weakened the
earlier flow-cytometry benchmark. However, the comparison now tests Finch against its base LLM without the same data
access or execution environment: Finch receives the capsule data and can execute code in a Jupyter notebook, while
Sonnet 3.7 is evaluated as a no-harness text-only baseline. The 22.8% versus 1.6% result therefore supports the general
need for a tool-using data-analysis agent, but does not establish that Finch's specialized harness outperforms a general-
purpose coding agent operating under matched data-access and execution conditions. I also note in passing that BixBench
was developed by the same group (Mitchener et al., FutureHouse — Mitchener is also first author on the present manuscript)
and that Finch trajectories execute inside the BixBench-env Docker container, so the benchmark and the system being
benchmarked share authorship and infrastructure. The manuscript currently presents BixBench as an external benchmark
via reference #29 and does not disclose this overlap. This is not disqualifying — BixBench is publicly released and has
been used by external groups, including OpenAI — but the shared provenance should be stated explicitly in the Methods or
in a brief note where the benchmark is introduced, so readers can weigh the result accordingly. I note the editorial
determination that comparisons against post-submission frontier models and agents are out of scope and I defer to the
editors on this point, but the manuscript text should describe the BixBench result more narrowly and avoid implying that it
settles the broader architectural question. The added Discussion language acknowledging that general-purpose coding
agents may achieve parity with specialized harnesses is appropriate. 
Several claim-calibration and consistency issues remain in the manuscript that should be addressed before publication.

First, the abstract continues to overstate what the data support. "First multi-agent system capable of fully automating both
hypothesis generation and data analysis," "first AI system to autonomously discover and validate a novel therapeutic
candidate," and "promising therapeutic candidate" are all stronger than the proof-of-concept framing the authors adopted
elsewhere in the revision. The abstract sentence stating that "all hypotheses, experimental directions, data analyses, and
data figures in the main text of this report were produced by Robin" is also inconsistent with the manuscript itself: the Figure
2 and Figure 3 legends explicitly state that the figures were "formatted for readability in publication by a human," the
Methods section states that "Read demultiplexing and alignment was performed by a human" prior to Finch's differential
expression analysis, and the Results compare Finch's analyses to parallel human analyses (e.g., the 1.89-fold versus 1.75-
fold MFI comparison for ripasudil in ARPE-19, and the human flow-cytometry gating shown alongside Finch's for the RPE-
SC experiments). This sentence should be removed or narrowed to accurately reflect the distribution of labor between Robin
and the human team. 
Second, the ABCA1 finding in the RPE-SC validation contains internal inconsistencies that need to be resolved. The
Results text and the Supplementary Figure S26 caption both describe ABCA1 as upregulated in ripasudil-treated cells, and
on inspection the green ABCA1 marker in the volcano plot does sit at positive log2 fold change, consistent with the
upregulation claim. However, the caption reports log2 fold changes of "-0.55 and -0.74" — both negative — which
contradicts the positions plotted in the figure and the direction stated in the text. The most likely explanation is a sign error in
the reported numerics (the values should presumably read +0.55 and +0.74), but as written the caption is internally
contradictory and should be corrected. Separately, even taking the values as positive, |log2FC| of 0.55–0.74 sits below the
|log2FC| > 1 significance threshold the manuscript applies elsewhere, including the dashed reference lines visible at ±1 in
Supplementary Figure S26 itself. The qualifier "modestly upregulated" is therefore generous: the effect does not meet the
manuscript's own significance criterion, and the very small p-values reflect low within-experiment variance at n=4 rather than
a biologically meaningful magnitude. This matters because the Results explicitly state that the RPE-SC RNA-seq
experiment was performed "to further validate the ABCA1 finding" from the ARPE-19 cells, and a sub-threshold effect of
roughly 1.4–1.7 fold is directionally consistent but is not a clean replication of the original 3-fold change (log2FC ≈ 1.6). The
text should be revised to (1) correct the sign error in the caption, and (2) describe the RPE-SC result as directionally
consistent with the ARPE-19 finding but substantially smaller in magnitude and below the manuscript's own significance
threshold, rather than as an unqualified validation. Reading the manuscript as currently written, the average reader would
not realize that the validation experiment produced a sub-threshold effect. 
Third, the RPE-SC experiments appear to rely on primary cells from a single aged donor with technical replicates (n=4). This
is appropriate as an in vitro proof-of-concept, consistent with the revised framing, but the Results and figure captions should
explicitly state "one donor, n=X technical replicates" so that readers do not interpret these experiments as donor-level
biological validation. 
Fourth, the time-on-task analysis contains numbers that are difficult to reconcile at first reading. The Introduction states that
Robin analyses "551 papers in 30 minutes compared to an estimated time of 540 hours" for a human; Table 1 reports 551
papers and 359–424 hours for the literature-search line item; and a later Results paragraph reports an end-to-end discovery
cycle of "872–937 human hours" and "approximately 825 references." If these represent different workflow scopes (per-run
literature search alone versus end-to-end case-study totals, with 825 references being the cumulative count across all
rounds rather than the 551-per-run figure), that distinction should be stated explicitly so that the 200-fold speedup claim is
not read as internally inconsistent. The 540-hour figure and the 359–424-hour Table 1 figure for what appears to be the
same workflow step are particularly hard to reconcile as currently written. 
Fifth, the new Methods text on the Bradley–Terry–Luce pairwise comparison framework is a welcome addition, but it asserts
that position bias is mitigated "by utilizing a highly distributed tournament paired with the BTL model." BTL aggregates
pairwise outcomes and estimates latent strength parameters, but it does not by itself remove systematic positional bias if the
LLM judge consistently prefers the first- or second-listed option. The authors should state whether the order of items within
each pairwise comparison was randomized or counterbalanced across comparisons; if it was, a single sentence
documenting this would resolve the concern. 
The remaining points from the prior round have been adequately addressed. The biological framing is appropriately
softened: the autophagy claim now requires further validation, ABCA1 is described as having potential implications for
dAMD, and references to expediting therapeutic discovery have been removed. The Table 1 caption and accompanying
Results text clearly flag the human time-on-task values as estimates derived from published surveys. The open-source
release of Finch with a containerized Docker implementation appears to substantially address the earlier reproducibility and
installation issues raised in both my first-round review and Reviewer 2's concerns, pending editorial confirmation that the
end-to-end loop runs as described. A final copyedit pass is also warranted — the manuscript in its current state contains
small errors that are distracting for a Nature submission, including a missing period between the autophagy caveat and the
following sentence ("Further work is necessary to validate any effects on autophagy The human version..."), "Figure4E" and
"Figure4F" without spaces, doubled punctuation ("(Figure4E)..", "(Figure4F).)"), the spelling "phagoytosis" in a figure
caption, and "dose dependant" in the Results.
