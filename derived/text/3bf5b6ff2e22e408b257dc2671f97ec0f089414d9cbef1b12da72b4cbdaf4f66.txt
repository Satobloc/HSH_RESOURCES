Abstract 
This paper analyzes the hypothesis of the technological singularity in artificial intelligence —the 
moment at which AI would irreversibly surpass human intelligence —from an interdisciplinary 
perspective integrating compu ter science, formal and natural sciences, neuroscience, and the 
Thomistic framework adopted as the logical foundation of the analysis. 
The paper defines and classifies AI and projects a hypothetical timeline of its evolution. It then 
distinguishes between types of singularity—functional and strong ontological—focusing the analysis 
on the latter. The core tenets of Thomism, its key principles, and concepts are subsequently set 
forth. The human cognitive process is explained and contrasted with what AI can produce. 
Two contributions from contemporary science that engage with this perspective are then 
incorporated: the contemporary cosmological framework (the Big Bang and the fine -tuning of 
physical constants) and Gödel’s incompleteness theorem. The recent Encyclical Magnifica Humanitas 
(Leo XIV, 2026), which provides ethical and social guidance for the global governance of AI, is 
likewise integrated. 
The result is a well -grounded conclusion regarding the singularity in artificial intelligence, 
demonstrating tha t the strong ontological singularity is philosophically incoherent within the 
Thomistic framework. Finally, the paper poses the true challenge of our era: what place the human 
being will occupy by the end of this century, in the face of a technocratic para digm that threatens to 
reduce persons to mere cogs serving the interests of large supranational entities. 
Keywords: artificial intelligence; technological singularity; Thomism; ontology; strong ontological 
singularity; human person. 
1. Introduction 
Does yo ur virtual assistant feel anything when it converses with you? If you have ever interacted 
with a virtual assistant, you may have thought: “it seems so human.” But does it really understand 
what it says? Could it ever possess consciousness, emotions, or desires of its own? 
This question is not merely technical; it is also philosophical and ethical. In the current debate on 
technological singularity, some futurists —such as Ray Kurzweil (2005) —argue that machines will 
surpass us entirely and that we will eventually merge with them. Others, such as Sam Altman, CEO of 
OpenAI, foresee a profound and gradual transformation, advocating for governance frameworks to 
orient AI development toward beneficial outcomes while acknowledging its potential long-term risks 
(Altman, 2023). From a philosophical tradition of more than 700 years —Thomism—the answer is 
clear: a machine, however sophisticated, will never be a person. 
It should be noted from the outset that this conclusion is not unanimous in contemporary 
philosophy. Currents such as functionalism (Putnam, 1967; Fodor, 1975) maintain that the mind can 
be reproduced in any substrate that performs the same cognitive functions; computationalism 
(Turing, 1950; Minsky, 1967) proposes that thought is, in essence, information processing; and 
emergentism holds that properties such as consciousness can arise spontaneously from systemic 
complexity, without requiring a prior vital principle. These positions form the counterpoint against

1.1 Methodological Note 
This paper adopts an interdisciplinary approach combining philosophical-deductive analysis, a review 
of specialized literature in computer science, mathematics, physics, and neuroscience, and an 
analysis of magisterial documents. The Thomistic framework is employe d as a normative reference 
system—not as an empirical description —which means that its conclusions depend on the rational 
acceptance of its foundational metaphysical premises. The scope of the paper is analytical -
argumentative; it does not aim to experimen tally refute computationalist positions, but to 
demonstrate the internal coherence and explanatory robustness of the Thomistic framework in 
relation to them. 
The term strong ontological singularity is proposed by the author of this paper to designate the most 
radical variant of the singularity scenario, and does not constitute standard terminology in the prior 
literature. 
2. Artificial Intelligence (AI) 
Given that no single, consensus definition of AI exists among experts, this paper opts for an original 
integrated definition that brings together the fields of knowledge mentioned above, with the aim of 
articulating a broad range of analytical perspectives. 
2.1 Integrated Definition 
Artificial intelligence (AI) is a field of computer science that develops com putational systems, 
inspired by human cognition, capable of processing large volumes of data, learning from experience, 
adapting after deployment, and operating with varying degrees of autonomy. These systems 
perform operations that imitate or are equivale nt to cognitive functions such as perception, 
reasoning, or decision-making, and generate predictions, recommendations, or content. In essence, 
AI functions as a multilevel automation: a system that organizes and executes tasks in successive 
layers, from basic data processing to complex decision -making. From the Thomistic perspective that

2.2 Classification of Artificial Intelligence 
From a conceptual perspective widely accepted in the specialized literature (Russell & Norvig, 2020), 
artificial intelligence is classified into three levels: 
• Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI), also called “weak” or “task -specific” AI: refers to systems 
designed and trained to execute a specific task or a limited set of functions (e.g., facial 
recognition, recommendation systems, or machine translation). These systems la ck 
consciousness, genuine semantic understanding, or emotional states. 
• Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) : a hypothetical type of intelligence that could carry out 
any intellectual task that an average human being is capable of performing. Unlike narrow  AI, 
an AGI could transfer learning across domains, reason flexibly, and adapt to novel contexts 
without specific retraining. To date, no functional AGI has been developed. 
• Artificial Superintelligence (ASI) : a theoretical category proposed in future techn ological 
singularity scenarios (Bostrom, 2014). A superintelligence would vastly surpass human 
cognitive performance in virtually every domain. Its study belongs primarily to philosophical 
and prospective speculation. 
Note. ANI: Artificial Narrow Intelligence; AGI: Artificial General Intelligence; ASI: Artificial Superintelligence.

3. Technological Singularity 
The technological singularity is a hypothetical future scenario in which AI -driven growth becomes 
uncontrollable and irreversible (Vinge, 1993). This process would be driven by a recursive self -
improvement cycle, in w hich an AI capable of redesigning itself would trigger an intelligence 
explosion, giving rise to a superintelligence with capabilities vastly superior to those of humans in 
virtually every domain. 
Two fundamental conceptions are distinguished from this notion: 
3.1 The Functional Singularity 
This refers solely to the exponential growth of technical capabilities, without implying an ontological 
mutation in the human being. AI becomes extraordinarily capable, but does not acquire 
consciousness, a soul, or mora l status; it remains an artifact. From the Thomistic framework, this 
variant does not pose an ontological problem: the development of increasingly powerful tools is 
consistent with human nature as a technical being, provided that such tools remain ordered toward 
the good of the person.

3.2 The Strong Ontological Singularity 
A term proposed by the author (§1.1): it posits the transformation or substitution of the essential 
nature of the human being, either because a machine attains the status of a person ( consciousness, 
intellect, soul, freedom, moral dignity) or because the human being becomes, in essence, an artifact. 
The analysis of this paper will focus on the singularity in general and, in particular, on this strong 
ontological variant, which constitutes the most radical position in the debate. 
4. Reference Framework: Thomism 
4.1 What Is Thomism? 
 
Figure 2. Saint Thomas Aquinas by the Spanish painter Bartolomé Esteban Murillo (1650). 
Thomism is the philosophical and theological current that arises from  the teachings of St. Thomas 
Aquinas (1225–1274). Aquinas incorporated the knowledge of Aristotle (ca. 350 BC)—considered the 
father of logic —and brought it to perfection, integrating it into a complete logical system in which 
ideas are linked like pieces of a puzzle, and each conclusion follows necessarily from the preceding 
ones. To understand it better: if a few basic principles are accepted (for example, that nothing arises 
from nothing, or that a thing cannot “be and not be” at the same time and in the  same respect), 
then, by applying step -by-step reasoning, Thomism leads to solid conclusions about the physical 
universe, human nature, and the transcendent. This internal structure is what leads many to regard 
Thomism as a philosophical system of remarkab le deductive coherence: once its premises are 
accepted, each conclusion follows necessarily from the preceding ones, in a manner analogous —
allowing for differences in nature —to a mathematical proof. It is, in any case, a coherence internal 
to its own frame work, which implies that its validity depends on the rational acceptance of its 
metaphysical starting points. Among its fundamental features: 
• Moderate realism : universal concepts are derived from sensory experience; things exist 
independently of the one who thinks them. 
• Corollary of the principle of causality : “none gives what it does not have”; an effect cannot 
possess a perfection not present in its cause (the principle of causality proper is formulated in 
§4.3). 
• Distinction between natural substance and artifact: the former possesses an intrinsic 
principle of operation; the artifact has a form imposed from without.

4.2 What Does the Thomistic Framework Contribute to This Study? 
Thomism offers four key advantages for analyzing AI: 
• It avoids technological reductionism. It distinguishes between operations that a machine can 
imitate and intelligence as a faculty for understanding reality, making it possible to 
differentiate simulation from genuine understanding. 
• It defines what a human being is.  It characterizes the person as a substantial unity of matter 
and its vital principle (rational form or soul), identifying which aspects of humanity are 
emulable and which are ontologically irreducible. 
• It provides operative metaphysical categories.  Concepts such as po tency/act, 
substance/accident, and the four types of causality allow for a structural analysis of the 
difference between the living and the artificial. 
• It provides a foundation for an ethics of AI.  It distinguishes between persons (subjects of 
rights) and artifacts (tools). 
4.3 Fundamental Premises and Logical Principles 
Thomistic analysis rests on the following premises: 
• Principle of Non -Contradiction: it is impossible for the same thing to “be and not be” at the 
same time and in the same respect. A door cannot be open and closed simultaneously. 
• Principle of Identity: every being is identical to itself. A cat is a cat; it cannot, at the same time, 
be a dog. 
• Principle of Sufficient Reason: nothing occurs without a cause or reason to explain it. If a glass 
of water appears on the table, someone must have placed it there. 
• Principle of the Analogy of Being: “being” is not predicated in the same way of all entities. We 
say that a person is intelligent, that a parrot is skilled at imitating sounds, and that a calc ulator 
is fast. These are entirely distinct modes of being. 
• Principle of Causality: every “contingent being” (one that begins to exist or may not exist) has 
a cause that produces it. 
• Principle of Finality : every “agent” acts for an end (seeking an objectiv e, a good, or a 
perfection proper to it). 
• Distinction between Essence and Existence : in all created beings, what something is (its 
essence) is distinct from the fact that it exists. We may have in mind a perfectly defined 
essence of a “unicorn,” but that does not mean that unicorns exist. 
• The Intellect as the Faculty of the Universal : the human being possesses a capacity that 
neither animal nor machine possesses: the capacity to abstract from sensory data the 
universal essence of things. A child sees severa l different chairs and grasps the universal idea 
of “chair.” An AI, by contrast, can only compare the image with millions of previously labeled 
examples and calculate a probability; it does not understand what it means to sit.

4.4 Natural Substance and Artifact (The Key Distinction) 
For Thomism, there are two fundamental types of beings: 
• Natural substance : something that exists in itself and has within it the principle of its own 
activity. Examples: a dog, a tree, a human being. What makes something a natural substance is 
its substantial form (in the human being: the rational soul). 
• Artifact: something fabricated by an external (human) intelligence. Its organization and 
functioning do not arise from within, but are imposed from without by its designer. Examples: 
a chair, a robot, an AI program. 
Categorical (not gradual) difference: this is not a matter of the natural substance having “more” life 
and the artifact “less.” Rather, the mode of being is radically distinct. An AI program, however 
complex, remains an artifact: its “intelligence” is a functional imitation, not an internal principle of 
genuine understanding. 
4.5 Potency and Act 
• Act: what something is now (its current state). 
• Potency: the capacity to become something it is not yet. A seed is, in act, a seed; but it has the 
potency to become a tree. 
Application to AI: a calculator has the potency (capacity) to add numbers, but that potency does not 
arise from itself: it was given by its creators in the design of the circuit. AI has no potencies of its 
own; it only actualizes the functions programmed into it.

4.7 The Soul as Vital Principle 
When Thomism speaks of the “soul,” it does not refer to a ghost or a mysterious energy. It refers to 
the internal principle that organizes a living being and causes it to act from within (Aquinas, n.d.). 
Three types are distinguished: 
• Vegetative Soul : the m ost basic, present in all living beings. Its principal functions are 
nutrition, growth, and reproduction. 
• Sensitive Soul : proper to animals and human beings. It adds the capacities of sensory 
perception, local movement, sensitive appetites, and memory and imagination. 
• Rational Soul: exclusive to the human being. It includes the capacities of the vegetative and 
sensitive souls and adds intellectual operations: understanding, reasoning, learning universal 
essences, and exercising will and freedom. 
In human beings, these are not three separate souls. We possess a single, unique vital principle: the 
rational soul, which contains and exercises in unity all the functions proper to the vegetative and 
sensitive levels. 
4.8 Human Cognitive Process According to Thomism 
St. Thomas Aquinas (n.d.) described human knowledge as a process that begins in the senses and 
culminates in the intellect. Contemporary neuroscience distinguishes functionally equivalent stages: 
sensory information is processed in the primary cortex, in tegrated in association areas (parietal and 
temporal cortex), categorized into patterns, and finally consolidated into concepts that guide 
reasoning in the prefrontal cortex. Aquinas’s model, formulated more than 700 years ago, remains a 
valid framework for describing this process (Ocampo Ponce, 2024; Martínez Barrera, 2011).

An Example: Knowing a Chair 
Consider the case of a chil d encountering a chair for the first time, and compare it with an AI 
system’s simulation of that process. 
What Does the Human Being Do? 
• External senses : perceive something brown, with four legs, a flat surface, and a backrest. 
Touch its smooth texture. 
• Common sense : the mind unifies all those sensations and communicates: “all of this is the 
same object.” 
• Imagination: retains a mental image of that particular chair (mahogany color, high backrest, 
etc.). 
• Cogitative power: without yet possessing the concept, intuits that the object “seems good for 
sitting.” This is an intelligent perception, not an instinctive one. 
• Agent intellect : the intelligence “illuminates” the image, separates the accidental details 
(wood, brown) and grasps the universal essence: an objec t with a horizontal surface and a 
backrest, designed to be sat upon. 
• Possible intellect : receives that essence and forms the stable concept of chair. From that 
moment, the child will recognize any chair for the rest of its life. 
What Does AI Do? 
AI possesses none of these faculties in the proper sense: 
• It replaces the senses with sensors (cameras, microphones, etc.): it captures data. 
• It uses data-fusion algorithms to unify information (analogous to common sense, but without 
consciousness). 
• It stores statistical patterns instead of mental images. 
• It has no cogitative power: it does not intuit utility or meaning; it only calculates probabilities. 
• It has no agent intellect: it does not abstract essences; it only finds correlations between data. 
• It has no possi ble intellect: it does not form universal concepts with meaning; it only assigns 
probabilistic labels. 
Corollary: the human being sees a new chair and understands that it is used for sitting. AI compares 
the image with millions of examples labeled “chair” and responds “98% probability that this is a 
chair.” It does not know what sitting means. This comparison is revisited and formalized in Chapter 
7, where the full ontological distinction between the human being and AI is established.

5. Contributions of Contemporary Science to the Thomistic Framework 
Thomistic metaphysics is not a closed system; it can engage in dialogue with the most recent 
scientific discoveries. This chapter presents two contributions from contemporary science that 
reinforce the theore tical framework of this paper: the contemporary cosmological framework —
which confirms the contingency of the universe and its intelligible order —and Gödel’s 
incompleteness theorem, which demonstrates the intrinsic limits of every formal system, including 
AI. 
5.1 The Contemporary Cosmological Framework and Thomistic Metaphysical 
Realism 
Two findings from twentieth-century physics and cosmology are especially relevant to the Thomistic 
framework. The first is the Big Bang theory —postulated by Georges Lemaître in 1931 (Lemaître, 
1931) and today widely accepted —which establishes that the universe had a definite beginning 
approximately 13.8 billion years ago: matter and time likewise had a beginning (Hawking, 1988). If 
the universe began to exist, the Thomistic pr inciple of causality invites the question of its cause, 
which cannot itself be physical. The second finding is the so -called “fine -tuning” of the physical 
constants: a set of fixed numbers —such as the strength of gravity or the mass of the electron —
which, if altered by even a minimal fraction, would make impossible the existence of stars, 
chemistry, or life (Rees, 1999). This extraordinarily precise order actualizes Aquinas’s Fifth Way: the 
constant orientation of natural things toward their ends points to an ordering Intelligence. 
It should be noted that the move from scientific data to philosophical conclusion requires additional 
argumentative steps —alternative positions exist, such as quantum models without an initial 
singularity or the multiverse hypothe sis. However, the contribution of these findings to the 
framework of this paper is more circumscribed and precise: they confirm that the universe is 
contingent (it does not explain itself) and that it exhibits an intelligible order, two premises that 
Thomism already maintains and that contemporary science supports empirically. The central 
argument of the following section rests on this ontological foundation. 
5.2 Gödel’s Theorem and the Limits of Formal Systems 
Kurt Gödel, the Austrian mathematician and con temporary of Albert Einstein, published in 1931 a 
theorem that many regard as one of the most important intellectual milestones of the twentieth 
century (Gödel, 1931; Raatikainen, 2014). 
What Is a “Formal System”? 
In mathematics, a formal system is a set o f clear and precise rules that allow theorems to be 
generated mechanically, step by step, without the need for interpretation or meaning. One begins 
with assumed truths (axioms) and applies mechanical rules to deduce new information. This makes it 
possible to study the structure of pure reasoning, independently of what the symbols mean. 
A computer —including any AI system —can be understood, in this relevant sense, as a formal 
system: it executes rules programmed by its creators and manipulates symbols accord ing to 
predefined algorithms.

Gödel’s Theorem: The Dream of Completeness Collapses 
In the early twentieth century, the great mathematician David Hilbert dreamed of constructing a 
perfect mathematical system: one that would be both complete and consistent. Gödel (1931) 
demonstrated that this was impossible. His incompleteness theorems establish two fundamental 
propositions: 
• First theorem : in any sufficiently complex formal system, there exist true statements that 
cannot be proven within the system itself. That is, the system is intrinsically incomplete. 
• Second theorem: that same system cannot prove its own consistency using only its own rules. 
Gödel constructed a statement that said, in essence: “This assertion cannot be proven within this 
system.” If the system were to prove the statement true, it would be false (because it asserts that it 
is unprovable). If the system were to prove it false, it would be true. The only way out is to accept 
that the statement is true but unprovable within the system. 
AI as a Formal System: An Inherited Incompleteness 
Computation—and therefore any artificial intelligence system —inherits this character of a formal 
system: it operates by means of algorithms, that is, finite sets of rules applied mechanically. 
Consequently, everything that an AI can “know” or “prove” is limited by the rules that its creators 
have programmed into it. In this sense, Gödel’s theorem provides a weighty contribution: insofar as 
artificial intelligence operates as a formal system, it is subject to intrins ic limits that it cannot 
overcome from within its own rules. No formal system of this kind can guarantee its own internal 
consistency or prove all the truths expressible in its own language (Gödel, 1931; Raatikainen, 2014); 
for the implications of this lim itation for artificial intelligence in particular, see also Observatorio de 
Bioética (2025) and Rivero González (2025). 
It should be noted that this identification of AI with a formal system is more straightforward in the 
case of classical symbolic systems , based on explicit logical rules, than in the case of contemporary 
deep learning systems, whose behavior emerges from the statistical adjustment of parameters 
rather than from an explicit axiomatics. Even so, since these systems ultimately run on determin istic 
digital hardware—governed by a finite set of discrete logical -arithmetic operations—they retain in a 
broad sense the character of a formal system relevant to the argument, although the mapping 
between “rules” and “trained weights” is less transparent than in a traditional axiomatic system. This 
qualification does not weaken the central conclusion of this section, but requires that it be advanced 
with the same argumentative caution applied below in examining the Penrose-Lucas argument. 
The Penrose-Lucas Argument: The Human Mind and Algorithms 
The philosopher John Lucas (1961) applied Gödel’s theorem to the debate on artificial intelligence, 
an argument that the mathematician and Nobel Prize in Physics Roger Penrose took up and 
reformulated independently almost three decades later (Penrose, 1989/1996), giving rise to what is 
now known as the Penrose-Lucas argument. This argument holds the following: 
• A machine (as a formal system) cannot prove the truth of its own “Gödel statement.” 
• Yet a human mathematici an can recognize, from outside the system, that the statement is 
true, even though the system cannot prove it. 
• Therefore, the human mind cannot be identical to a computational machine: there is 
something in human understanding that transcends algorithms.

It is important to note that this argument is one of the most influential in the philosophy of mind, 
but also one of the most academically contested. Among the most frequently cited objections: 
Benacerraf (1967) argued that, for the argument to work, it mus t first be possible to explicitly 
construct the Gödel sentence of the formal system attributed to the human mind; if that system is 
too complex to characterize with precision, such a construction might not be possible, and the 
argument would be unable to g et off the ground. LaForte, Hayes, and Ford (1998), for their part, 
objected not on grounds of a petitio principii, but rather identified a terminological ambiguity: they 
argued that Penrose’s reasoning shifts, without acknowledging it, between precise and  informal 
senses of key terms such as ‘proof’ and ‘truth,’ and that this shift is what generates the appearance 
of an anti-mechanist conclusion. From the Thomistic framework adopted in this paper, however, the 
Penrose-Lucas argument is coherent with the di stinction between the agent intellect and algorithm 
established in the preceding sections, a distinction grounded independently in Thomistic 
metaphysics. 
“The conclusion seems inescapable: mathematicians are not using a knowable and 
verifiable computationa l procedure to determine mathematical truth; it follows that 
mathematical understanding cannot be reduced to unconscious computation” (Penrose, 
1989/1996). 
It is necessary to clarify, with academic rigor, that Gödel’s theorem does not by itself prove that the 
human mind transcends formal systems: establishing this would first require proving that the mind is 
not a formal system, which is precisely the point at issue. What the theorem does permit affirming, 
in combination with Thomistic premises, is the foll owing: if the human mind possesses a genuine 
faculty of abstraction—the agent intellect—that is not reducible to algorithmic operations, then that 
faculty is situated, by definition, beyond the reach of any formal system. In this sense, Gödel’s 
theorem fun ctions as a logical confirmation of the limits of the artificial, while the distinction 
between intellect and algorithm rests on the Thomistic philosophical foundation.

6. Contributions of the Encyclical Magnifica Humanitas (Leo XIV, 2026) 
6.1 The Document and Its Relevance to This Paper 
 
Figure 3. Pope Leo XIV with Christopher Olah (co-founder of Anthropic) and Professor Anna Rowlands (University of

Pope Leo XIV signed t he Encyclical Magnifica Humanitas on 15 May 2026, with the subtitle “On the 
Safeguarding of the Human Person in the Age of Artificial Intelligence.” It constitutes the first 
document of the Magisterium dedicated entirely to the impact of AI on human dignity and the social 
doctrine of the Church (Leo XIV, 2026). Its contribution to the framework of this paper is threefold: it 
confirms, from magisterial authority, the ontological distinction between person and artifact that 
Thomism grounds philosophically; it  denounces the technocratic paradigm that underlies the 
singularity hypothesis; and it proposes ethical criteria for orienting AI development toward the 
common good. 
6.2 AI as Artifact: Convergence with the Thomistic Framework 
The Encyclical defines AI as a system that imitates certain human cognitive functions, but does not 
possess genuine understanding or subjectivity: “these systems merely imitate certain functions of 
human intelligence,” yet they “do not undergo experiences, do not possess a body, do not feel joy or 
pain,” nor do they “have a moral conscience,” since they do not judge good and evil (Leo XIV, 2026, 
n. 99). Pope Leo XIV insists that AI lacks a body, affections, moral experience, and consciousness: it 
can simulate empathy, but cannot feel i t (n. 99). This characterization is fully consistent with the 
Thomistic distinction between natural substance and artifact developed in Chapter 4: AI does not 
possess an internal vital principle, but a form imposed from without by its designers. Far from b eing 
neutral, it “takes on the characteristics of those who devise, finance, regulate and use it” (n. 9). 
At this point, the Encyclical may be read in convergence with the position advanced in this paper: 
the sharp distinction the document draws between hu man intelligence and artificial intelligence is 
consistent with the rejection, from Thomistic philosophy, of the strong ontological singularity —the 
hypothesis that an AI could become a person or a human being could become an artifact. The 
artificial imitation of communication can induce deception, especially in lonely or vulnerable persons 
(n. 100), precisely because superficial resemblance conceals a fundamental ontological difference.

Three principal risks of the personal use of AI (n. 100): 
• Ease of delegation: the speed of AI can weaken personal judgment and creativity.

6.3 Rejection of the Technocratic Paradigm and the Strong Ontological Singularity 
The Encyclical explicitly analyzes the transhumanist and posthumanist currents (nn. 115–118), which 
constitute the ideological substrate of the strong ontological singularity as defined in this paper. Leo 
XIV does not reject technology itself, but rather the anthropological vision that animates these 
currents: the idea that the human limit —illness, old age, fr ailty, death —is a correctable error 
through technology. Confronting this paradigm, the Pope affirms: “humanity flourishes not despite 
limitations, but often through them” (n. 118). Frailty is not a design flaw; it is the space in which 
compassion, tenderne ss, and solidarity are born. This magisterial position converges directly with 
the central conclusion of this paper: the strong ontological singularity is not only philosophically 
incoherent according to Thomism, but ethically inadmissible according to the  social doctrine of the 
Church, because it entails reducing the human being to an improvable system or a replaceable 
substrate. 
6.4 The Social Challenge: Hyperautomation, Work, and Dignity 
The Encyclical illuminates with particular acuity the true challeng e posed in the conclusion of this 
paper: not whether machines will eventually awaken, but what place the human being will occupy in 
a world of hyperautomation. Leo XIV denounces that “innovation is often pursued solely for 
reducing costs and increasing pro fits” (n. 151), and that behind the apparent immateriality of AI 
there are concrete chains of exploitation —the so-called invisible labor: millions of people who label 
data and train models for meager wages, mineral extraction under inhumane conditions, and  the 
mass control of data about fragile populations as a new form of data colonialism (nn. 173 –178). In 
response, the document demands traceability of algorithmic decisions, effective human oversight, 
protection of data as a common good, and coins the expr ession “disarming AI”: withdrawing it from 
the logic of the arms race —today not only military, but also economic and cognitive —breaking the 
equivalence between technological power and the right to govern, and freeing it from monopolies in 
order to orient it toward the good of all humanity (n. 110). 
6.5 Corollary: What the Encyclical Contributes to the Central Argument 
For this paper, Magnifica Humanitas provides three elements that reinforce and complete the 
Thomistic framework: (1) magisterial confirmation  of the ontological distinction between person 
(natural substance with a rational soul) and artifact (AI), operating on the ethical and social plane 
what Thomism grounds philosophically; (2) denunciation of the technocratic paradigm underlying 
the singularity hypothesis, which reduces the person to a cog; and (3) a positive proposal: an AI in 
the service of the human being, oriented toward the common good, in which every person has a 
place, dignified work, truth, and freedom. This horizon coincides with the  “third way” proposed in 
the conclusion of this paper.

8. General Conclusion 
Restatement of the Question 
This paper began by asking whether a machine can ever become a person. The answer, within the 
Thomistic framework adopted, is clear: no. But this is not a simple negation; it is an affirmation 
grounded in logic and in the nature of the universe. 
Synthesis of the Argument 
We have seen that artificial intelligence, however sophisticated, is nothing more than an artifact: its 
rules, ends, and functioning are imposed from without by its creators. Thomism has provided us with 
a solid logi cal framework for distinguishing between what acts from an internal principle (natural 
substance, such as the human being) and what merely executes what has been programmed 
(artifact, such as AI). This difference follows from the fact that their natures ar e logically mutually 
exclusive: a thing cannot be both at once. 
Contemporary science reinforces this distinction. The current cosmological framework—the Big Bang 
and the fine-tuning of the physical constants —confirms that the universe is contingent and exh ibits 
an intelligible order that does not explain itself, two premises that Thomism already upholds and 
that twentieth -century physics has supported empirically. And Gödel’s theorem (1931) 
demonstrates that no formal system can exhaust truth from within it s own rules; combined with 
Thomistic premises, it points to the fact that the human mind operates in a register that algorithms 
cannot attain, though it is important to underline that this conclusion rests on the Thomistic 
philosophical premise and not solely on the mathematical theorem. 
The Encyclical Magnifica Humanitas (Leo XIV, 2026) confirms from the Magisterium that AI is not 
neutral, that it can imitate the human, but does not possess consciousness or subjectivity, and 
denounces the technocratic paradigm that reduces the person to a cog. 
Final Answer 
For all these reasons, the most radical hypothesis within the AI singularity scenario —the so-called 
strong ontological singularity (a term proposed by the author), according to which an AI would 
become a person or a human being would become an artifact —is incoherent with the logical and 
causal principles set forth above. A machine will never be a person, because it lacks the vital 
principle we call the soul. 
Final Reflection 
The true challenge, however, is not whether machines will eventually awaken. The challenge is what 
place we, human beings, will occupy in an increasingly automated world, where efficiency and 
economic profit threaten to reduce us to data, to profiles, to dispensable cogs. 
Faced with two  extremes—the transhumanist fusion that dissolves the distinction between person 
and artifact, and the technocratic paradigm that reduces the person to a cog serving profit —this 
paper aligns with a third way: that of an artificial intelligence in the servi ce of the human being. A 
technology that empowers persons without annulling them, that respects their limits without 
seeking to eliminate them, that protects their freedoms rather than dissolving them in algorithms 
governed by others.

9.1 Thomistic — General 
Term Meaning 
Abstraction Mental operation that separates the universal essence from concrete details in order 
to form a general concept. 
Accident Quality or part added to a substance that does not alter its essence (e.g., a dental 
implant, hair color). 
Soul Internal principle that organizes a living being and causes it to act from within. 
Artifact Something fabricated by an external (human) intelligence, whose organization is 
imposed from without (e.g., a chair, a robot, an AI program). 
Cause (four types) Modes of answering why something is: material (what it is made of), formal (its 
structure), efficient (who made it), final (what it is for). 
Contingent Something that can exist or not exist; its existence depends on an external cause. The 
universe is contingent (it had a beginning; it could not have been). 
Essence / Existence 
Essence is what a thing is (its nature or set of defining characteristics). It is that which 
allows the object to be defined. 
Existence is the act by which that thing is real and present in the world. 
Substantial Form Internal principle that makes a substance what it is (in the human being: the rational 
soul). 
Potency / Act Potency: the capacity to become something not yet actual. Act: the state of already 
being it. 
Principle of the Analogy of 
Being 
“Being” is not predicated identically (univocally) nor entirely differently (equivocally) 
of all entities, but proportionally. 
Principle of Causality Postulates: “Every contingent being (one that begins to exist or may not exist) has a 
cause that produces it.” 
Principle of Finality Postulates: “Every agent acts for an end.” Any being that acts, operates, or moves in 
nature does so in pursuit of an objective, a good, or a perfection proper to it. 
Principle of Identity Postulates: “Every being is identical to itself.” 
Principle of Non-Contradiction A thing cannot “be and not be” at the same time and in the same respect. 
Principle of Sufficient Reason Postulates: “Every being (or proposition) has a reason for being.” If it exists by itself, 
its reason lies in its own essence; if by another, its reason lies in an external cause. 
Natural Substance Something that exists in itself and acts from within (e.g., a human being, a tree, a dog).

Term Meaning 
Avatar Artificial (robotic or other synthetic) support or body to which human consciousness

Functional Singularity Variant of the singularity scenario referring solely to the exponential growth of AI’s

Formal System 
An abstract and logical structure used to prove theorems rigorously. It consists of four 
elements:  
Formal language: A finite set of symbols combined to form formulas or mathematical 
expressions. 
Axioms: A set of formulas accepted as true by initial definition, without the need for 
proof within the system. 
Rules of inference: The 'engine' of the system. These are the logical rules that allow 
one to take axioms or other previously proven formulas and transform or combine 
them to derive new formulas. 
Theorems: The formulas successfully constructed step-by-step from the axioms 
through the application of the rules of inference. 
Penrose-Lucas Argument 
Argument formulated by the philosopher John Lucas (1961) and the mathematician 
Roger Penrose (1989/1996) from Gödel’s theorem: a machine cannot prove the truth 
of its own “Gödel statement,” but a human mathematician can recognize it from 
outside the system. Therefore, the human mind cannot be reduced to an algorithm. 
Functionalism / 
Computationalism 
Functionalism (Putnam, Fodor) holds that the mind can be reproduced in any 
substrate that performs the same cognitive functions. Computationalism (Turing, 
Minsky) proposes that thought is, in essence, information processing. 
Fine-Tuning of Physical 
Constants 
Cosmological phenomenon by which the physical constants of the universe (such as 
the strength of gravity or the mass of the electron) have extremely precise values: a 
minimal variation in any of them would make impossible the existence of stars, 
chemistry, or life.
