Abstract
The frequent presence of non-Gaussian transient noise, or glitches, in gravitational-wave
detector data can affect gravitational-wave searches, parameter estimation, and downstream
analyses. To identify and mitigate transient noise near gravitational-wave candidates in a
timely manner, the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA Collaboration employs the Data Quality Report.
In the fourth observing run,GSpyNetTree-O4was deployed within this framework as a tool
for glitch classification and event validation. We describeGSpyNetTree-O4and the main
developments relative to its predecessor,GSpyNetTree. The most important update was a
new architecture that allowed the simultaneous identification of glitches and
gravitational-wave signals when both were present in the same input. We also expanded and
augmented the training set with examples in which simulated gravitational-wave signals
overlapped with real glitches, and applied 60 Hz calibration corrections to better match the
data expected during the fourth observing run. On test data, the low-mass, high-mass, and
extremely high-mass classifiers identified 97.9%, 97.7%, and 95.4% of glitches, respectively.
Among samples without a glitch, including gravitational-wave-only and No Glitch samples,
the classifiers correctly reported no data-quality issues in 97.1%, 96.6%, and 96.0% of cases,
respectively. We further assessed the robustness ofGSpyNetTree-O4on unseen glitch
morphologies, a small set of Virgo glitches from the fourth observing run, and different
choices of theQ-value used to construct the time-frequency inputs.GSpyNetTree-O4was
successfully deployed as a Data Quality Report tool and increased automation in
gravitational-wave event validation workflows.
1 Introduction
Recent upgrades in instrumentation have steadily improved the sensitivity of ground-based
gravitational-wave (GW) detectors, including LIGO [1, 2], Virgo [3, 4], and KAGRA [5, 6]. Up to
the second part of the fourth observing run, the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA Collaboration (LVK) has
reported more than 250 GW signals [7–10], with additional candidates reported by external
teams [11–14]. With analyses of the remaining fourth observing run (O4) data still ongoing 1, the
catalog is expected to expand further. Future observing runs with increased detector sensitivity
may also enable observations of GW sources that have so far remained undetected, such as
core-collapse supernovae and spinning neutron stars.
However, GW detection is still challenging. The extreme sensitivity of ground-based
interferometers makes these detectors prone to a variety of transient noise features, known as

glitches, which can limit astrophysical analyses [15, 16]. These non-Gaussian, short-duration
artifacts can arise from environmental and instrumental disturbances and often occur
frequently [17]. For example, during the first part of O4 (O4a), a rate of approximately one glitch
per minute2 was observed in the LIGO detectors [18]. Such a high glitch rate increases the
likelihood that GW candidates occur in close proximity to glitches. Indeed, this was the case for
more than 30% of the GW candidates reported in the recent GWTC-4 catalog [10], as well as for
the binary neutron star merger GW170817 [19]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated across a
broad range of compact-binary masses that glitches occurring close in time to GW signals can
distort source property estimation [20–28].
To address these challenges, the LVK performs event validation to identify and mitigate glitches
that could impact downstream analyses [15, 17, 18]. To manage the increasing rate of candidate
signals, the LVK implemented the Data Quality Report (DQR) [29, 30] to automate the production
of event validation results. The DQR compiles results from a variety of tools designed to assess the
quality of detector data in the vicinity of a GW candidate. Despite the success of this approach in
reducing validation time in previous observing runs [17], pre-existing tools had limitations that
hindered further automation. For example,GlitchFind[31] can identify cases where glitches
elevate detector noise relative to surrounding data stretches, but is less effective when the glitch
rate is elevated for a longer period, e.g., during light scattering, which can last for hours when local
ground motion is elevated [32]. Similarly,iDQ[33–35] is extremely effective in cases where one or
more sensors witness a glitch, but is otherwise blind to glitches without witnesses, as is the case for
most recurring short-duration glitches, e.g., blips [17, 18].
Alvarez-Lopez et al. [36] previously introducedGSpyNetTree, a deep learning algorithm
developed from the glitch classification frameworkGravity Spy[37, 38], and demonstrated its
potential to effectively mitigate these challenges and increase automation in the event validation
process [36].GSpyNetTreeconsisted of an ensemble of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) that
classified inputs as one of several glitch classes, a GW signal, or No Glitch. Its performance,
however, decreased when the background-noise properties differed from those represented in the
training set, reflecting the limited variation in background noise within the training data. It also
performed less reliably on glitch morphologies that were absent or poorly represented in the
training set, including Low-frequency Lines, which became a prominent glitch class in O4 [18].
Other development pathways forGSpyNetTreehave also been explored, including
GSpyNetTree-S[39], a segmentation algorithm built on theYou Only Look Once[40]
architectures, andGSpyNetTree-Bayes[41], which incorporates Bayesian convolutional neural
networks for uncertainty estimation.
In this paper, we describeGSpyNetTree-O4, which was deployed by the LVK at the beginning
of O4 for glitch classification and event validation and operated throughout the run. It was
implemented as part of thedqr-taskspackage 3 within theDQRbuildtoolkit [30]. We also present
the principal improvements relative to the original algorithm. In Section 2, we describe the
methodology used to improveGSpyNetTree-O4’s performance relative to the originalGSpyNetTree.
In Section 3, we present performance results for the version ofGSpyNetTree-O4that ran in
production in the LVK’s DQR and enabled validation of events in the LVK’s most recently
published catalogs [10, 42], as well as future LVK catalogs expected within the year. In Section 4,
we describe pathways for future improvement toward full automation of GW candidate validation.
In Section 5, we summarize our conclusions.
2GSpyNetTree-O4improvements
GSpyNetTree-O4follows the architectural philosophy of its predecessor [36]. It consists of a
decision tree of three InceptionV3 [43] CNNs, trained on simulated GW signals and
morphologically similar glitches. BothGSpyNetTreeandGSpyNetTree-O4use a feature set similar
toGravity Spy[37, 38, 44–46]: each input sample is composed of a 2×2 matrix of spectrogram
visualizations that are 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 seconds in duration. To capture the different morphologies
between short-duration and long-duration glitches and GW sources, as recommended by Jarov
et al. [47], different models were deployed depending on a GW candidate’s estimated total mass,
M: the Low-mass (LM) classifier handled events withM <50M ⊙, the High-mass (HM) classifier
covered events in the 50M ⊙≤M <250M ⊙ range, and the Extremely high-mass (EHM) classifier
was used for events withM≥250M ⊙.

Building onGSpyNetTree’s original architecture, we developedGSpyNetTree-O4by
implementing several key recommendations from Alvarez-Lopez et al. [36]. These enhancements
madeGSpyNetTree-O4more robust to variations in background noise and GWs occurring in close
proximity to glitches. In the following sections, we expand on the implementation of these
improvements, along with other upgrades introduced inGSpyNetTree-O4.
2.1 Construction of a representative and comprehensive glitch sample set
In Alvarez-Lopez et al. [36], all glitches in the training set were obtained through LIGO-DV
web [59], and the Gravitational Wave Open Science Center (GWOSC) [60–62]. In this work, we
continued to use these glitches forGSpyNetTree-O4, but we increased the number of glitch samples
in the training set in two ways. First, we included glitches found in Virgo data from the third
observing run (O3). AlthoughGSpyNetTree-O4was deployed on LIGO data, Virgo glitches in the
classes considered here exhibit morphologies similar to their LIGO counterparts. Including these
samples allowed us to improve robustness to subtle morphological variations within the same glitch
category. Second, we increased the number of glitches in our training set by including 193 manually
verified glitches from non-quiet data segments flagged by the algorithm described in Section 2.2.
We included only glitches with excess power above 10 Hz. Figure 2a shows an example of a Koi
Fish glitch identified in this way during O3 at LIGO Livingston Observatory (LLO). We also found
a considerable number of Virgo noise transients below 10 Hz, one of which is shown in Figure 2b.
We did not include these low-frequency transients becauseGSpyNetTree-O4was developed
primarily to run on LIGO data. A future version designed for Virgo data will require an expanded
training set that includes these types of transients.

2.2 Construction of a GW set in representative background noise
To generate GW samples, we simulated signals and injected them into quiet (i.e., glitch-free) time
series detector data. ForGSpyNetTree, all quiet segments were manually selected from a small set
of manually inspected 64-second time series data segments from LHO and LLO. However, the
selected time series segments only covered a few days during the first (O1) and second (O2)
observing runs [36] and were not representative of the background noise of an entire observing run.
Changes in detector configurations or environmental disturbances (e.g., earthquakes) may produce
long-term variations in the background noise [18], which may be reflected in the spectrograms used
as inputs toGSpyNetTree-O4. In fact, Alvarez-Lopez et al. [36] found that the performance of
GSpyNetTreestrongly depended on background noise.

Ensuring robustness to changes in background noise (i.e., steady-state, Gaussian detector noise)
was a central focus of the O4 implementation ofGSpyNetTree. ForGSpyNetTree-O4, we expanded
the range of quiet time series segments used as background noise. In addition to the quiet data
segments from O1 and O2 used inGSpyNetTree, we implemented a method that automatically
identified quiet O3 data segments into which we could inject simulated GW signals. Our method
for identifying quiet time series segments is similar to that of theGlitchFindDQR task [18, 30].
We calculated the energy of the time-frequency tiles of a spectrogram and verified Gaussianity by
fitting an exponential distribution to the energy of the time-frequency tiles. If the normalized
energy of four consecutive time-frequency bins exceeded 24, the time series segment corresponding
to the spectrogram was flagged as a segment containing a glitch. This value was determined
empirically. Otherwise, the time series segment was considered to be ‘quiet’ and was used for
injecting GW signals.
Using this procedure, we obtained 984, 980, and 958 distinct ten-second quiet time series
segments from O3 for LHO, LLO, and Virgo, respectively. This considerably increased the number
of quiet time series segments available. While the development ofGSpyNetTree-O4was focused
primarily on the implementation for LIGO detectors (GSpyNetTree-O4did not run on Virgo data),
including quiet Virgo time series segments madeGSpyNetTree-O4more robust to changes in
background noise. Figures 2c and 2d show 10-second spectrograms of ‘quiet’ time identified by the
algorithm from LHO and LLO, respectively.
After quiet time series segments were identified, we simulated and injected GW signals using a
procedure similar to Alvarez-Lopez et al. [36]. We first sampled parameters uniformly in the
following ranges: total source-frame massMbetween 5M ⊙ and 350M ⊙, with individual
component source-frame massesm 1,m 2 ranging from 2M ⊙ to 175M ⊙; signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
between 8 and 35; individual dimensionless component spinsa 1,a 2 between 0.05 and 0.95. We also
randomized GW source orientations and sky locations across the entire sky. This was an
improvement over the data set forGSpyNetTree, which assumed the optimal orientation of GW
signals with respect to detectors. GW signals were then simulated usingLALSuite[64] and the
IMRPhenomPv2waveform model [65, 66]. In total, we simulated 1800, 2100, and 1500 GWs for the
LM, HM, and the EHM classifiers, respectively, which were then injected into the time series
obtained above.
To mitigate overfitting, we applied the data augmentation techniques suggested by Jarov et al.
[47]. For each simulated GW signal, we generated four additional copies by randomly shifting the
simulated GW signal in time. The time offsets were drawn from a uniform distribution over±0.5 s
relative to the merger time. Having samples that were not always perfectly centered in the
spectrograms madeGSpyNetTree-O4robust to small offsets in candidate merger times, as first
reported in Alvarez-Lopez et al. [36].
2.3 Construction of an overlapping set of GWs and glitches
With increased detector sensitivity and a higher detection rate, the probability of GWs overlapping
with or occurring in close proximity to glitches increases. This scenario already occurred with more
than 20% of candidates during O3 [8, 9], and more than 30% of candidates in the first catalog
released from O4 data [10]. This issue has remained relevant throughout O4 [18].
As described in Alvarez-Lopez et al. [36],GSpyNetTreefollowed a multi-class architecture. This
meant that, given an input, the classifiers could only predict a single class, which was assumed to
be independent and mutually exclusive from the others. When tested on overlapping samples of
GWs and glitches,GSpyNetTreeclassified more than 60% (70% for the HM classifier) of GW
signals as only glitches [36]. By identifying glitches as well as GWs in close proximity,
GSpyNetTree-O4could also inform event retraction and enable timely decisions for critical
detector-characterization tasks like glitch mitigation [17, 18].
To enable simultaneous identification of both glitches and GWs present in the same input, we
generated samples of overlapping GWs and glitches for training and testingGSpyNetTree-O4. We
also modifiedGSpyNetTree-O4’s classifier architecture to enable the simultaneous identification of
glitches and GWs. In this section, we discuss the generation of overlapping samples. The
architecture changes made to the classifiers are discussed in the next section.
To test the performance ofGSpyNetTreeon samples where GW signals overlapped with a
glitch, Alvarez-Lopez et al. [36] generated 30 such samples for each glitch type. Each of them was
created and manually verified by simulating 3 GW signals, and shifting them with respect to the
glitch, using 10 time offsets drawn from a Normal distribution with meanµ= 0 s and standard
deviationσ= 0.25 s. With these parameters, a non-negligible number of glitches overlapped with
the injected GWs. In some cases, this led to saturated spectrogram visualizations, where the GW

ForGSpyNetTree-O4, we designed a new method to inject GW signals near glitches. We
selected 30 random examples of each glitch class and calculated their average duration, as shown in
Table 2. We targeted the same common glitch classes as described in Alvarez-Lopez et al. [36]. We
found that glitches related to light scattering (e.g., Scratchy, Fast Scattering, and Light Scattering
glitches [18, 32, 67]), were all long in duration (>1.5 s). However, compared to GWs, they
occurred at rather low frequencies (except for Scratchy glitches, which in turn had lower SNRs). In
contrast, glitches related to a sharp, step-function-like change in the time series data (e.g., Blips,
Low-frequency Blips, Tomte, and Koi Fish glitches [18, 68]) were shorter in duration (<1 s) but
could be found in a broader frequency range.
To generate samples of overlapping GWs and glitches, we first determined whether the glitch
was short (Blip, Low-frequency Blip, Tomte, or Koi Fish) or long (Fast Scattering, Scratchy, or
Light Scattering). If short, we generated two samples per GW signal: one sample with the glitch
before the GW and one with the glitch after the GW signal. The offset in time between the GW
signal and each glitch was drawn from a uniform distribution. For each sample, we ensured that
the offset was large enough such that the glitches were not added directly on top of the GW signal.
If the glitch was long, we generated three samples per GW signal: in addition to the two samples
described above, we added one glitch exactly at the GW merger time. The reason we included this
extra sample is twofold: first, it allowed us to represent in the data set the fact that long glitches
like Light Scattering and Fast Scattering were the most common during O3 [17, 63]. Second, since
they all occurred at low frequencies and/or had low SNR, the probability that the resulting
spectrogram would be over-saturated was low.
To further minimize the probability of fully overlapping GWs and glitches, we standardized the
Q-value of all our spectrograms toQ= 20. For some samples, fixing theQ-value atQ= 20 limited
the energy per tile in our time-frequency visualizations, thereby lowering the likelihood of
spectrogram saturation due to excess power. Additionally, it improved the visualization of
low-mass GW signals, increasing the probability that these signals were correctly identified. In
Section 4.2, we tested the impact of samples generated using differentQ-values on the performance
of the classifiers.

80% of the entire data set for training (20% of which was used for validation). The remaining
samples were used for testing.
2.4 Architecture
ForGSpyNetTree-O4’s three classifiers, we implemented a multi-label architecture, which allowed
for the simultaneous identification of multiple classes within the same input [69]. We kept the
InceptionV3 [43] model fromGSpyNetTreedue to the high accuracy achieved in Alvarez-Lopez
et al. [36]. To enable multi-label classification, we changed the output-layer activation function
fromsoftmaxtosigmoidand optimized a binary cross-entropy loss for each output. To convert
the output probabilities into predicted labels for evaluation, we treated a class as present when its
probability exceeded 50%. We monitored recall rather than single-label accuracy during training.
Recall is defined as the ratio of true positives to the sum of true positives and false negatives. A
false negative occurs whenGSpyNetTree-O4fails to identify one or more objects present in the
input; for example, when it detects no object in an input that contains one, or detects fewer than
all objects in an input that contains multiple objects. By using recall as the evaluation metric, we
directly quantified the fraction of glitches or GW signals that the classifier identified. The change
from multi-class architecture to multi-label architecture was the most significant upgrade we
included in O4, and it resulted in dramatic improvements in glitch identification in the presence of
a GW candidate. We discuss our results in the next section.
3 Results
The primary objective ofGSpyNetTree-O4as a DQR task was to identify data quality (DQ) issues:
glitches in the vicinity of a GW candidate. In this section, we evaluate the performance of
GSpyNetTree-O4on the test set, which consisted of 11960, 13216, and 9891 samples for the LM,
HM, and EHM classifiers, respectively. Throughout the remainder of this paper, we used a

samples. For all other classes (Light Scattering, Fast Scattering, Low-frequency Lines, and

set, for which more than 95% of glitches were correctly detected (see Figure 5). Across the Virgo
test set, more than 34%, 42%, and 51% of the samples analyzed by the LM, HM, and EHM
classifiers, respectively, were incorrectly classified as containing no glitch. These false-negative rates
are considerably higher than those shown in Figure 5 (2.1%, 2.3%, and 4.6% for each classifier,
respectively).
These results suggest that a robust application to Virgo data would require a larger, more
representative Virgo-specific training set. A representative Virgo training set may include samples
of low-frequency noise, such as the one shown in Figure 2b. In Section 2.2, we found that such
low-frequency noise was common in Virgo during O3, as first reported in Acernese et al. [4]. Future
developments could consider building larger Virgo glitch data sets using Virgo-focused pipelines,
such asVIGILant[76].
5 Conclusions
In this paper, we describedGSpyNetTree-O4, a machine learning algorithm for LVK event
validation that was deployed in production as a DQR tool during O4. Building uponGSpyNetTree,
18

===== PAGE 19 =====
Preprint ´Alvarez-L´ opezet al.
GSpyNetTree-O4consisted of an ensemble of three CNNs, referred to as the Low-mass (LM),
High-mass (HM), and Extremely high-mass (EHM) classifiers, respectively. These classifiers, based
on the InceptionV3 architecture [43], were designed to target GW candidates with total mass in
different ranges as well as glitches morphologically similar to GWs in each corresponding mass
range.
To developGSpyNetTree-O4, we implemented a series of modifications and improvements
relative toGSpyNetTree, for both data generation and the classifier architecture. First, we
expanded the set of background time series segments from LHO, LLO and Virgo by verifying the
Gaussianity of the segments. This allowed us to not only add simulated GW signals in more
representative background noise, but also to increase the number of glitches in our data set.
Moreover, the most significant upgrade for data generation included the construction of a data set
where glitches occurred in the proximity of GW signals. This data set was designed to mimic the
increasingly likely scenarios where GW signals overlap with glitches as detector sensitivity
increases. In addition, we applied calibration at 60 Hz to remove line artifacts, doubling the
number of LHO and LLO samples in our data set. These improvements and modifications allowed
us to construct a data set that was more representative of the data expected in O4. Finally, to
enable simultaneous identification of GWs and glitches in a single input, we implemented a
multi-label architecture for the LM, HM and EHM classifiers ofGSpyNetTree-O4, which was one of
the most significant upgrades introduced in this work.
On the test sets,GSpyNetTree-O4achieved glitch true-alarm rates>95%. Among samples with
no true DQ issue, including GW-only and No Glitch samples,GSpyNetTree-O4correctly reported
no DQ issue in 97.1%, 96.6%, and 96.0% of cases for the LM, HM, and EHM classifiers, respectively.
In the case of GWs, particularly in the LM regime, low-SNR signals were often misclassified as No
Glitch. Likewise, when a low-SNR GW signal occurred near a glitch,GSpyNetTree-O4typically
detected only the glitch. Although this was sufficient forGSpyNetTree-O4’s use case (i.e.,
identifying glitches responsible for or in the proximity of candidate events), in future work, we plan
to develop improved classification techniques for fainter GW signals.
We further assessedGSpyNetTree-O4’s ability to detect glitch types that were not included in
the training set. We found that it generalized well to glitches that were morphologically similar to
those in its training set, correctly identifying more than 95% of them as glitches. We also found
similar performance when testingGSpyNetTree-O4on glitches characterized by repeated excess
power with morphologies resembling those in the training set (e.g., Repeating Blips). However, we
found poor performance on high-frequency glitches, primarily becauseGSpyNetTree-O4’s training
set did not include these types of glitches.
We also evaluatedGSpyNetTree-O4’s performance on a small set of Virgo glitches from O4 as a
preliminary study of its transferability to Virgo data in future observing runs. Because
GSpyNetTree-O4was developed primarily for LIGO data, most of the training samples came from
the LIGO detectors. Additionally, the tested Virgo glitches were morphologically different from
glitches in the training set. As a result,GSpyNetTree-O4did not generalize well to the tested Virgo
glitches. However, the development of a training set with sufficient and representative samples from
Virgo is expected to improve the performance ofGSpyNetTree-O4for Virgo.
Moreover, we investigated the impact of differentQ-values used for generating the training set
for the LM and HM classifiers. We found that theQ-value employed in this work (i.e., 20) was the
optimal for the LM classifier among the tested values, while the optimal value for the HM classifier
was 30. Overall, the effect of theQ-value was small.
During O4,GSpyNetTree-O4was successfully deployed as a DQR tool. It demonstrated
promising classification performance and increased the degree of automation available to GW event
validation workflows.
