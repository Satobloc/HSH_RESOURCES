Panagnosticism: The Epistemic Impossibility of
Knowing Another’s Consciousness
Abstract
We formulate and prove the thesis ofpanagnosticism: the assertion that no agent
can have knowledge of whether any distinct entity is conscious. Using standard modal-
epistemic logic, we define an agent-indexed knowledge operatorK a and a predicate
C(x) for ”xis conscious.” Under minimal assumptions (factivity and introspection
for knowledge; privacy of experience; absence of any a priori entailment from physi-
cal/behavioral evidence to consciousness), we show that for any agentaand any distinct
entitye, neitherK aC(e) norK a¬C(e) can hold.
Intuitively, one can always posit a ”zombie” or alternate world in whiche’s con-
sciousness differs while all ofa’s evidence stays the same, defeating any claim to knowl-
edge. Formally, for each agentaand distincte, there exist two epistemically indistin-
guishable worlds (froma’s perspective) in whichC(e) is true in one and false in the
other. It follows thatacannot knowC(e) (nor¬C(e)).
We address and refute major objections: neural/behavioral evidence, mutual ver-
ification, epistemic closure, and bridge principles. We contrast panagnosticism with
panpsychism, solipsism, eliminativism, Cartesian skepticism, and proposed AI con-
sciousness tests, showing that panagnosticism is agnostic about ontological claims but
categorical about epistemic limits. Implications for AI, ethics, and metaphysics are
discussed. Our main theorem is presented formally and proved deductively using epis-
temic modal logic (S5) with knowledge operators. To our knowledge, this is the first
fully rigorous, formal proof of panagnosticism.
1 Introduction
The problem of other minds is a longstanding puzzle in philosophy: how (if at all) can one
know that others are conscious beings like oneself? Panagnosticism takes a radical epistemic
stance: for any two distinct entities, it is impossible for an agent to know whether the other
is conscious or not. In other words, for any agentaand any entitye̸=a, neither the
proposition ”eis conscious” nor ”eis not conscious” can ever be known bya.
We formalize this claim in standard epistemic modal logic and prove it as a theorem
under natural assumptions. Two key ideas underpin our approach:
(i)Privacy of Experience: any conscious experience is accessible only to its subject,
never directly to an outsider.

4 Objections and Responses
4.1 Neural or Behavioral Evidence
Behavioral or neural observations cannot logically entailC(e) due to the possibility of zombie
worlds. Analogical reasoning (Mill, Russell) is insufficient; introspection does not transfer
knowledge externally (Locke, Malcolm, Wittgenstein). Even precise neural correlates cannot
yield deductive knowledge.
4.2 Mutual Verification
Brain links or telepathy do not succeed: shared experiences become ”ours,” not ”theirs”
(Ayer). The privacy of experience prevents independent verification.
4.3 Epistemic Closure
Closure requires knowledge ofE(e)→C(e) andE(e); both fail a priori. Empirical correla-
tions do not satisfy deductive closure, so panagnosticism remains consistent.
4.4 Bridge Principles (E→C)
No known law or identity necessarily links physical evidence to consciousness. Empirical
correlations (e.g., Φ measures) are contingent; logical certainty is unattainable.

5 Contrast with Competing Views
•Panpsychism: ontological claim vs epistemic limitation.
•Solipsism: extreme denial vs agnostic acknowledgment.
•Eliminativism: denies existence; panagnosticism denies knowledge.
•Cartesian Skepticism: invokes God; panagnosticism relies on logic alone.
•AI Tests: no behavioral or computational test can conclusively reveal consciousness.
6 Implications
6.1 AI and Ethics
Panagnosticism suggests adopting the precautionary principle in AI ethics: treat potential
consciousness as morally relevant, despite epistemic uncertainty.
6.2 Epistemology and Mind
Highlights asymmetry between first-person self-knowledge and knowledge of others. Com-
patible with physicalist or non-physicalist frameworks.
6.3 Social and Philosophical Reflection
Encourages epistemic humility and caution in attributing consciousness, supporting careful
intersubjective practices.
7 Relation to Key Philosophers
•Nagel: formalizes inaccessibility of the ”what-it-is-like.”
•Chalmers: formalizes the zombie argument.
•Descartes: restricts doubt to consciousness, no God-hypothesis required.
•Searle: observation cannot reveal subjective states; aligns with Chinese Room insights.
8 Conclusion
We have provided a formal proof of panagnosticism: no agent can know the consciousness
status of any distinct entity. Using standard epistemic logic and natural assumptions, we
derived thatK aC(e) andK a¬C(e) are always false fora̸=e. This recasts the problem
of other minds into a rigorous logical theorem. The epistemic impossibility of knowing
others’ consciousness has implications for AI, ethics, and philosophy of mind, emphasizing
the distinction between belief and knowledge.
