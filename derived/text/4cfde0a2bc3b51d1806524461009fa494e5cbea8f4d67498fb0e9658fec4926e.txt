Abstract
A non-iterative geometric correction architecture is formulated for non-autonomous
restricted four-body dynamics. The central construction replaces the assumption of a time-
independent Jacobi integral by an explicitly propagated pseudo-Jacobi target generated by the
temporal flux of the time-dependent effective potential. The numerical method is organized
into two conceptually separable layers. Loop A tracks the moving pseudo-Jacobi target,
while Loop B provides a phase correction. An optional scalar velocity projection can map a
numerical state onto the instantaneous target manifold without nonlinear iteration.
The method is evaluated through a hierarchy of independent numerical tests. First, the
analytical temporal flux is compared with a symmetric finite-difference derivative. Second,
the flux equation is integrated on an externally supplied reference trajectory in order to
isolate quadrature error from state-predictor error. Third, the algebraic projection residual
is evaluated independently. Autonomous CR3BP is used as a control problem, while the
principal non-autonomous validation is performed for a Sun–Jupiter–Saturn restricted four-
body configuration.
The CR4BP short-horizon refinement experiment (h∈ {0.08, 0.04, 0.02, 0.01}) shows
approximately first-order scaling of the complete flux-tracking residual while retaining ap-
proximately fourth-order trajectory convergence of the underlying RK4 state propagator.
The flux-only formulation produces residuals from2.75× 10−6 to 3.49× 10−7 on that grid.
A separate long-horizonh-refinement at fixedT exhibits an approximately fourfold residual
reduction whenh is halved (empirical order near two on that pair of steps). In contrast,
hard post-step velocity rescaling reaches the double-precision residual floor, but introduces
a positional discrepancy of approximately4.3 relative to the finest pure-RK4 reference and
does not converge as a trajectory correction. An ablation experiment further shows that the
hard scaling configuration produces a positional deviation approximately11.4 times larger
than the corresponding flux-plus-phase configuration without scaling.
Over integration horizons up toT = 500, the flux-only pseudo-Jacobi residual remains
bounded and exhibits no detectable secular growth in the reported experiments. Trajectory
drift is controlled by retaining flux tracking and (when used) symmetric phase correction
while disabling hard velocity scaling; optional soft or threshold projection may replace hard
closure when a residual guard is required. These results demonstrate that invariant tracking
accuracy and orbital trajectory fidelity are distinct numerical objectives and should not be
conflated.
Keywords: CR3BP; CR4BP; non-autonomous dynamics; Jacobi integral; pseudo-Jacobi invari-
ant; flux tracking; geometric projection; RK4; numerical refinement; long-horizon integration.

1 Introduction
Long-term numerical integration of resonant and libration-point trajectories in restricted gravi-
tational systems is complicated by the loss of a strict time-independent Jacobi integral when
explicitly time-dependent perturbations are introduced.
For the autonomous circular restricted three-body problem (CR3BP), the Jacobi quantity is
constant along an exact trajectory and therefore defines a fixed invariant manifold. In the presence
of a moving perturbing body, the effective synodic potential becomes explicitly time-dependent.
The corresponding scalar quantity is no longer constant. Treating it as a conserved quantity
therefore imposes an incorrect constraint on the non-autonomous system.
The central distinction of the present construction is consequently betweenconservation and
tracking. The objective is not to force a fictitious constant onto a non-autonomous trajectory.
Instead, the moving target implied by the exact continuous flux identity is propagated numerically.
The proposed architecture, referred to here as Synodic Birkhoff–PEC, contains two conceptu-
ally distinct numerical layers. Loop A tracks the pseudo-Jacobi flux. Loop B provides phase
correction through rotational operations. A third, optional operation is a scalar velocity closure
that projects the post-step state onto the instantaneous target manifold.
The present study deliberately uses CR4BP as the principal validated non-autonomous
model. General CRNBP scalability is treated as an extension rather than as an experimentally
established result.
The numerical methodology is constructed around a strict separation of error sources. In
particular, the following quantities are not identified with one another:
(i) error in the analytical temporal flux;
(ii) error introduced by numerical integration of the flux;
(iii) algebraic projection error;
(iv) state-propagator error;
(v) trajectory deviation caused by phase or velocity corrections.
This separation is essential because a numerical state can satisfy a scalar constraint to
machine precision while simultaneously exhibiting poor orbital fidelity.

2.5 Phase Layer
Loop B operates on the phase degrees of freedom. In the planar CR4BP limit, the phase
operation reduces to a rotation about the synodicz axis. In the three-dimensional extension,
non-commuting rotation generators can be composed through symmetric Strang splitting.
The present numerical validation separates the flux contribution from the phase contribution
wherever possible. This is necessary because the effect of a phase correction on trajectory error
cannot be inferred from a scalar invariant residual alone.

2.7 Recommended Drift-Controlled Configuration
The numerical campaign separates three operations that must not be conflated:
(i) flux tracking of the moving pseudo-Jacobi target (Loop A);
(ii) phase correction (Loop B);
(iii) hard scalar velocity projection.
Ablation results below show that trajectory drift is localized primarily to the hard projection
step. The recommended baseline for long-horizon CR4BP integration is therefore
flux tracking+phase correction, hard scaling disabled.

trajectory convergence of the underlying RK4 propagator.

8.3 Empirical verification of the scaling invariance hypothesis
To delineate the operational boundaries of the flux-tracking layer we formulate and test a joint
empirical hypothesis for the complete residual as a function of model dimensionN, step sizeh,
horizon T, and initial condition (IC):
Eflux(N,h,T ; IC)≈ A(IC)h2, (40)
on the domainN ∈{ 4,..., 10} and T ≤ 1000. The hypothesis asserts that the residual is
controlled by the truncation structure of the temporal quadrature and by local trajectory
geometry, remaining invariant to the dimension of the perturbing potential (N 0) and to the
integration duration (T 0) within that domain.
The campaign is organized as four isolation tests.
(i) Step-size scaling (E ∝ h2). Least-squares fits of the maximum residual on h ∈
{0.08, 0.04, 0.02, 0.01} at T = 100 confirm quadratic scaling. The maximum relative
fit error is bounded by0.14% for CR4BP (A = 6.023× 10−5) and remains below0.61%
through CR10BP (A≈ 6.106× 10−5). The mean prefactor is ¯A = 6.09× 10−5 with
cross-model CV(A)≈ 0.47%.
(ii) Dimensional invariance (E∝N 0). Cross-model variation ofEflux at fixed step sizes
over N = 4,..., 10 is small: at h = 0.05 and T = 100, CV≈ 0.63%, consistent with
∂E/∂N ≈ 0 under these non-autonomous extensions.
(iii) Temporal boundedness (E ∝ T 0). Secular ratios RT = E(T )/E(100) track unity
through T = 1000, with R1000∈ [1.000, 1.004] on the checked models. No energy-like
systematic drift is detected.
(iv) Geometric sensitivity (A =A(IC)). NearL4/L5, minor IC shifts change the residual by
only∼ 0.2%. A deeper excursion onto a far-equilibrium orbit raises the baseline by about
38% (uniformly for low and highN at h = 0.05). Thus A depends on local trajectory
geometry rather than on model dimension; theh2 law itself is preserved.
Table 7 summarizes the experimental verdict. Accepting a controlled structuralO(h2) residual
is preferred to forcing an artificialO(ϵmach) floor by hard velocity projection, which degrades
orbital fidelity without improving dimensional scalability. CR8BP–CR10BP synthetic shells are
stress tests of the architecture, not Solar-System ephemerides.

9 Related Work
Classical treatments of the circular restricted three-body problem and its invariants are given by
Szebehely [1]. Mission design and quasi-periodic motion near libration points are surveyed by
Gómez et al. [2, 10]. The present construction addresses the complementary numerical question:
how to track amoving scalar target once an explicit perturber destroys the autonomous Jacobi
integral.
Structure-preserving integration is developed systematically by Hairer, Lubich and Wanner [3]
and by Blanes and Casas [4]; higher-order symplectic composition goes back to Yoshida [5].
Projection and discrete-gradient methods for first integrals are discussed by Norton et al. [6]
and in the DAE literature [7, 9]. Soft residual control is conceptually related to Baumgarte
stabilization [8], which introduces damping toward a constraint manifold rather than enforcing it
exactly at every step—the same distinction drawn here between hard isopotential scaling and a
tolerance or soft projection.
Apreliminaryframeworknote[ 11]introducedthetwo-loopSynodicBirkhoff–PECarchitecture.
The present paper supplies the independent numerical audit: refinement, ablation, and long-
horizon tests that separate invariant residuals from trajectory fidelity and motivate disabling
hard velocity scaling in the recommended CR4BP baseline.
10 Discussion
Figure 4 summarizes the central numerical trade-off: scalar residual accuracy and trajectory
fidelity occupy independent axes and need not be minimized simultaneously.
Three numerical properties must be kept separate.
First, the analytical flux provides the correct moving pseudo-Jacobi target. Second, the
numerical quadrature determines how accurately that target is propagated. Third, the optional
scalar projection determines how closely the computed state is placed on that target.
The numerical results show that these operations need not have the same effect on trajectory
fidelity.
The hard projection demonstrates the clearest example. In both autonomous and non-
autonomous configurations it can drive the scalar residual to the floating-point floor. However,
the CR4BP trajectory experiment shows a large positional discrepancy and a lack of trajectory
convergence. Therefore,
Eprojection→O(ϵmach) (41)
does not imply
Etrajectory→ 0. (42)

Why hard velocity scaling degrades the trajectory.Hard projection multiplies the entire
post-step velocity by a scalarα chosen so that the instantaneous isoenergetic constraint is satisfied
exactly. In the synodic frame this changes the speed without a corresponding adjustment of
the direction that would be consistent with the continuous flow. The operation is therefore
equivalent to an instantaneous non-physical impulse: it alters the angular momentum relative
to the rotating frame and shifts the particle’s phase on the libration torus. Over many steps
these impulses accumulate as a secular positional drift, even though the scalar residual remains
at machine level. Soft projection (β≪ 1) or a pure tolerance guard avoids this impulse-like
correction and is preferred when orbital fidelity is required.
Computational overhead. A reference implementation of the flux-only layer (Loop A trape-
zoidal update with no phase andβ = 0) adds approximately2% wall-clock cost relative to a
pure RK4 step on the same right-hand side, confirming that the scheme is non-iterative and
inexpensive. The dominant cost remains the gravitational force evaluation.
Extended horizon check. An independent flux-only run toT = 1000 at h = 0.05 and
h = 0.025yields max|∆J|of 1.51×10−7 and 3.76×10−8 respectively, statisticallyindistinguishable
from theT = 100 and T = 500 values. No secular growth is detected on this longer interval
either.
The preferred CR4BP configuration is consequently the flux-plus-phase variant with hard
scaling disabled (equivalently, flux-only when the phase layer is inactive). It does not minimize
the instantaneous scalar residual as aggressively as hard projection, but it retains the fourth-order
trajectory convergence of the underlying RK4 propagator while providing bounded pseudo-Jacobi
tracking and suppressing the trajectory drift induced by aggressive velocity closure. When a
residual guard is required, the soft or threshold projection(19)–(18) is used in place of hard
scaling.
The results also demonstrate why the distinction between an invariant and a moving target
is not merely terminological. In the non-autonomous problem, enforcing
CJ(t) =CJ(0)
would impose the wrong physical condition. The appropriate numerical objective is instead
CJ(t)≈Ctarget(t),
where Ctarget is generated by the continuous flux law.

12 Limitations
The conclusions are restricted to the validated CR3BP control and Sun–Jupiter–Saturn CR4BP
configuration and to the integration horizons explicitly tested.
No universal trajectory-preservation claim is made for the hard scalar projection. Observed
residual rates—approximately first-order on the short refinement grid and near second-order on
the long-horizonh-pair—are treated as empirical properties of the complete scheme in those
regimes and are not identified with the formal order of an isolated quadrature rule.
A flux-only scalability ladder through CR10BP (Section 8), including anN×h×T matrix
and multi-IC checks, supports the empirical lawEflux≈A(IC)h2 with A nearly independent of
N andT on the tested domain (N≤ 10,T≤ 1000). This does not constitute a proof for arbitrary
CRNBP or infinite time; further bodies and horizons still require independent campaigns.
Similarly, the absence of detectable secular growth up toT = 500 should not be interpreted
as a mathematical proof of boundedness for all times.
The distinction between scalar constraint accuracy and orbital fidelity should therefore remain
explicit in any subsequent extension of the method.
13 Conclusion
Anon-iterativeflux-trackedgeometricprojectionarchitecturehasbeenformulatedandnumerically
audited in autonomous CR3BP and non-autonomous CR4BP settings.
The central construction replaces the inappropriate assumption of a constant Jacobi integral in
the non-autonomous problem with an explicitly integrated pseudo-Jacobi flux and a corresponding
moving target manifold.
The short-horizon CR4BP refinement campaign establishes approximately first-order scaling
of the flux-only residual onh∈{ 0.08, 0.04, 0.02, 0.01}:
EJ(h) =O(h) (empirical on that grid),
while the underlying trajectory retains approximately fourth-order convergence:
Etrajectory(h) =O(h4).
The measured residual decreases from 2.75× 10−6 at h = 0.08 to 3.49× 10−7 at h = 0.01
(consecutive orders near unity). Independently, the long-horizonh-refinement at fixedT yields
an approximately fourfold residual drop whenh is halved (plong≈ 2 on h∈{ 0.05, 0.025}). These
rates are empirical properties of the complete scheme in the tested regimes.

The validated conclusion is therefore not that the smallest scalar constraint residual defines
the best numerical solution. Rather, the preferred CR4BP configuration is the one that tracks the
movingpseudo-Jacobitarget—withoptionalsymmetricphasecorrectionandwithouthardvelocity
scaling—while preserving the convergence properties of the underlying trajectory integrator.
Drift is controlled by separating flux tracking from aggressive isopotential projection, not by
forcing the scalar residual to machine precision at every step.
The principal methodological result is consequently the separation
moving-manifold tracking̸= instantaneous scalar projection̸= trajectory fidelity.
This separation provides a basis for further extensions to higher-dimensional restricted many-
body configurations, provided that each extension is subjected to the same independent analytical,
quadrature, projection, refinement, ablation, and long-horizon validation protocol.
