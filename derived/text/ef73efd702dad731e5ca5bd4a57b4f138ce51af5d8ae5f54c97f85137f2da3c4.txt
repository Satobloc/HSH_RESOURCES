Abstract 
Many apparently different problems in theoretical physics share a common structural pattern: 
propagation between states, local interaction or scattering, phase accumulation around closed 
cycles, and the repeated generation of admissible histories. This wor k introduces a four-
component architectural framework based on propagation, interaction, holonomy or closed-
path monodromy, and recursive closure. The first three components define the primitive internal 
dynamics, while recursive closure organizes arbitrarily many repeated processes into a finite 
operator representation. A general resummation theorem is established for systems in which 
admissible histories can be uniquely factorized into primitive return blocks. Under this 
condition, the infinite family of repeated histories is represented exactly by a finite resolvent 
acting on an internal architectural state space. The resulting framework is then applied to several 
well-known physical problems, including Dyson resummation, Lippmann –Schwinger 
scattering, the Bethe–Salpeter equation, Foldy–Lax multiple scattering, Fabry –Pérot 
interference, quantum double-barrier resonant tunneling, and Aharonov–Bohm ring transport. 
These examples show that physically distinct systems can share the same recursive architecture 
despite differences in their microscopic interpretation. In particular, Fabry–Pérot interference 
and quantum double-barrier tunneling are shown to possess the same scalar recursive structure, 
while the Aharonov–Bohm ring provides a nontrivial realization i n which closed-path 
information carries genuine gauge holonomy. The individual physical solutions and the 
underlying resolvent identities are established results. The contribution of the present 
framework is their systematic sector -resolved factorization into a common architectural 
calculus and the associated interpretation of recursive closure as an exact compression 
mechanism for infinite families of physical histories. The approach does not claim a 
fundamental reconstruction of quantum mechanics, but pro poses a unified mathematical 
language for a broad class of recursive Green-function, scattering, interference, and network 
problems. 
Keywords: Green functions; resummation; Dyson equation; Lippmann–Schwinger equation; 
Bethe–Salpeter equation; multiple scattering; Fabry–Pérot interferometer; resonant tunneling; 
Aharonov–Bohm effect; quantum graphs; holonomy; resolvent 
1. Introduction 
Recursive expansions occur throughout mathematical and theoretical physics. Dyson’s 
formulation of quantum electrodynamics provided one of the best-known examples in which 
repeated interaction insertions reorganize perturbative contributions into a dressed propagator 
[1]. Schwinger’s Green-function formulation established Green functions as fundamental tools 
for representing quantized fields and their dynamics [2]. Feynman’s space-time formulation

===== PAGE 2 =====
simultaneously emphasized the complementary idea that physical amplitudes may be organized 
as sums over histories or paths [3]. 
In scattering theory, the Lippmann–Schwinger construction expresses the full scattering state 
or transition operator through repeated free propagation and interaction [4]. Closely related 
recursive structures appear in multiple-wave scattering, beginning with Foldy’s treatment of 
isotropic scatterers [5], Lax’s general multiple-scattering formulation [6], its self-consistent 
extension to dense systems [7], and Watson’s many-body multiple-scattering expansion [8]. 
Relativistic two-particle physics produces a formally analogous structure. The Bethe–Salpeter 
equation generates repeated propagation of a particle pair between irreducible interaction 
kernels [9]. Although the physical interpretation differs substantially from ordinary potential 
scattering, the algebraic recurrence has a recognizable common skeleton. 
Recursive histories also appear far outside quantum field theory. Fabry and Perot’s 
interferometric construction generates an infinite sequence of multiply reflected optical beams 
[10]. In semiconductor physics, tunneling through finite superlattices was analyzed by Tsu and 
Esaki [11], and resonant transmission through semiconductor double barriers was subsequently 
formulated explicitly by Chang, Esaki, and Tsu [12]. The multiple -reflection structure is 
strikingly analogous to an optical Fabry–Pérot cavity. 
Closed-path phases introduce another ingredient. The Aharonov–Bohm effect demonstrates 
that a gauge potential can produce an observable phase associated with a closed path even when 
the particle propagates through a field -free region [13]. Byers and Yang e stablished the 
associated flux-periodicity structure for multiply connected systems [14]. Mesoscopic ℎ/𝑒 
Aharonov–Bohm oscillations were subsequently observed in normal-metal rings [15], while 
multichannel transport through such rings was formulated within scattering theory by Büttiker, 
Imry, Landauer, and Pinhas [16]. 
The relation between Green functions and measurable transmission was developed further 
through the Fisher–Lee relation [17], building on the transmission-based transport viewpoint 
introduced by Landauer [18]. Network formulations therefore connect Green  functions, 
scattering matrices, propagation amplitudes, and experimentally accessible transport 
observables. 
Quantum graphs provide an especially transparent mathematical setting in which these elements 
coexist. Kottos and Smilansky showed that networks can be quantized in a manner that exposes 
their path and spectral structure [19], and subsequently developed an  exact periodic-orbit 
formulation of quantum-graph spectra [20]. Kostrykin and Schrader formulated general 
scattering theory and composition rules for quantum wires represented as graphs [21], while 
Barra and Gaspard studied transport and dynamics in open quantum graphs [22]. 
Recursive compression is also familiar from numerical Green-function methods. Haydock, 
Heine, and Kelly developed an influential recursion method for electronic-structure calculations 
[23]. For quantum graphs, exact Green functions can be organized as sums over scattering paths 
and reduced to closed analytic expressions [24]. An adjacency-matrix construction was later 
shown to be unitarily equivalent to the Green-function formulation [25]. Exact convergent 
periodic-orbit expansions have been obtained for regular quantum graphs [26], and analytic 
summations over families of graph trajectories have been used to describe Anderson 
localization [27]. Open graphs additionally provide exact scattering matrices and resonance 
structures in terms of graph dynamics [28].

The role of the closed-path sector becomes still clearer from gauge theory. Wu and Yang 
formulated gauge fields in terms of nonintegrable phase factors associated with paths [29]. 
Berry demonstrated that cyclic evolution can generate an additional geometric phase [30], while 
Wilczek and Zee showed that cyclic evolution can support non-Abelian gauge structure [31]. 
The existence of these many mathematically related constructions raises a structural question: 
Can propagation, local interaction, closed-path phase information, and repeated-history 
summation be separated into a common minimal calculus that applies across these 
otherwise different physical problems? 
The present paper investigates this question.

The purpose is not to claim that the individual solutions are new. They are not. Nor is the 
Neumann-series identity new. 
The proposed contribution is instead a sector-resolved structural statement: 
many apparently different recursive physical constructions→one architectural factorization. 
The strongest consequence of this viewpoint is a compression principle: 
infinitely many histories→finite internal kernel→one resolvent.

mathematical justification. 
23.5 Singular continuum operators 
Quantum-field and continuum integral equations may involve unbounded operators, ultraviolet 
divergences, renormalization, or singular kernels. 
The finite-dimensional theorem cannot simply be transferred without domain analysis.

31. Discussion 
The principal result of this paper is the identification of a common architecture behind several 
historically and physically distinct constructions. 
At first sight, Dyson dressing, quantum scattering, relativistic two -particle propagation, 
multiple-wave scattering, optical cavity interference, semiconductor resonant tunneling, and 
flux-threaded quantum networks belong to different branches of physics. 
Their primitive objects are different. 
Their observables are different. 
Their physical interpretations are different. 
Nevertheless, once the irreducible physical content has been isolated, each contains a repeated-
history structure. 
In the simplest cases this structure reduces to a scalar geometric series. 
In network and multiple-scattering problems it becomes a finite-dimensional matrix resolvent. 
The PIHR formulation separates four roles that are frequently mixed together: 
• propagation determines connectivity and dynamical transfer; 
• interaction determines local modification; 
• holonomy determines closed-cycle information; 
• recursion sums all admissible repeated histories. 
The most important mathematical refinement is recognizing that recursion is not a fourth local 
building block.

computationally useful. Explicit path families can proliferate indefinitely, while the internal 
kernel remains finite dimensional.

32. Limitations 
Several limitations should prevent overinterpretation. 
First, the core resummation theorem is mathematically a structured application of the Neumann 
resolvent. 
Its value, if any, lies in the factorization principle, not in a new matrix identity. 
Second, many areas of physics already use transfer matrices, scattering matrices, recursion 
methods, graph reductions, and Green-function resolvents. PIHR must therefore demonstrate 
practical or conceptual simplification beyond a change of notation. 
Third, the present examples are linear. 
Nonlinear recursion cannot generally be reduced to 
(𝟙−𝐾)./. 
Fourth, no fundamental constants, particle masses, or new experimental effects are derived here. 
Fifth, the construction does not derive quantum mechanics. 
Hilbert spaces, complex amplitudes, Green functions, and scattering operators enter the 
examples through their established physical theories. 
The purpose of the architecture is to organize recursive structure within those theories. 
33. Falsifiable Criteria for the PIHR Program 
The proposed architecture should be judged by explicit criteria. 
It would gain significance if: 
1. physically different problems can be reduced by the same factorization rules without 
problem-specific modifications; 
2. complicated path or diagram expansions can be compressed to substantially smaller 
kernels; 
3. the minimal internal kernel can be constructed systematically; 
4. nontrivial holonomy can be separated from ordinary dynamical propagation; 
5. the resulting reduction yields analytic or computational advantages over standard 
formulations. 
Conversely, the program would lose significance if: 
1. every application requires an ad hoc redefinition of 𝑃,𝐼,𝐻,𝑅;

2. the kernel is no smaller or simpler than the original problem; 
3. the factorization contains no information beyond standard transfer-matrix notation; 
4. no new equivalence classes, invariants, algorithms, or deductions emerge from the 
framework. 
These criteria make the proposal testable.
