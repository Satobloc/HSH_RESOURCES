Abstract
In this paper, we study the Casimir effect on the wormhole geometry in 
gravity. We derive the field equations for the generic  function by assuming

existence. Subsequently, we analyse the obtained wormhole solutions for each
scenario, assessing the energy conditions at the wormhole throat with a radius of .
Our findings reveal that for some arbitrary quantities, there is a violation of classical
energy conditions at the wormhole throat. Additionally, we delve into the behaviour
of the equation of state (EoS) for each case. Furthermore, we explore the stability of
the Casimir effect wormhole solutions by employing the generalized Tolman–
Oppenheimer–Volkoff (TOV) equation. Finally, we utilize the volume integral
quantifier to determine the amount of exotic matter required near the wormhole
throat for both models.

Introduction
In 1916, a year following Einstein’s introduction of the Theory of General Relativity
(GR), Schwarzschild presented the inaugural non-trivial analytic solution for
Einstein’s GR [1]. This solution by Schwarzschild exhibits a removable singularity at a
regular point termed the event horizon. This concept laid the foundation for the idea
of a black hole, whose existence has recently been confirmed [2], [3]. Concurrently, L.
Flamm identified another potential solution, now recognized as a wormhole [4]. A
wormhole is a geometric configuration that resembles a corridor connecting various
universes or separate regions within the same universe. Subsequently, Einstein and
Rosen delved into the precise nature of a wormhole, forming a hypothetical bridge
based on Flamm’s idea, now known as the Einstein–Rosen bridge [5]. Essentially,
wormholes serve to link two asymptotically flat regions of space–time through a
throat [6]. Finally, Morris and Thorne, in [7], introduced static traversable wormholes
that redefine interstellar travel and offer exact solutions within general relativity
(GR). The authors demonstrated that a wormhole could be traversable if it exhibits
exotic matter with minimal surface area, satisfying the flare-out condition. Exotic
matter, violating null energy conditions (NEC) and defined by  for any
null vector  (where  is the stress–energy tensor), is considered an essential
ingredient in constructing a wormhole in GR. Indeed, in classical GR, wormhole
solutions contravene all energy conditions [6]. However, for a physically plausible
model, challenging the existence of the hypothetical fluid becomes necessary. Since
ruling out the presence of such a candidate within the GR framework was difficult
[8], [9], an alternative approach gained support, aiming to minimize or even
eliminate the dependence on exotic matter [10], [11], [12]. It is known that GR
describes the universe well enough; however, some recent observations demand
modifying the classical relativity into some alternative that could explain well, such
as the late-time acceleration expansion of the universe [13], [14], [15], [16]. To tackle
this, several modified theories are introduced such as  gravity [17], [18], 
gravity [19], [20],  gravity [21], Rastall gravity [22] etc.

Recently, it has been discovered that hypothetical fluids can be effectively addressed
within modified gravity theories. In the realm of  gravity, Lobo and Oliveira
examined wormhole (WH) structures [23], presenting significant findings as detailed
in [24]. This discussion specifically omits exotic matter. The exploration of wormhole
solutions has gained momentum, evidenced by Maldacena and Milekhin’s creation of
traversable WHs using the Randall–Sundrum model [25]. Additionally, Rahaman et al.
put forward the notion of WH existence within the galactic halo [26]. Investigating
blackbounce spacetime, studies have delved into the dynamics of spherically
symmetric traversable WHs [27]. Garcia and Lobo analysed WH behaviour within
curvature-matter coupling gravity [28]. The literature also encompasses WH
solutions across various gravitational theories, including teleparallel gravity [29],
extended theories of teleparallel gravity [30], [31], brane [32], Einstein–Gauss–
Bonnet theory [33], Brans–Dicke theory [34],  gravity [35], and Rastall gravity
[36].
Dutch physicist Hendrik Casimir proposed the Casimir effect in 1948, suggesting the
existence of a force between uncharged, parallel, conducting plates [37]. Garattini
[38] viewed Casimir’s energy as a potential avenue for exploring traversable
wormholes. The Casimir effect’s strength is intricately tied to the geometry of the
plates and serves as an artificial energy source. This energy form becomes
particularly relevant for traversable wormholes, as the quantum field of the vacuum
between parallel uncharged plates generates a negative energy density. Lamoreaux’s
experimental confirmation in 1997 [39] supported the earlier hypothesis about the
Casimir effect, and subsequent refinements by Bressi [40] yielded even more precise
results. In the context of the weak limit approximation, Javed et al. [41] delved into
examining the weak deflection angle of photons around a Casimir wormhole. They
applied the Gauss–Bonnet theorem to the Gaussian optical spacetime to determine
the Gaussian optical curvature. In a separate publication, the authors investigated the
connection between a remarkably harmless traversable wormhole and Casimir
energy [42]. They extended the concept of an Absurdly Benign Traversable
Wormhole, revealing that the wormhole throat is Planckian but significantly large.
Muniz et al. conducted a study on the Casimir effect between parallel plates within
the spacetime of a rotating wormholes [43]. Casimir wormhole have been extensively
studied in several modified gravity theories [44], [45], [46].
Among the generalized  gravity, the theory that takes into account the influence
of an ‘extra’ force by considering the coupling between the matter Lagrangian density
and the Ricci Scalar is  gravity [13], [47]. Interestingly, this additional force,
perpendicular to the four-velocity, is accountable for the non-geodesic motion of the
test particles. Wang and Liao derived the energy conditions in  gravity [48].
In [49], Solanki et al. obtained wormhole solutions in  gravity by assuming
the linear barotropic, anisotropic, and isotropic equation of state (EoS). Also, Naseer
et al. constructed a traversable wormhole solution in  theory [50]. Several
works have been done in the context of this theory [49], [51], [52], [53]. In the
present work, we have explored the effect of the Casimir wormhole in the framework
of  gravity.
