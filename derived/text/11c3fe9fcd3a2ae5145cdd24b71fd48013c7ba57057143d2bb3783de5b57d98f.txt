BAYESIAN COMPARISON OF BEC, NFW, AND GNFW HALO
MODELS USING GALAXY ROTATION CURVES
ABSTRACT
We present a Bayesian comparison of three dark-matter halo models—Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW), 
generalized NFW (gNFW, with free inner slope α), and Bose–Einstein condensate (BEC) halos 
including a rotating variant—fitted to galaxy rotation curves from the SPARC database to address 
the core–cusp problem. Unlike earlier studies that relied on heuristic or genetic-algorithm searches 
of parameter space, we sample the full posterior distributions with Markov Chain Monte Carlo, 
marginalizing over baryonic nuisance parameters and using both the Bayesian Information Criterion 
(BIC) and a posterior-based harmonic-mean evidence estimator to assess model support. Across the 
sample most galaxies are statistically indistinguishable between models, but allowing a free inner 
slope yields a modest, systematic evidence gain for gNFW relative to fixed-slope NFW. BEC models 
show a much broader spread in performance: a subset of galaxies strongly favour BEC, but many 
exhibit substantial evidence loss compared to gNFW, with the rotating BEC giving the best mean 
performance yet large galaxy-to-galaxy variability. These results highlight that flexibility in the 
inner slope is crucial for rotation-curve fits, and demonstrate that full Bayesian evidence estimation 
provides a more robust, population-level assessment of halo models than previous genetic-algorithm 
or point-estimate approaches.

1 Introduction
Dark matter is an invisible form of matter that neither emits nor absorbs electromagnetic radiation. Although it cannot 
be observed directly, it makes up 85 percent of the total matter in the universe and plays a critical role in shaping cosmic 
structure. Its presence is inferred solely through gravitational influence on visible matter, light, and the universe’s 
large-scale structure. Admittedly, there is sufficient evidence pointing to this unseen mass component which dominates 
the dynamics of galaxies and clusters [1].
The very first evidence of dark matter came when Fritz Zwicky studied the Coma galaxy cluster and he noticed that the 
galaxies were moving much faster than what would be expected based on just the visible mass. After applying the virial 
theorem, he concluded that the cluster must contain far more mass than what was observable and he coined the term 
‘dark matter’. The discrepancy has since been confirmed and further extended by other observations.
One of the most compelling lines of evidence comes from the study of galactic rotation curves as Newtonian physics 
predicts that the orbital velocities of stars would decrease with increased distance from the galactic center just as 
planets in the solar system which is further described in the methods section [2]. However, observations show that these 
velocities remain flat even far from the galactic bulge. This unexpected result implies that galaxies are surrounded by 
massive, extended halos of dark matter, which provide the extra gravitational pull needed to keep stars in orbit.
Gravitational lensing also reveals the presence of dark matter. The bending of light depends on total mass, not just 
visible mass, allowing astronomers to “weigh” galaxies and clusters. In many cases, the observed lensing cannot be 
explained by luminous matter alone. A particularly dramatic example is the Bullet Cluster, a collision of two galaxy 
clusters in which the luminous gas (visible in X-rays) lags behind the main gravitational mass, suggesting that the 
majority of matter is non-interacting and invisible. On even larger scales, the cosmic microwave background (CMB) 
offers a snapshot of the early universe.
Detailed measurements of the CMB’s temperature fluctuations by satellites such as WMAP and Planck have allowed 
cosmologists to extract precise information about the universe’s composition. The amount of normal (baryonic) matter 
is actually insufficient to explain the gravitational effects observed; instead, a significant contribution from cold, 
non-baryonic dark matter is required to match the observed power spectrum. Finally, simulations of structure formation 
in the universe rely on dark matter to explain how galaxies and clusters emerged from tiny initial fluctuations.
The Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW) profile, derived from cold dark matter simulations [ 3], has become a standard 
description of galactic dark matter halos. However, the NFW form is fixed to an inner logarithmic slope of α = 1, 
which may not adequately capture the diversity of observed galaxy rotation curves. To address this, the generalized 
NFW (gNFW) profile introduces a free slope parameter α, modifying the inner density law. This additional degree of 
freedom allows the gNFW profile to interpolate between cored halos (α  0) and steeper cusps (α > 1), making it a 
more flexible baseline model.
The core-cusp problem arises from the discrepancy between simulations and observations of dark matter density profiles 
in galaxies. N-body simulations based on CDM predict a steep density increase towards the center of galaxies (a "cusp"), 
described by the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile. However, observations of galaxy rotation curves often suggest a 
constant-density core in the inner regions of galaxies. This contradiction indicates the need to explore alternative dark 
matter models that can naturally produce cored density profiles.
One such alternative is Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC) dark matter. A BEC is a state of matter that occurs at extremely 
low temperatures, where a large fraction of bosons occupy the lowest quantum state, resulting in macroscopic quantum 
phenomena [4]. In the context of dark matter, it is hypothesized that ultra-light bosons could form a galactic-scale 
condensate, leading to a smooth, core-like density distribution. This model has the potential to resolve the core-cusp 
discrepancy while maintaining consistency with large-scale structure formation.
In this study, we analyze a rotating BEC dark matter halo model [ 5] and fit it to observational data. Unlike previous 
models that considered only static halos, rotation introduces a more realistic dynamical behavior and alters the predicted 
rotation curves.

For our analysis, we made use of the SPitzer Photometry and Accurate Rotation Curves (SPARC) dataset [ 6], which 
provides high-quality rotation curves for a large sample of nearby galaxies spanning a wide range of morphological 
types, luminosities, and surface brightnesses. Each galaxy in SPARC includes both observed kinematic data and 
carefully derived baryonic contributions from stellar and gas components, enabling detailed mass modeling. In this 
work, we fit these rotation curves with a set of competing dark matter halo profiles, extracting posterior distributions for 
their parameters and comparing their Bayesian evidences across the sample. This framework allows us to statistically 
evaluate which models are most consistent with the diversity of observed galaxy dynamics.
In this work, we treat gNFW as the reference model when comparing alternatives such as Bose–Einstein condensate 
(BEC) halos, since it subsumes NFW as the special case α = 1 and provides a direct test of whether observed galaxies 
favor shallower or steeper inner slopes. This approach also links directly to the long-standing “core–cusp problem” 
in astrophysics, where low-mass galaxies often appear to prefer cored dark matter distributions in tension with the 
canonical NFW prediction.
Aside from the astrophysical motivations, this work will also be addressing a crucial methodological gap as previous 
studies examining BEC halos have not incorporated rotation in the condensate model or lack a Bayesian framework for 
statistical comparison. By using Bayesian inference, we may obtain full posterior distributions for each parameter as 
well as the uncertainties or standard deviations [ 7]. In addition, we access the Bayesian evidence which provides us 
with a principled measure of relative model support.
While the BIC provides a quick approximation to the Bayesian evidence using only the maximum likelihood and the 
number of parameters, it ignores the full shape of the posterior. In this work, we go beyond ln BIC by also evaluating 
the harmonic–mean estimator, which uses the complete set of posterior samples from MCMC [ 8]. This allows the 
evidence calculation to reflect the integrated support across parameter space rather than being tied only to the best–fit 
point, offering a more faithful comparison between halo models despite the known noisiness of the harmonic mean 
approach.
Rotation curves serve as an especially powerful probe in this context. Unlike lensing, which predominantly constrains 
cluster-scale structures, or the cosmic microwave background, which reflects cosmological scales, rotation curves 
directly trace the distribution of mass within galaxies. This makes them uniquely sensitive to the inner halo structure 
where the long-standing core–cusp tension between observations and ΛCDM predictions arises. By comparing rotating 
BEC halos with NFW and gNFW models, our analysis connects directly to this debate, testing whether quantum 
condensate models can naturally reproduce the cored profiles suggested by low-mass galaxies, or whether classical 
cuspy models remain statistically preferred.

2.2 Observational Data
The rotation curves for galaxies are constructed using Doppler spectroscopy. In the inner galaxy optical emission 
lines like Hα can be used [ 10]. In the outer regions they are traced using the 21-cm line from neutral hydrogen (HI). 
These measurements provide velocities of gas and stars, which are then corrected for the inclination angle of the galaxy 
relative to earth in order to derive the circular velocities about the galactic center. By plotting velocity as a function of 
radius, the rotation curve of the galaxy is obtained.
We employ data from the SPitzer Photometry and Accurate Rotation Curves (SPARC) database [ 11] which contains 
rotation curves for 175 galaxies. The sample spans a wide range of luminosities, mass and morphological types.

2.5 BEC Dark Matter Halo Model
In this work, we model the dark matter halo as a self-gravitating Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC). A BEC is a state of 
matter in which a large fraction of bosons occupy the ground quantum state, allowing the system to be described by a 
single macroscopic wavefunction. In the astrophysical context, such a condensate forms when ultra-light bosonic dark 
matter particles (e.g., axions) thermalize and condense under gravity.

introduced by the rotational degree of freedom highlights the dynamical richness of the condensate model, motivating 
its comparison to cuspy profiles such as NFW and the more flexible gNFW.

2.8 Bayesian Inference
Bayesian inference offers several advantages over traditional fitting methods such as genetic algorithms or least-squares. 
First, it provides a full posterior distribution for each parameter, enabling uncertainty quantification through credible 
intervals and parameter correlations. It also allows the natural incorporation of prior knowledge, for example from 
previous literature or physical constraints. By marginalizing over parameters instead of relying solely on a single best-fit 
point, Bayesian methods are more robust against overfitting to noise in the data. Finally, Bayesian evidence provides a 
rigorous framework for model comparison, making it possible to quantitatively assess the relative support for competing 
models, such as BEC versus NFW.
2.9 Bayesian Model Comparison
In Bayesian statistics, model comparison is based on the Bayesian evidence (or marginal likelihood) Z, defined as
Z =

where L(θ) is the likelihood of the data given parameters θ, and π(θ) is the prior. Models with larger ln Z are more 
strongly supported by the data. The difference ∆ ln Z between two models is directly related to the Bayes factor,
quantifying how much more the data favor one over the other. Following Jeffreys’ scale, ∆ ln Z ≳ 2 indicates positive 
evidence, while ∆ ln Z ≳ 5 constitutes strong evidence.
Directly evaluating the integral for is generally infeasible for high–dimensional parameter spaces, as encountered in 
halo modeling. We therefore employed two complementary approaches: a simple approximation based on the Bayesian 
Information Criterion (BIC) and a posterior-based estimator using the harmonic mean [12]. The combination of these 
methods allows us to balance computational efficiency with a more faithful use of the full posterior.

ence is similar (about 1.9), but the wide IQR ( 8.9 to +2.3) reflects the model’s highly variable performance—some 
galaxies show modest improvements over gNFW while many incur a significant evidence loss.
The rotating BEC model displays a positive median ∆ ln Zharm of about +1.1 and a wide upper quartile reaching  +8, 
implying that for a subset of galaxies the inclusion of rotation dramatically improves the fit. However, the large spread 
and occasional extreme outliers underscore that this gain is not universal; in many systems rotation adds complexity 
without a corresponding evidence increase.
Taken together, these results suggest that flexibility in the inner slope (gNFW) yields the most robust population–level 
improvement, whereas BEC halos—especially when rotation is included—can outperform gNFW for certain galaxies 
but at the cost of large variability.

4 Discussion
This analysis compares NFW, gNFW, and BEC (with and without rotation) halo models on SPARC rotation curves 
within a single Bayesian framework. Two lines of evidence drive our interpretation: (i) the distributions of evidence 
differences relative to gNFW (Fig. 4) and their summary statistics (Table 2); and (ii) the posterior behaviour of the 
inner slope parameter in gNFW, summarised by the sample KDE of best-fitting α (Fig. 3), which peaks near but slightly 
below the canonical NFW value of 1.
The evidence summaries in Table 2 show that, relative to gNFW, the fixed-slope NFW model generally underperforms 
across the sample, with a median ∆ ln Zharm  1.5 and an interquartile range extending down to about 6.4. This 
pattern is also visible in the left panel of Fig. 4, where the NFW–gNFW histogram skews toward negative values. 
The result indicates that allowing a free inner slope yields a modest but systematic improvement in fit quality for a 
substantial fraction of galaxies and this is also shown through the KDE of alpha.
The BEC models exhibit broader and more heterogeneous behaviour. The non-rotating BEC has a median similar 
in magnitude to NFW (about  1.9), but its interquartile range is wider (  [ 8.9, +2.3]), indicating considerable 
galaxy-to-galaxy variability. Introducing rotation into the BEC model shifts the median to positive values ( +1.1) and 
produces a long positive tail (IQR roughly [ 1.1, +7.8]), showing that a subset of galaxies benefit substantially from 
rotational support in the condensate model relative to the NFW. At the same time, the distribution remains broad: the 
positive gains are not universal and several systems still strongly disfavour BEC relative to gNFW.
The inner-slope diagnostics from gNFW are consistent with these evidence patterns. The KDE of best-fitting α across 
the sample peaks near α   0.918, close to the NFW value of 1 but spanning both cored (α < 1) and cusp-like 
(α ≳ 1) regimes. This dispersion in preferred inner slopes offers a natural explanation for why a flexible profile 
(gNFW) outperforms a fixed cusp (NFW) in median evidence, and also why BEC—which can mimic a core in some 
systems—occasionally achieves large positive evidence differences yet remains inconsistent across the full sample.
Methodologically, we report both a posterior-based harmonic-mean evidence and a BIC-derived approximation. The 
harmonic-mean approach used here leverages the full set of posterior samples and, in our implementation, employs a 
learned density model to stabilise the estimator; it is therefore more computationally involved but directly tied to the 
sampled posterior mass. By contrast, the BIC is a simple large-sample approximation based on the maximum likelihood 
and parameter count. Because the assumptions underlying the BIC-to-evidence mapping need not hold exactly for 
these data, agreement between the two should be interpreted as a coarse consistency check rather than a requirement. 
In practice, the qualitative ranking seen with the harmonic-mean estimator is reflected, though more weakly and with 
narrower tails, in the BIC-derived differences.
Two caveats are especially relevant. First, the treatment of stellar mass-to-light ratios can influence inner halo inferences. 
Within the scope of this work we adopt informative priors on Y d and Y b; broadening these priors provides a direct 
robustness test and is a natural extension. Second, other observational systematics (e.g. non-circular motions, bar/warp 
signatures, beam smearing) are not explicitly modelled here; the SPARC products do not by themselves allow uniform 
corrections across the full sample. A dedicated follow-up using higher-resolution inner kinematics and explicit inner-
radius quality cuts would help quantify their impact. We do not present a formal correlation analysis between α 
and global galaxy properties here. Methodologically, a Bayesian treatment provides an appropriate route for such 
investigations.
5 Conclusions
Within a common Bayesian framework applied to SPARC rotation curves, we find that allowing a free inner slope 
(gNFW) provides a modest but systematic evidence gain over fixed-slope NFW on average, consistent with the sample 
KDE of α peaking near  0.92 with substantial spread to both cored and cusp-like values. Bose–Einstein condensate 
halos exhibit heterogeneous performance: the non-rotating model shows wide scatter with a slightly negative median 
relative to gNFW, while the rotating variant achieves a positive median and a pronounced positive tail, indicating strong 
improvements for a subset of galaxies but not uniformly across the sample. These results favour flexible inner profiles 
at the population level and suggest that condensate rotation can play a role in individual cases, though with significant 
galaxy-to-galaxy variability.
Future work will extend the analysis along two axes. On the statistical side, we will (i) probe robustness to broader 
stellar mass-to-light priors and (ii) explore alternative evidence estimators (e.g. bridge or thermodynamic integration, 
nested sampling) alongside the learned harmonic-mean approach. On the astrophysical side, we will (i) perform a 
hierarchical population analysis of the α distribution and its dependence on galaxy properties and (ii) incorporate 
higher-resolution inner kinematics to better control non-circular motions and beam-smearing effects.
