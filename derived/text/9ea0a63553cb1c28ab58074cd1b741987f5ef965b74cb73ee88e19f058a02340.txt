1. Introduction: the terahertz gap and the limits of conventional sources 
The terahertz (THz) region of the electromagnetic spectrum, roughly spanning 0.1 –10 THz, has long been a 
formidable frontier in physics and engineering. Often called the "THz gap," this frequency range lies between 
the domains of well -established microwave  electronics and optical photonics, where neither traditional 
technologies provide efficient, high-power, and coherent sources. 
1.1. The Limitations of Microwave and Optical Approaches 
• Vacuum Electronics (Gyrotrons, Free -Electron Lasers): These devices can achieve high power, but 
require massive cryogenic magnetic systems (superconducting solenoids, 𝐵 > 5 T), are large in scale 
(often tens of meters for FELs), and are inherently inefficient at frequencies above 1 THz due to

CH₃OH) to generate THz radiation. However, they are plagued by self-terminating transitions (upper-
state lifetime much shorter than lower -state lifetime), severe  avalanche ionization  at high field 
strengths, and inherent diffraction limits that cause rapid beam divergence.

The conventional approach to THz generation is fundamentally based on  brute-force energy pumping —
injecting as much power as possible into a medium and hoping for efficient extraction. This inevitably leads
