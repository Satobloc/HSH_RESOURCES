1. Introduction: The Problem of Time in Modern Physics
The concept of time is at once the most universal and the least understood element of fundamental 
physics. Despite the unprecedented success of the two pillars of modern science — quantum  
mechanics (QM) and general relativity (GR) — their unification remains elusive, largely because 
of a fundamentally incompatible treatment of the nature of time. In each of the accepted physical  
paradigms, time plays a critical role, yet in none of them is it treated as a full-fledged physical  
object capable of possessing its own dynamics. This conceptual deficit leads to a series of deep  
theoretical crises.
Historical Perspective
The search for a single underlying principle from which the diversity of physical phenomena can  
be derived is one of the oldest traditions in theoretical physics.
In different eras, different candidates for such a foundational substrate have been proposed.  
Nineteenth-century physics often viewed the ether as the fundamental medium underlying  
electromagnetic phenomena. Later, the development of field theory shifted attention toward  
electromagnetic and quantum fields as the primary constituents of reality. General Relativity  
introduced a different perspective, identifying the geometry of spacetime itself as the fundamental 
structure governing gravitational phenomena.
The present work follows the same methodological tradition, without presupposing that its  
outcome will be comparable in scope or success. It does not introduce a new scientific method, but 
rather explores a different candidate for the role of the primary substrate.
The central hypothesis of the Temporal Theory of the Universe (TTU) is that time is not merely an 
external parameter used to describe physical processes. Instead, it is treated as a physical field  
possessing its own dynamics. Within this framework, geometry, gravity, matter, and information  
processes are investigated as possible emergent manifestations of a deeper temporal structure.
Whether this hypothesis is ultimately correct remains a matter for experiment and mathematical  
consistency. The purpose of the present work is therefore not to claim a final description of nature, 
but to explore the consequences of adopting time as the primary dynamical entity and to examine 
whether known physical structures can be consistently reconstructed from that assumption.

1.1. Time in Classical Physics: A Coordinate Without Dynamics
In classical Newtonian mechanics, time t is postulated as an absolute, true, and mathematical entity 
that “flows uniformly, without relation to anything external.” In this formalism, time acts as an  
ideal background metronome.
Mathematically, classical time is merely a parameter of evolution. It has no equation of motion of 
its own, possesses no energy or momentum, does not interact with matter, and experiences no back-
reaction from physical processes. Here time is an absolutely rigid, passive stage on which the  
dynamics of physical bodies unfold. In classical physics, time has no dynamics — it simply sets the 
rhythm.
1.2. Time in Quantum Mechanics: An External Parameter That Is Not Quantized
In standard quantum mechanics, despite its radical departure from classical determinism, the  
treatment of time remains strikingly Newtonian. In the Schrödinger equation:
iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Hψ (2)
time t appears as a continuous, external evolution parameter.
The fundamental asymmetry of quantum theory lies in the fact that position (the coordinate x) and 
momentum (p) are elevated to the status of Hermitian operators xK  and pK , which obey Heisenberg’s 
uncertainty principle. Time t, by contrast, is not an operator (the well-known Pauli theorem states 
that introducing a self-adjoint time operator canonically conjugate to the Hamiltonian leads to an  
energy spectrum unbounded from below). Thus quantum mechanics quantizes everything except  
time itself. Time remains an unquantized, classical parameter that exists, as it were, “outside” the 
quantum system, dictating its unitary evolution.
1.3. Time in General Relativity: A Geometric Coordinate Fused with Space
Albert Einstein, in special and later in general relativity, stripped time of its absolute status, turning 
it into the fourth dimension of a pseudo-Riemannian manifold — spacetime. Time became local  
and relative: it slows in gravitational fields and at speeds approaching that of light.
Nevertheless, despite this geometric flexibility, time in GR (x ) remains merely a coordinate.⁰  
Together with the spatial dimensions, it forms the metric tensor gμν, which describes the  
gravitational field. But the coordinate t itself has no physical substance of its own; it is dissolved

1.4. Consequences: The Problem of Time, the Absence of a Unified Description, and 
the Black-Hole Information Paradox
The attempt to unify quantum mechanics (in which time is an external, absolute parameter of  
evolution) with general relativity (in which time is a local, dynamically curved coordinate) leads to 
catastrophic contradictions:
• The Problem of Time: canonical quantization of gravity yields the Wheeler–DeWitt  
equation, HĤΨ = 0. This equation, which describes the wave function of the entire  
universe, contains no time derivative. The global universe turns out to be static; time  
mathematically vanishes from the fundamental description, in contradiction with our  
macroscopic experience of change.
• The black-hole information paradox: according to GR, gravitational collapse into a  
black hole produces a singularity where the curvature of spacetime diverges and time  
“stops.” When the black hole evaporates via Hawking radiation, a conflict arises: if time as 
a coordinate ends at the singularity, where does the quantum information go? The absence 
of a unified temporal background makes a correct description of this process impossible.
• Singularities: the infinities of the Big Bang and of black holes indicate that the classical  
continuous model of time has exhausted itself. The continuity of the coordinate t allows  
energy to be compressed into an infinitesimally small point.
Singularities and information paradoxes are a consequence of treating time as the “stage” and  
matter fields as the “actors.” However, if we recognize that time and the quantum field are one and 
the same physical entity, the mathematical infinities disappear. In this paper we show that time is 
not a background; it is the primary quantum field, whose excitations give rise to all known physical 
phenomena.
Conclusion to Section 1: all of these unresolved paradoxes point to a fundamental error — treating 
time exclusively as a passive coordinate (GR) or a background parameter (QM) is mistaken. The  
current crisis in theoretical physics demands a radical revision of ontology: time must be endowed 
with its own physical degrees of freedom, integrated into the structure of a quantum field.

7.5. Methodological Limitations and Open Problems
This work does not attempt a derivation of spacetime from absolute nothingness — a separate and

curvature, matter, and the gravitational and gauge interactions — is shown to emerge from the

Several aspects of this construction remain open and are stated explicitly below, so that the reader 
can distinguish established results from working hypotheses.
(i) The background manifold. The manifold M₅ = M₄ × S¹ — including its dimensionality,  
signature, and the compactness of Θ — is postulated as an effective scaffold on which the field  
τ(x,Θ) is defined; it is not derived from τ in this work. What is derived here is the effective metric, 
curvature, and gravitational dynamics induced by τ on this fixed background (Sections 4.1–4.4,  
Appendix D). A deeper, pre-geometric derivation of M₅ itself — for example from the phase  
structure or correlation geometry of the temporal condensate — remains an open problem for  
future work.
(ii) The thermal interpretation of Θ. As noted in Section 3.2, the identification of Θ with a  
Euclidean thermal circle is an interpretive analogy rather than a proven equivalence with finite-
temperature field theory.
(iii) The three-generation bound. The saturation argument of Section 4.3 does not predict exactly 
three generations from first principles with no free input; rather, it shows that the geometric  
saturation criterion | τ| < τ∇ crit provides a plausible mechanism for the observed three-generation  
pattern. A dedicated numerical study of the parameter dependence of nmax is left for future work.
(iv) The LA-ODR frequency scale. The derivation of the dimensionless infrared fixed point RΘ,LA ≈ 
1.245 lies outside the scope of the present paper and is given in Ref. [7]; here it is used only as an 
input to the falsifiable prediction of Section 7.4.
(v) Terminology. The term chronon has an established historical usage denoting a discrete, minimal 
unit of time (Caldirola, 1980; Snyder, 1947). In the present work it is repurposed to denote a scalar 
excitation of the temporal field τ (Section 5.3) and should not be conflated with that earlier  
literature.
