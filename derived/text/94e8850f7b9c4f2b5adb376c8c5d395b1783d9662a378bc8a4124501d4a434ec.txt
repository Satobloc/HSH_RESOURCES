Evidence for Three Subpopulations of Merging Binary Black Holes at Different
Primary Masses
Sharan Banagiri , * Eric Thrane , and Paul D. Lasky
School of Physics and Astronomy, Monash University , Clayton Victoria 3800, Australia
and OzGrav: The ARC Centre of Excellence for Gravitational Wave Discovery , Clayton Victoria 3800, Australia
(Received 29 September 2025; revised 18 February 2026; accepted 8 May 2026; published 6 July 2026)
With the release of the fourth LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA gravitational-wave catalog (GWTC-4), we are
starting to gain a detailed view of the population of merging binary black holes. The formation channels of
these black holes is not clearly understood, but different formation mechanisms may lead to subpopulations
with different properties visible in gravitational-wave data. Adopting a phenomenological approach, we
find GWTC-4.0 data supports the presence of at least three subpopulations, each associated with a different
range of black hole mass and with sharp transition boundaries between them. Each subpopulation is
characterized by different distributions for either the mass ratios, the black-hole spin magnitudes or both.
Subpopulation A with primary mass m1 ≤ 27.7þ4.1
−3.4 M⊙ (90% credibility), is characterized by a nearly flat
mass ratio distribution q ¼ m2=m1, and by small spin magnitudes ( χ ≤ 0.5þ0.1
−0.1 ). Subpopulation B, with
27.7þ4.1
−3.4 M⊙ ≤ m1 ≤ 40.2þ4.7
−3.2 M⊙, has a much sharper preference for mass ratio q ≈ 1. Subpopulation C,
with m1 ≥ 40.2þ4.7
−3.2 M⊙, has support for large spin magnitudes, and tentative support for mass ratios
q ≈ 0.5. We interpret these transitions as evidence for multiple subpopulations, each potentially associated
with a different formation pathways. We suggest putative formation scenarios for each subpopulation, and
explore chemically homogeneous evolution, population III stars and dynamical formation channels as an
explanation for subpopulation B. Our findings for subpopulation C are largely consistent with recent claims
of hierarchical mergers but with some curious differences in properties.

Introduction—The sensitivity of gravitational wave (GW)
detectors has increased massively in the last ten years[1,2] as
a result of technological upgrades [3–6] and an improved
understanding of instrumental noise [7]. With the recent
GWTC-4.0 catalog [8–10] by LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (L VK)
[11–13], the number of confidently detected binary black
hole (BBH) mergers has now surpassed 150, offering an
unprecedented view into the nature and formation of these
systems and their populations [14]. In the early days of GW
astronomy, it seemed plausible that just a small number of
detections might enable us to determine the primary for-
mation channel of of BBHs mergers (e.g., [15–17]).
However, this hope has since dissipated as GW data has
unveiled a rich and complex picture of the population of
merging binaries, not easily explained with a single for-
mation channel. Furthermore, uncertainties in the physics
underlying black hole formation and merger among the
various possible channels make it hard to obtain robust
predictions (see Ref. [18] for a review). The relative con-
tributions of various BBH formation channels is still a topic
of active study (e.g., [19–22]).
Several analyses have claimed evidence of BBH sub-
populations. References [14–16,23–27] used BBH spin tilts
to discriminate between different subpopulations. While
promising from a theoretical perspective [28–31], using this
as a clean discriminator has proven difficult. First, precise
measurements of BBH spin tilts with GWs is difficult.
Second, recent studies have challenged commonly held
assumptions and shown that these models can be subject to
significant theoretical uncertainties [32–35]. There is also
evidence for global correlations between various parame-
ters [14,36–39] but interpreting them astrophysically is not
straightforward.
Another potentially fruitful way is to adopt a phenom-
enological approach: to look for subpopulations that have
clear and distinguishable properties. If two groups of
binaries are found to have different distributions for some
parameters like mass ratio, spin or redshift, one can then
infer that the relevant physical processes behind their
formations are different [40]. Several studies have pursued
this strategy to search for subpopulations with interesting
properties [14,27,41–51].
It can be particularly informative to find subpopulations
separated by clear transition masses. Knowing both the
BBH mass scales and their properties can enable us to
constrain and infer their formation physics. This is the
approach we take in this Letter by searching for subpopu-
lations with distinct primary black hole masses. Using
cumulative GWTC-4.0 data [10], we find evidence for at

Methods and results —We model the BBH population
with simple phenomenological components. We use the
strongly parametrized fiducial models from Ref. [14]. The
primary mass distribution pðm1Þ is a broken power law
with two Gaussian peaks. The spin magnitude distributions
pðχÞ are (independently and identically distributed) trun-
cated normals, and the spin tilt distributions pðcos θÞ are
mixtures between an isotropic component, and an aligned
component modeled with a truncated Gaussian distribution
[25,52]. The redshift distributions pðzÞ are modeled as a
power law in (1 þ z) [53]. Finally, we model the mass-ratio
distributions, pðqÞ, where q ¼ m2=m1, either as a power
law or a truncated normal.
A key feature of our work is that the distributions of z, q,
χ and cos θ are allowed to change shape at up to three
transition masses, corresponding to four subpopulations
[54]. The functional form of the distribution may be the
same on either side of the transition, but the hyper-
parameters controlling the shape are allowed to have
distinct values. For example, our model can accommodate
a scenario where binaries with m1 < 30M⊙ are charac-
terized by a mass ratio distribution of pðqÞ ∝ q2, but
binaries with m1 > 30 are characterized by a mass ratio
distribution pðqÞ¼ const. More details about model
parameterization and the priors used can be found in
Supplemental Material [55].
We use standard hierarchical Bayesian inference tech-
niques [56–58] for inferring the BBH population properties.
We analyze data cumulative to GWTC-4.0[10,59,60,70,71]
including all BBHs mergers with a false alarm rate less than
1 yr−1. We do not include GW190814 [72] because of the
ambiguous nature of the secondary. We also do not include
GW231123 [73] in our analysis, driven by concerns that the
seemingly extremal spins makes it susceptible to poorly
understood waveform systematics. Moreover, if its measured
properties are real, the most parsimonious explanation would
likely put it in a class of its own highly spinning, ultramassive
BBH systems [74].
We then tested several models allowing up to three
transition masses. These yield a more-or-less consistent
picture of the BBH population, wherein we find subpo-
pulations with different mass-ratio or spin magnitude
distributions, but no statistically significant evidence that
the distribution of other parameters have a transition. We
relied on Bayes factors for model selection and to avoid
over fitting the data. Bayes factors carry an Occam penalty
for more complex models and ensure that a more flexible
model is selected only when its flexibility is required by the
data. We direct the reader to Sec. 3 of Ref. [57] for an
overview on Bayes factors and Bayesian model selection in
gravitational-wave astronomy.
The rest of this Letter focuses on the model with the
highest Bayes factor. All quoted uncertainties correspond to
90% credible levels unless stated otherwise.

we refer to this model as MULTIPOP. An overview of our

We now turn to measuring the statistical significance of
individual features and transitions. We define two different
metrics for this purpose. The first is the relative Bayes
factor, BR, which is the gain in evidence when a new feature
is added to GWTC4-FIDUCIAL model. For example, we can
compare the evidence after adding a transition in pðqÞ to
GWTC4-FIDUCIAL with the evidence without any transitions.
We also define the differential Bayes factor, BD which
compares the evidence of the MULTIPOP model [Eqs. (1) and
(2)] to the evidence when a feature is taken out. We direct
the reader to Eqs. (S7) and (S8) in Supplemental Material
[55] for formal definitions. These two metrics allow us to
gauge the significance of a feature both on its own, and in
conjunction with other features that capture the presence of
multiple subpopulations. Table I shows the ln BR and ln BD
values for the features identified by the MULTIPOP model.
The individual feature most strongly supported by data is
that subpopulation C has a distinct spin-magnitude distri-

Finally, we note that when we tested a variant of MULTIPOP
that models the mass-ratio distribution of subpopulation C

to the standard version described in Eq. (1).
Astrophysical interpretation —We turn now to the astro-
physical interpretation of these subpopulations, guided not
just by the observed transitions inpðqÞ and pðχÞ but also the
lack of any transitions in spin-tilt or redshift distributions.
Subpopulation A: This subpopulation is characterized
by a somewhat flat mass-ratio distribution and spin
magnitudes constrained at χ ≤ 0.5. Astroseismological
observations of low mass stars find highly effective
angular momentum transport [80], which extrapolated
to more massive stars imply black hole spins with
χ ≲ 0.01 [81,82]. The fact that we see χ > 0.01 for this
subpopulation implies that they have been spun up or
retained their angular momentum in some other way.
Alternatively, such assumptions of extremely-efficient
angular momentum transport might be incorrect for mas-
sive stars; models based on the Taylor-dynamo [83] suggest

Subpopulation B: This subpopulation is characterized by
a stronger preference for equal mass pairing. The prefer-

independently models and pairs pðm1Þ and pðm2Þ. Thus,
we propose that the previously identified m1 bump might
be due to model misspecification [91]. Future data and
analysis with different models will help to shed more light
on the possibility of misspecification and the true nature of
this feature.

equivalent to our subpopulation B. Comparing GW infer-

they conclude that none of the formation channels considered

While we mostly agree with their assessment, we highlight
two potentially promising formation scenarios. The first is
chemically homogeneous evolution (CHE) [93–97]. Unlike
Ref. [92], we find a gentler and flatter pðm1Þ, but still with a
preference for equal-mass pairing, consistent with CHE[98].
Perhaps a more difficult thing to reconcile with this formation
scenario is that we do not find any difference in spin
magnitude or tilt distributions between subpopulation B
and subpopulation A. While CHE typically involves stellar
progenitors that are rotating at a significant fraction of their
critical speed, the spins of the subsequent black holes are
much more uncertain, with predictions ranging from χ ≈
0.4–1 [97,99,100]. Subpopulation B might be consistent
with CHE formation if the black holes are spun up toχ ≈ 0.4;
see Fig. 2.
Another potential origin for this subpopulation is from
population III stars. Multiple studies robustly identify a
characteristic excess of BBH mergers with masses around
30M⊙ [101–104] from population III stars. However, the
star-formation rate (and hence the merger rate) of such
systems should naturally peak at high redshift. While some
fraction can experience long delay times and merge at low
redshift, a degree of fine tuning is likely required to ensure
the same star-formation history as the rest of the BBHs
subpopulation. However, we caution that this conclusion
can be susceptible to model misspecification as we only
looked for (and failed to find) different redshift distribu-
tions at clearly separated m1 ranges.
Some studies have suggested a dynamical origin for this
subpopulation [44]. Canonically, such a population should
have randomly aligned spins [29] and— assuming highly
efficient angular momentum transport [82]— very small
spin magnitudes. As we do not find evidence for a separate
spin-magnitude or tilt distribution from Subpopulation A,
this interpretation is somewhat at odds with our results.
However, the caveat about assumptions of extremely
efficient angular momentum transport for massive stars
also apply here and, as previously mentioned, models based
on the Taylor-dynamo [83] suggest that the spin of isolated
black holes can be as high as χ ≈ 0.1 [84–86]. Furthermore,
recent suggestions of stellar collisions in dense clusters [35]
might be able to produce aligned spinning BBHs through
dynamical scenarios.
Finally, it is unlikely that this subpopulation originates
from pair-instability physics, a possibility that attracted
much attention in the early days of GW astronomy
[105,106]. However, multiple studies recently have argued
that the putative pileup due to pair-instability physics
should lie at least above 40M⊙ (for e.g. [107–111]), and
more likely above 45m⊙— beyond the upper limit of this
subpopulation. Although, for an alternative point of view
see Refs. [112,113]. In any case, with the more complex
pairing model introduced in this work, evidence of a pileup
at 35M⊙ is diminished. We find tentative support for a
bump in m2 at ≈30M⊙, but this may be too low of a scale
for pair instability physics to be relevant.
Subpopulation C: The idea that massive black holes
might be formed from hierarchical mergers of earlier
generations of black holes has been explored with some
of the first unexpectedly massive BBHs detected by the
L VK (for, e.g., [114–120]). Recently, this hypothesis has
gained traction because of the discovery of an apparent
transition in the effective spin distribution [27,45,46] and
an apparent gap in the distribution of secondary mass [48].
Our inference of the mass-ratio distribution subpopula-
tion C— with a mean at q ≈ 0.5— provides further tentative
support for the hierarchical interpretation as this qualita-
tively resembles the distribution one might expect from the
merger of a second-generation and a first-generation black
holes. While the inferred spin distribution is very broad, it
does have support at at χ ∼ 0.7 (Fig. 2), consistent with the
interpretations of Refs. [27,45,46,48].
On the other hand, this distribution also has support at
small χ, indicating that a fraction of this subpopulation has
small spins. Furthermore, we found that a variant of
MULTIPOP that allowed only pðχ1Þ to transition at mt
BC is
disfavored by ln B ¼ 1.5 compared to the version that lets
both pðχ1Þ and pðχ2Þ transition. Another potential incon-
sistency is that we find only a transition in the distribution
of spin magnitudes but not of spin tilts between subpopu-
lation C and the rest of BBH population. These features, if
confirmed, might suggest that hierarchical mergers in dense
star clusters cannot fully explain subpopulation C. Alternate
scenarios such as CHE [97,99,100] and accretion-based
spin up in AGN disks [121] might better explain some of
these features. However, it is hard to see how either of these
explanations would explain the preference for q ≈ 0.5 if
that is confirmed by future data. Conversely, alternative
accretion scenarios have been proposed that might be able
to explain these properties better [122,123].

Conclusion—In this Letter, we investigate the possibility
of multiple subpopulations of BBHs each associated with a
different range of black hole masses. We find strong
evidence that there are at least three such subpopulations
with different mass-ratio and spin-magnitude distributions,
with transitions between them inferred at 27.7þ4.1
−3.4 M⊙ and
40.2þ4.7
−3.2 M⊙. A caveat to these results is that we use
strongly parametrized models, potentially subject to model
misspecification. Model misspecification of the pðqÞ dis-
tributions might be particularly important (Fig. 3) as we can
resolve the features more precisely in the mass distributions
than with spins or redshift. While our results are internally
self-consistent when the power-law and normal distribu-
tions are swapped, testing with more models and future
data can always help shed light on model misspecifications.
However, the strength of the signal and the fact that these
transition points have been individually found in other
analyses [27,45,46,48,49,124,125] suggests that even with
some degree of model misspecification, the presence of
three subpopulations and their broad features are real.
Furthermore, these results imply that usual assumptions
of separability of the population distribution of BBH
parameters breaks down above ∼25M⊙.
Finally, our work focuses mostly on BBHs at higher
masses as we do not find any clearly localized subpopula-
tions at m1 < 25M⊙. In particular, we do not find evidence
for any transitions around the10M⊙ peak. There are reasons
to expect substructure at this peak, both based on previous
observations [14,42,47,126] and based on theoretical con-
siderations. Isolated binary formation channels that undergo
stable mass transfer often predict a peak near10M⊙ (for, e.g.,
Refs. [127–131]). Some studies of cluster formation chan-
nels also find a peak around 10M⊙ [132,133] while others
[134] place the peak at higher masses. We plan to revisit this
subpopulation in a future study.
