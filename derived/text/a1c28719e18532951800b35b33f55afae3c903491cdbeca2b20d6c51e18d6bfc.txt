CONSISTENCY WITHOUT COHERENCE
On the Unfalsifiable Substrate of Empirical Physics
Eris Arete
Independent Researcher
Abstract
Physics presents itself as the paradigm of empirical accountability, yet its mathematical 
apparatus is judged by internal consistency rather than empirical coherence. This essay 
argues that the asymmetry—between a substantive theory layer subject to empirical test 
and a mathematical apparatus layer insulated by consistency—systematically favors 
framework preservation through auxiliary elaboration over framework revision. The 
pattern is illustrated through five cases: the luminiferous aether, dark matter and dark 
energy, the cosmological constant problem, the proliferation of competing quantum gravity 
programs, and the symmetrization postulate. Each exhibits the same structural dynamic: 
framework commitments and the empirical record diverge; the substantive theory is 
elaborated; the framework is preserved; the cumulative effect is the accumulation of 
unfalsifiable elaborations and the foreclosure of alternatives at coherence-based grounds. 
The essay specifies the criteria—Lakatosian progressiveness applied at framework level, 
combined with explicit ansatz commitment—by which alternative frameworks should be 
evaluated, addressing the circularity worry that the call to alternatives might otherwise 
raise. The essay concludes that the philosophy of physics must treat alternative-framework 
construction as a structural responsibility rather than a marginal pursuit: only through the 
development of formally precise alternatives can foundational commitments be rendered 
accountable to evidence at the level where the discipline currently exempts them.
Keywords: consistency vs. coherence; mathematical apparatus; framework preservation; dark 
matter; cosmological constant; quantum gravity; symmetrization postulate; relational ontology; 
alternative frameworks; philosophy of physics.

I. The Standard View
Physics presents itself, both to philosophers of science and to the broader public, as the paradigm 
of empirical accountability. Its claims are formulated such that they could in principle be wrong; 
its theories are revised in the face of disconfirming evidence; its progress consists in the 
replacement of less accurate accounts by more accurate ones. The standard philosophical accounts 
of how physics achieves these features form a familiar lineage. Popper’s demarcation criterion 
holds that scientific theories distinguish themselves from non-scientific systems by being framed 
in ways that admit of empirical falsification; a claim that cannot in principle be refuted by any 
conceivable observation is, on Popper’s account, not a scientific claim at all.1 Lakatos refined this 
picture by recognizing that working scientists rarely abandon a theory on the basis of a single 
disconfirming result; they operate within research programmes whose hard cores are protected by 
belts of auxiliary hypotheses that absorb anomalies, with the programme as a whole adjudicated 
by whether it remains progressive—predicting novel facts—or degenerates into a series of ad hoc 
rescues.2 Kuhn supplied the historical and sociological complement, describing the periods of 
normal science in which a paradigm’s puzzle-solving capacity is taken for granted, and the periods 
of revolutionary science in which an accumulation of anomalies prompts the paradigm’s 
replacement.3
These accounts have been developed, criticized, and refined across the subsequent decades. 
Contemporary philosophers of physics have offered substantial defenses of the empirical-
accountability picture against various challenges. Maudlin has argued that the foundational debates 
in quantum mechanics and relativity have been settled, in their philosophical essentials, by careful 
attention to what the theories themselves say about the world. 4 Earman has developed extended 
analyses of how general relativity is and is not subject to empirical revision, distinguishing the

to human inquiry, with disagreements about specific cases sitting against a shared background

This essay does not contest the standard view as a description of physics’ aspirations or as an 
account of the discipline at its best. It accepts that physicists’ substantive empirical claims—about 
specific particles, specific forces, specific cosmological parameters—are formulated in ways that 
admit of empirical test, and are revised when test results conflict with prior expectation. The 
General Relativistic prediction of light deflection at the 1919 eclipse, the prediction of the Higgs 
boson at the LHC, the discovery of the cosmic microwave background by Penzias and Wilson, the 
prediction and detection of gravitational waves by LIGO—these episodes are paradigmatic 
instances of the standard picture operating as advertised. Theory predicts; experiment tests; theory 
survives or is revised. The essay accepts that this is happening, in the cases where it happens.
What the essay raises is a structural asymmetry that operates beneath these surface successes. The 
substantive empirical claims of physics—the claims about particles, forces, fields, cosmological 
parameters, and the rest—are formulated within a mathematical apparatus that itself shapes what 
counts as a substantive empirical claim, what counts as a permissible theory-space within which 
such claims can be formulated, and what counts as anomaly versus what counts as feature. The 
mathematical apparatus is not itself a substantive empirical claim. It is the substrate within which 
substantive empirical claims become formulable. And this substrate is not subject to the 
falsifiability standard that physics applies to its substantive claims. The substrate is constrained by 
internal consistency requirements—by the demand that its deductions follow from its axioms, that 
its theorems not contradict one another, that its formal apparatus cohere with itself. It is not 
constrained by coherence requirements in the empirical sense—by the demand that its outputs 
match what experiment produces in the world.

This is the asymmetry the essay names and develops. The substantive claims of physics are subject 
to one epistemological standard; the mathematical substrate on which those claims rest is subject 
to a different and weaker standard. Both standards are reasonable in their own terms. Mathematical 
apparatus that was internally contradictory would be useless; mathematical apparatus whose 
external coherence-with-evidence requirement was elevated to the same status as its internal-
consistency requirement would dissolve mathematics as a discipline, since mathematical objects 
are not themselves empirical and their truths are not vindicated by observation. The point of the 
essay is not that mathematics ought to be falsifiable in the empirical sense, which it cannot be. The 
point is that physics, in adopting a particular mathematical apparatus and treating that apparatus 
as the framework within which all physical questions must be posed, inherits both the consistency 
requirement and the absence of empirical-coherence requirement. The framework’s choices about 
what kind of entities can exist, what kind of properties they can have, what kind of relations they 
can stand in, are not themselves subject to revision in the face of empirical anomaly. They are 
subject only to revision in the face of internal inconsistency, which the framework rarely exhibits 
because it has been designed to be internally consistent.
The consequence is that empirical anomalies in physics are routinely resolved by elaborations of 
the framework rather than by revisions of it. When the framework’s outputs and the empirical 
record diverge, the empirical record is treated as a puzzle for the framework to eventually 
accommodate, rather than as evidence that the framework’s assumptions about possible-entities 
and possible-relations may require revision. This is not a moral failing of physicists. It is a 
structural consequence of the framework’s epistemic status: because the framework itself is not 
subject to empirical falsification, the natural response to disconfirming evidence is to seek a 
framework-internal accommodation rather than to question the framework. The accommodations 
accumulate. Each accommodation is locally reasonable. The cumulative effect is a framework 
increasingly elaborated, with increasingly elaborate auxiliary entities and increasingly elaborate 
auxiliary postulates, none of which can be falsified at the level the framework operates because 
the framework’s mathematical structure forecloses on questions that would put the framework 
itself at risk.
The essay develops this asymmetry in five sections. Section II introduces the philosophical 
literature on the relationship between mathematics and physics, and locates the asymmetry within

Section VI argues that the philosophy of physics must take seriously the question of what 
alternative mathematical frameworks would look like, not as a matter of intellectual hobby but as 
a structural requirement for physics to remain accountable to evidence.
II. Mathematics and Physics: A Relationship Worth Examining
The relationship between mathematics and physics has been a topic of sustained philosophical 
attention since at least the early twentieth century, but the literature exhibits a striking pattern. 
Where philosophers have addressed the relationship, they have largely addressed two questions: 
why mathematics is so effective in describing physical reality, and what the ontological status of 
mathematical objects must be if such effectiveness is to be intelligible. The first question is 
associated with Wigner’s famous essay on “the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the 
natural sciences,” in which he observed that mathematical concepts developed without reference 
to physical application have proven, with striking regularity, to be exactly the concepts physics 
turns out to need. 6 The second question is associated with the Quine-Putnam indispensability 
argument, which holds that since mathematics is indispensable to our best scientific theories, and 
since we should be ontologically committed to the entities indispensable to our best theories, we 
should accept the existence of mathematical objects on broadly empiricist grounds. 7 Maddy’s 
subsequent naturalist response has refined this picture by distinguishing the practice of

This picture is not quite right, and the ways in which it is not quite right matter for the essay’s 
central argument. Mathematical frameworks are not epistemologically neutral with respect to what 
they can be used to represent. They embody commitments about what kinds of entities exist, what 
kinds of properties such entities can have, what kinds of relations they can stand in. These 
commitments are not themselves derived from empirical inquiry; they are inherited from the 
framework’s foundational choices, which were made for reasons largely internal to mathematics 
itself—for reasons of formal economy, of internal consistency, of generality, of fit with the 
surrounding mathematical apparatus that the framework needs to interoperate with. A framework 
built around discrete entities and their properties will permit certain kinds of physical theories and 
exclude others. A framework built around continuous fields and their values will permit different 
theories and exclude different others. A framework that treats spatial location as a primitive will 
permit different theories than one that treats interaction as primitive. The choice of mathematical 
framework is not posterior to physical inquiry; it is constitutive of what physical inquiry can find.
This observation is not novel in the philosophy of mathematics, where the constitutive role of 
mathematical frameworks has been examined in the foundations literature.12 Nor is it novel in the 
philosophy of physics, where authors including Stein and Belot have noted that the mathematical 
structure of physical theories carries commitments not always foregrounded by the theories’ 
physical interpretations. 13 What has been less examined is the asymmetry this constitutive role 
introduces between the substantive empirical claims of physics and the mathematical framework 
within which those claims are formulated. The substantive claims are subject to falsification by 
empirical evidence. The framework within which they are formulated is not subject to falsification 
by empirical evidence, because the framework determines what counts as evidence in the first 
place. A framework that does not permit a certain kind of entity to exist will not register evidence

The implications of this asymmetry have been noted in passing by various authors but have not, to 
the essay’s knowledge, been developed into a sustained philosophical analysis. Lakatos came 
closest, in his treatment of hard cores and protective belts; the hard core of a research programme, 
on his account, includes mathematical commitments that are protected from revision by the belt of 
auxiliary hypotheses around them. 14 But Lakatos’s framework was concerned with the empirical 
progressiveness of research programmes, not with the structural question of whether the hard 
core’s mathematical commitments are themselves subject to empirical accountability in any 
meaningful sense. He acknowledged that they are not, and treated this as a description of how 
science actually works rather than as a problem requiring philosophical attention. The essay takes 
the same observation as Lakatos’s starting point and treats it as a problem requiring attention.
The problem has the following structure. Physics, as a discipline, presents its claims as empirically 
accountable. Empirical accountability requires that the claims could in principle be falsified by 
evidence. Falsification by evidence requires that the framework within which the claims are 
formulated permits the evidence to register as evidence. If the framework does not permit a class 
of possible evidence to register, the claims formulated within the framework cannot be falsified 
by that class of evidence—not because the evidence does not exist, but because the framework 
forecloses on the question of whether it exists. The framework’s choices about what kinds of 
entities and relations are possible are therefore prior to the empirical accountability physics claims 
for its substantive results. The framework is not itself empirically accountable. The substantive 
results that depend on it are accountable only within the framework. The accountability is real, but 
bounded; the boundary is set by the framework; the framework itself sits outside the accountability 
it makes possible for its outputs.
This is not yet an argument that the framework is wrong, or that physics is in any specific way 
deluded about what it is doing. It is an observation about the structural relationship between the 
framework and the substantive empirical claims. The observation creates a question: what happens

when the framework’s structural commitments and the empirical record diverge? Two responses 
are available in principle. The first is to elaborate the framework—to add auxiliary entities, 
auxiliary postulates, auxiliary hypotheses that accommodate the divergence within the 
framework’s existing commitments. The second is to revise the framework—to question whether 
the framework’s structural commitments themselves require modification in light of the 
divergence. The essay’s central claim, to be developed across the following sections, is that 
contemporary physics consistently selects the first response and consistently declines the second, 
and that this consistent selection is itself a structural feature of the discipline’s epistemic situation, 
not a contingent feature subject to easy correction.
Section III develops the philosophical machinery the essay needs to make this argument precise. 
The machinery is the distinction between consistency and coherence, between the internal logical 
integrity of a formal system and the system’s correspondence with the empirical record. Both are 
reasonable epistemic standards. They are not equivalent. When they diverge, a choice must be 
made about which to prefer. Contemporary physics, the essay will argue, has increasingly 
preferred consistency, and the preference has costs the field has not adequately acknowledged.
III. Consistency and Coherence
The distinction the essay relies on is between two epistemic standards that a body of claims can be 
expected to satisfy. The first standard, consistency, is internal. A body of claims is consistent if its 
members do not contradict one another, if its inferential apparatus produces no theorem that 
conflicts with another theorem, if its formal structure does not yield both a proposition and its 
negation as derivable. Consistency is a property a body of claims has with respect to itself. It is 
verified by examining the body of claims in isolation, checking that the inferential moves the body 
permits do not generate contradictions, and confirming that the axioms from which the body is 
generated do not themselves imply incompatible conclusions. A body of claims is consistent or it 
is not, and the question of its consistency is, in principle, settleable by formal examination alone.
The second standard, coherence, is external. A body of claims is coherent with the empirical record 
if its predictions match what experiment produces, if its descriptions accord with what observation 
discloses, if its outputs correspond to what the world delivers when interrogated. Coherence is a 
property a body of claims has with respect to something outside itself. It is verified by comparing

===== PAGE 10 =====
the body’s outputs with measurements taken from a domain the body purports to describe, and 
confirming that the comparison yields agreement within whatever tolerances the inquiry has 
established as acceptable. A body of claims is coherent with the empirical record or it is not, and 
the question of its coherence is settled empirically, by inquiry into the domain the body claims to 
describe.
These two standards are distinct. A body of claims can be consistent without being coherent. 
Euclidean geometry is internally consistent—the proofs follow from the axioms, no theorem 
contradicts another—but is not coherent with the geometric structure of the physical universe at 
large scales, where general relativity tells us spacetime is curved and parallel lines do not preserve 
their parallelism. Euclidean geometry remains a perfectly good mathematical theory; what it is not 
is a description of how the physical universe geometrically behaves. A body of claims can also be 
coherent without being fully consistent, or at least without having its consistency fully verified. 
Early calculus achieved striking coherence with empirical mechanics for nearly two centuries 
before its foundations were rigorously developed; Newton’s fluxions and Leibniz’s infinitesimals 
were operationally effective long before they were given formally consistent treatment in the work 
of Cauchy and Weierstrass. The mathematics worked, in the sense of producing predictions that 
matched experiment, before anyone had demonstrated that the mathematics did not generate 
contradictions when used as Newton and Leibniz had used it.
Mathematics, as a discipline, prioritizes consistency. This is not a flaw in mathematics; it is 
constitutive of what mathematics is. Mathematical objects are not empirical objects; mathematical 
theorems are not empirical claims; mathematical proofs do not depend on observation for their 
warrant. The discipline’s standards of validation are internal to its inferential structure, and rightly 
so. A mathematical claim is vindicated by being demonstrably derivable from accepted axioms by 
accepted inferential rules, not by being verified through experimental observation. Mathematics 
that fails to be consistent is mathematics that has failed by its own standards. Mathematics that 
fails to be empirically coherent is, on the discipline’s own terms, not failing at all—mathematics 
was never offering empirical coherence as a deliverable.
Physics, as a discipline, prioritizes coherence—or at any rate claims to. Physical theories are 
vindicated by experimental confirmation; they are revised when experiment delivers results the 
theory did not predict; their warrant depends on their continuing to match what the world produces

===== PAGE 11 =====
under controlled interrogation. A physical theory that is internally consistent but empirically 
incoherent has failed; the inconsistency between its predictions and the experimental record is 
reason to revise it, regardless of how elegant or formally satisfying its mathematical structure 
might be. This commitment to coherence over consistency, when the two diverge, is what 
distinguishes physics from mathematics as a discipline. Mathematics asks: does the argument 
follow? Physics asks: does the world behave as the argument predicts? The two questions have 
different criteria of success, and the disciplines have been organized accordingly.
The trouble begins when physics adopts a mathematical apparatus as its medium of expression. 
The apparatus is, of necessity, mathematical—it inherits the consistency standard of mathematics, 
since a mathematical apparatus that was internally inconsistent would be useless even as a 
representational tool. But the apparatus also carries commitments: about what kinds of entities 
exist, what kinds of properties they can have, what kinds of relations they can stand in. These 
commitments are inherited from the mathematics’ foundational choices, which were made for 
reasons internal to mathematics. The commitments are protected by the consistency standard—
they cannot be revised in ways that introduce contradiction. They are not, however, protected by 
the coherence standard, because mathematics does not operate under a coherence standard. When 
the apparatus’s commitments and the empirical record diverge, the apparatus itself is not at risk; 
what is at risk is the substantive theory built on the apparatus.
The asymmetry this creates is the structural fact the essay identifies. The substantive theory is 
subject to coherence: it must match the empirical record, or be revised. The apparatus is subject 
only to consistency: it must be internally non-contradictory, but it need not match anything outside 
itself. When the substantive theory and the empirical record diverge, the response is to revise the 
theory, not the apparatus. When such revisions accumulate—when the substantive theory has been 
repeatedly elaborated to accommodate empirical surprises while the apparatus remains 
untouched—the discipline has, in effect, treated the apparatus as more secure than the empirical 
record itself. The apparatus survives unchanged across surprises that, if directed at the substantive 
theory alone, would have prompted revision. The apparatus has been protected from the coherence 
standard by being treated as part of mathematics rather than as part of physics.
This is the central diagnostic the essay offers. Physics presents itself as a coherence-prioritizing 
discipline. In practice, the discipline operates with two layers: a substantive theory layer at which

===== PAGE 12 =====
the coherence standard applies, and a mathematical apparatus layer at which only the consistency 
standard applies. The two layers are entangled—the substantive theory is formulated within the 
apparatus, and the apparatus shapes what counts as a permissible substantive theory—but they are 
subject to different epistemic standards. The substantive theory must be coherent with evidence; 
the apparatus need only be consistent with itself. When divergence between framework outputs 
and evidence arises, the discipline’s structural response is to seek revisions at the substantive layer 
that preserve the apparatus, because revisions at the apparatus layer would require giving up 
commitments that the discipline does not have a procedure for revising. The apparatus is, in effect, 
treated as background dogma—not because anyone has decided to treat it as dogma, but because 
the discipline’s two-layer structure makes the apparatus immune to the standard that would 
otherwise hold it accountable.
The word dogma is strong, and the essay uses it with care. It does not mean that the apparatus is 
arbitrary, that its choices were made without reason, or that mathematicians lacked good motives 
for the choices they made. The apparatus is, in its own terms, a remarkable intellectual 
achievement—internally consistent, generative, capable of representing extraordinary varieties of 
structure, refined across centuries of mathematical work. Calling it dogma is not a judgment about 
the apparatus’s mathematical merits. It is a judgment about its epistemic status within physics: it 
is treated as a fixed starting point rather than as a hypothesis subject to revision in light of evidence. 
The apparatus’s mathematical merits are what they are; the question is whether the discipline that 
adopted the apparatus as its medium has retained the capacity to question the adoption when the 
adoption produces sustained divergence from evidence.
The standard response to this diagnostic is to deny the asymmetry. The mathematical apparatus of 
physics, the response holds, is not dogma; it is whatever apparatus has proven most empirically 
successful. If the apparatus stopped being empirically successful, the discipline would adopt a 
different apparatus. The current apparatus is therefore not protected from empirical revision; it has 
been empirically vindicated, repeatedly, across the long history of physics’ successes.
This response is half right. The mathematical apparatus of physics has been empirically vindicated 
at the level of the substantive theories built on it; the predictions of general relativity, the 
predictions of quantum field theory, the calculations that produced the standard model and its 
various confirmations have been spectacular successes by any reasonable standard. What the

===== PAGE 13 =====
response elides is the structural question: would the discipline recognize the apparatus’s failure if 
the apparatus were failing? The discipline’s structural response to divergence is to revise the 
substantive theory while preserving the apparatus. If the apparatus is failing, the response that 
would detect the failure is precisely the response the discipline does not perform. Empirical 
vindication, on this picture, is a vindication of the substantive theory built on the apparatus, and at 
most an indirect vindication of the apparatus itself. The apparatus has been empirically vindicated 
in the sense that some substantive theory formulated within it has succeeded. Whether a 
substantive theory formulated within a different apparatus would succeed at the questions the 
current apparatus has struggled with is a question the current discipline does not have the procedure 
to answer, because answering it would require taking the apparatus itself as a hypothesis rather 
than as a starting point.
The essay’s substantive cases, developed in the next section, are intended to demonstrate that the 
structural pattern just described is operating in identifiable contemporary physics. Each case is one 
in which the apparatus’s commitments and the empirical record have diverged; in which the 
discipline’s response has been to elaborate substantive theory rather than to revise the apparatus; 
and in which the elaborations have accumulated to a degree that, on a coherence-prioritizing 
standard, would justify questioning the apparatus itself. The cases are not chosen as gotchas. They 
are chosen because each one has been extensively documented in the physics literature, each one 
is recognized as a substantive puzzle within the field, and each one exhibits the framework-
preservation dynamic at a scale that makes the dynamic visible.
IV. The Pattern in Practice
The argument of the preceding section is structural: physics operates as a two-layer system in 
which the substantive theory layer is subject to coherence with evidence and the mathematical 
apparatus layer is subject only to internal consistency. When the two layers diverge, the 
discipline’s structural response is to revise the substantive theory while preserving the apparatus. 
The argument has been developed at the conceptual level. The cases that follow demonstrate the 
structure operating in identifiable contemporary physics. Each case exhibits the same pattern: 
empirical divergence from framework predictions, followed by accommodation through 
substantive-theory elaboration rather than through framework revision, followed by accumulation

===== PAGE 14 =====
of elaborations to a degree that, on a coherence-prioritizing standard, would justify questioning the 
framework itself.
The cases are presented in order of increasing contemporaneity, beginning with a historical case 
whose resolution is now uncontroversial and proceeding to contemporary cases whose status 
remains contested. The order is deliberate. The historical case establishes the pattern’s shape in a 
setting where hindsight has confirmed which response was correct; the contemporary cases then 
become recognizable instances of the same shape playing out before the resolution has occurred.
A. The Luminiferous Aether
The luminiferous aether was, for most of the nineteenth century, the medium through which light 
was understood to propagate. The mathematical apparatus of nineteenth-century optics, modeled 
on the mechanics of waves in elastic media, required a substrate. Sound waves propagate through 
air; water waves propagate through water; if light is a wave, the reasoning ran, light must propagate 
through something. The aether was the entity the mathematical apparatus required, and its 
properties were specified by what the apparatus needed it to be: rigid enough to support the high 
frequencies of visible light, yet diffuse enough to permit the unimpeded motion of planets through 
it. The properties were peculiar but unavoidable, given the framework’s commitments about how 
wave propagation must work.15
When Michelson and Morley failed to detect Earth’s motion through the aether in their famous 
interferometer experiments of the 1880s, the standard response was not to question the 
framework’s commitment to a propagating medium. The standard response was to elaborate the 
framework: to postulate that the aether dragged along with Earth, then that the dragging was 
partial, then that bodies moving through the aether were physically contracted in the direction of 
motion in just the way required to mask the aether’s effect. Lorentz developed the contraction 
hypothesis as a substantial mathematical apparatus, deriving the transformation equations that bear 
his name and showing that the apparatus could accommodate Michelson and Morley’s null result

while preserving the aether and the framework that required it.16 FitzGerald independently arrived

Einstein’s 1905 paper on the electrodynamics of moving bodies did not introduce new empirical 
evidence against the aether. The Michelson-Morley result was already known. What Einstein 
introduced was a framework revision: the principle of relativity and the constancy of the speed of 
light, taken as primitive postulates, with the Lorentz transformations rederived as consequences 
rather than as ad hoc accommodations. 17 On Einstein’s framework, there was no aether to detect 
because the framework no longer required one. The empirical predictions of Einstein’s framework 
matched Lorentz’s framework in the cases Lorentz had constructed his accommodations to handle, 
and exceeded Lorentz’s framework in cases the accommodations had not anticipated.
The lesson of this case, for the structural argument of this essay, lies not in Einstein’s specific 
framework but in the structural situation that preceded its adoption. For roughly two decades after 
Michelson and Morley, the empirical record was already in conflict with the framework’s 
commitments. The discipline’s response was to elaborate. The elaboration was ingenious, 
mathematically rigorous, and internally consistent. It was also, in retrospect, a sustained exercise

B. Dark Matter and Dark Energy
The gravitational dynamics of galaxies, galaxy clusters, and the large-scale structure of the cosmos 
do not match what general relativity predicts on the basis of visible matter alone. Galactic rotation 
curves remain flat at large radii rather than declining as Keplerian dynamics would require. Galaxy 
clusters exhibit gravitational binding stronger than their visible mass should produce. Observations 
of the cosmic microwave background and of large-scale structure formation require approximately 
five times more matter than is visible, plus a separate component of “dark energy” driving 
accelerating cosmic expansion. The discrepancies between framework predictions and empirical 
observation are not subtle; they are factor-of-five disagreements about the mass content of the 
universe and factor-of-many-orders-of-magnitude disagreements about the energy density of the 
vacuum.18
This case differs from the aether case in an important respect that deserves explicit 
acknowledgment. The evidence for a gravitational anomaly is not confined to a single 
experimental result; it converges from multiple independent lines of investigation. Galaxy rotation 
curves, gravitational lensing including the colliding-cluster cases such as the Bullet Cluster, the 
acoustic peak structure of the cosmic microwave background, and the statistics of large-scale 
structure formation all require, within the standard framework, an additional gravitating 
component. The aether had essentially zero independent evidence beyond its role in the 
mathematical apparatus of wave propagation; the gravitational anomaly does not have this 
property. Multiple convergent probes point to the existence of an empirical phenomenon that

The careful version of the argument distinguishes three levels of claim. At the phenomenon level,

particles with definite mass, interaction cross-section, and abundance—is a further commitment

parameterized model is empirically successful within the standard framework; the ontic 
interpretation of the model’s additional component as a particle species is the layer at which the 
framework-dependence becomes visible.
The discipline’s response to the phenomenon has been to postulate dark matter as an entity at the 
ontic level. Dark matter is, by stipulation, matter that does not interact electromagnetically—it 
neither emits nor absorbs light, neither reflects nor scatters it, neither couples to the photon field 
nor exhibits any of the interactions through which ordinary matter has historically been detected. 
It interacts gravitationally, because the gravitational effects are what we are trying to explain. 
Beyond gravitational interaction, its properties are largely specified by what the framework 
requires: it must be cold (slow-moving relative to light at the relevant epoch), it must not interact 
strongly enough to dissipate energy and form structures the way ordinary matter does, and it must 
exist in approximately a five-to-one ratio with ordinary matter. 19 Dark energy is even more 
sparingly characterized: it has whatever equation of state is needed to produce the observed cosmic 
acceleration, with the simplest version being a cosmological constant whose value is specified by 
observation rather than derived from theory.

particulate ontology has not been revised by the accumulated non-detections.20

framework’s commitment to general relativity in its current mathematical form is protected from 
revision; the response to anomaly is to elaborate substantive theory (postulating new entities) 
rather than to revise the framework (modifying the gravitational dynamics themselves), and such 
modified-gravity programs as exist are treated as fringe pursuits despite their parametric economy.

C. The Cosmological Constant Problem
The vacuum energy density predicted by quantum field theory, on the basis of zero-point energies 
of the quantum fields that the framework requires, exceeds the observed cosmological constant by 
approximately 120 orders of magnitude. The discrepancy is, by a substantial margin, the largest 
known disagreement between theoretical prediction and empirical measurement in any physical 
science. The standard characterization in the physics literature is that this is “the worst prediction 
in theoretical physics.”22
The structural significance of how this characterization functions is worth examining. A 120-order-
of-magnitude disagreement between framework prediction and empirical observation, if it 
occurred in any substantive physical theory not protected by framework status, would be treated 
as definitive falsification. No theory whose predictions diverged from observation by 120 orders 
of magnitude would survive serious scientific consideration. The cosmological constant 
discrepancy survives consideration not because its magnitude has been disputed—it has not—but 
because the framework that generates the disagreeable prediction is treated as a starting point that

characterization of the problem as the worst prediction in theoretical physics is widely attributed to Weinberg’s 
review and has become standard.

must be preserved, with the discrepancy reclassified as a “problem” the framework will eventually 
solve rather than as evidence the framework’s assumptions about vacuum structure require 
revision.
The proposed solutions to the cosmological constant problem are instructive. Anthropic reasoning 
holds that we observe a small cosmological constant because only in universes with small 
cosmological constants could observers exist to do the observing; the framework’s prediction is 
rescued by appeal to a multiverse in which the prediction is correct for most regions but observers 
are confined to a region where the prediction happens to fail. 23 Fine-tuning proposals seek 
mechanisms by which the framework’s prediction is mostly correct but a precise cancellation 
reduces the net result by 120 orders of magnitude. Supersymmetric proposals invoke partner 
particles whose contributions cancel against the predicted contributions in just the right way. Each 
proposed solution preserves the framework’s commitment to quantum field theory as currently 
formulated and locates the failure of prediction in some additional structure that has not yet been 
identified.
What none of the leading proposals do is reconsider whether the framework’s calculation of 
vacuum energy might rest on assumptions that the empirical record is telling us are wrong. The 
calculation depends on treating the vacuum as the ground state of the quantum fields, with all 
modes of all fields contributing their zero-point energies, summed across the relevant momentum 
range. Each step in this calculation is a framework commitment: that the relevant entities are 
quantum fields, that they have ground states, that ground-state energies sum in the manner the 
calculation assumes, that the momentum range is what the calculation assumes. The 120-order-of-
magnitude discrepancy with observation is, on a coherence-prioritizing reading, evidence that one 
or more of these framework commitments may require revision. The discipline does not read it 
this way. It reads the discrepancy as a problem within the framework, requiring solution from 
within the framework’s resources.

For approximately seventy-five years, physics has lacked a consistent theoretical framework that

structures: general relativity treats spacetime as a dynamical entity whose geometry is determined 
by the matter content of the universe, while quantum field theory treats spacetime as a fixed 
background on which the matter content lives. Combining them in any naive way produces 
mathematical inconsistencies; producing a consistent combination has been the goal of the 
quantum gravity research program since the late 1940s.24
The result of seventy-five years of effort has been the proliferation of competing programs. String 
theory, in its various formulations, treats elementary entities as one-dimensional extended objects 
in higher-dimensional spaces. Loop quantum gravity treats spacetime itself as quantized at the 
Planck scale, with discrete structure beneath the apparent continuum. Causal set theory treats 
spacetime as a discrete partial order of events. Asymptotic safety holds that gravity becomes a 
well-defined quantum field theory at sufficiently high energies. Emergent gravity programs 
propose that gravity is not fundamental but arises from underlying microphysical structure. Each 
program has produced extensive mathematical apparatus, internally consistent, and each has 
produced predictions that are too distant from current experimental access to permit empirical 
adjudication between them.25 To be sure, some programs offer more concrete phenomenological 
signatures than others, but none has reached the level of empirical adjudication that would force a 
choice among them.

internally consistent formal continuations without resolution; a coherence-prioritizing discipline

diverged from any given program’s predictions. The fact that quantum gravity has sustained five 
or more major competing programs across seven decades without empirical adjudication is a sign 
of the consistency-prioritizing structure operating in full visibility.
E. The Symmetrization Postulate
A fifth case, briefer than the others, illustrates the pattern in a setting more elementary than 
cosmology or quantum gravity. The framework of standard quantum mechanics treats particles as 
labeled entities that can in principle be distinguished from one another, even when they share all 
of their measurable properties. The empirical record does not cooperate. Identical particles—two 
electrons in an atom, two photons in a cavity, two helium-4 nuclei in a condensate—cannot be 
distinguished by any experimental procedure. The framework’s commitment to labeled entities 
and the empirical record’s refusal to honor the labels are in immediate conflict.

The framework’s response is the symmetrization postulate. The postulate stipulates that, for 
systems of identical particles, the wave function must be either symmetric or antisymmetric under 
exchange of any two particles’ labels. The postulate is added to the framework’s standard 
machinery as an additional postulate, beyond the foundational axioms of quantum mechanics, and 
is justified by its empirical success: with the postulate, quantum mechanics produces predictions 
that match what experiment finds with identical particles.27
The structural reading of this situation is that the framework’s commitment to labeled entities has 
been preserved at the cost of an ad hoc postulate whose function is to neutralize the empirical 
content of the labels. The postulate does not eliminate the labels from the formalism; the labels 
remain, and operations on the wave function are still performed with reference to them. What the 
postulate does is require that all physical predictions be symmetric under permutation of the labels, 
with the consequence that the labels can never appear in any physical prediction. The empirical 
content of the framework—what it predicts experiment will find—is precisely the content a 
framework without labels would predict. The framework, however, retains the labels because 
removing them would require revising the framework at its foundations rather than at its postulate-
list.
The symmetrization postulate is, on a coherence-prioritizing reading, evidence that the 
framework’s commitment to labeled entities is the wrong commitment to make. A framework that 
started from indistinguishability rather than distinguishability—that treated the absence of 
distinguishing features as primary rather than derivative—would not require the postulate. The 
framework would predict identical-particle behavior as a feature, not need to legislate it as a 
postulate.28 The standard framework does not take this route, because doing so would require 
framework revision rather than postulate addition.

F. The Pattern
The five cases differ in their specifics. The aether is a historical case whose resolution is now 
uncontroversial. Dark matter and dark energy are contemporary cases at the limits of empirical 
investigation, with the framework-preservation dynamic visible at the ontic-interpretation layer of 
an empirically robust phenomenon. The cosmological constant problem is the case where the 
framework’s quantitative failure is most extreme. The quantum gravity proliferation is the case 
where the absence of empirical adjudication is most prolonged. The symmetrization postulate is 
the case where the framework’s structural commitment is most directly in tension with the 
empirical record on elementary grounds.
What the cases share is the structural pattern the essay has named. In each case, the framework’s 
commitments and the empirical record have diverged. In each case, the discipline’s response has 
been to elaborate substantive theory while preserving the framework. In each case, the elaboration 
has accumulated to a degree that, on a coherence-prioritizing standard, would justify questioning 
the framework itself. In each case, the questioning has not occurred at the level the structural 
situation suggests it should—or, as in the aether case, has occurred only after an extended delay 
and through intervention from a direction the discipline did not anticipate.
The next section draws the structural conclusion these cases make available. The conclusion is not 
that the framework is definitively wrong, that dark matter does not exist as a phenomenon, that the 
symmetrization postulate is unwarranted, or that any specific elaboration is illegitimate. The 
conclusion is structural: the pattern these cases share is a pattern, and the pattern is what the 
asymmetry between consistency and coherence makes possible. The pattern can continue 
indefinitely without internal pressure for revision. Whether physics, as a discipline, will eventually 
achieve the framework-revision response that the structural situation suggests it should is a 
question the discipline’s two-layer structure does not provide the resources to answer.

Across each of the cases examined, the framework’s commitments and the empirical record have 
diverged. In each case, the discipline’s response to the divergence has been to elaborate the 
substantive theory built on the framework, while preserving the framework’s foundational 
commitments. In the aether case, the elaboration took the form of dragging coefficients and length 
contractions; in the dark matter case, the elaboration takes the form of an ontic commitment to a 
particulate interpretation of a gravitational anomaly, sustained across four decades of failed direct 
detection; in the cosmological constant case, the elaboration takes the form of anthropic reasoning, 
multiverse postulation, and fine-tuning mechanisms; in the quantum gravity case, the elaboration 
takes the form of multiple competing internally consistent formulations whose empirical 
distinguishability lies beyond current experimental access; in the symmetrization postulate case, 
the elaboration takes the form of an ad hoc postulate whose function is to neutralize the empirical 
content of a framework commitment the empirical record does not honor. The specifics differ. The 
structural pattern is identical.
What the pattern indicates is not that physics is producing bad theories or that physicists are 
reasoning poorly. The substantive theories built on the framework remain, in many cases, 
extraordinarily successful at the levels the framework operates well. General relativity continues 
to predict gravitational waves and gravitational lensing and time dilation with extraordinary 
precision in the regimes where the framework’s commitments and the empirical record do not 
diverge. Quantum field theory continues to produce predictions in particle physics that match 
experimental observation to extraordinary precision in the regimes where the framework operates 
as designed. The framework has been astonishingly productive.
The asymmetry the essay names is, accordingly, more refined than a flat insulation of the 
framework from coherence with evidence. The framework is subject to coherence in the regimes 
where its substantive predictions are tested and confirmed; in such regimes, the framework’s 
commitments receive empirical vindication along with the theories built on them. The asymmetry 
is rather that successes accrue to the framework as a whole while failures are absorbed by the 
substantive theory layer alone. This asymmetry is not unreasonable as applied to any single

===== PAGE 26 =====
empirical divergence—a single failed prediction underdetermines whether the framework or the 
substantive theory built on it requires revision, and routing the revision to the substantive theory 
layer is the structurally conservative response when the source of the failure is unclear. The 
asymmetry becomes structurally consequential, however, when divergences accumulate across 
multiple independent cases, as the essay has documented across the five cases of section IV. The 
same two-layer system that produces both the successes and the failures is asked to take credit for 
one and assign blame for the other. This is a credit-assignment asymmetry rather than a pure 
insulation from test. The framework is shielded not by being beyond empirical evaluation but by 
being structurally positioned to receive credit while declining blame. Where the framework’s 
commitments and the empirical record agree, the framework is treated as having predicted the 
agreement; where the commitments and the record disagree, the framework is treated as having 
provided the medium within which the discrepancy can eventually be resolved through 
elaborations of the substantive theory layer. Vindication is at the framework level; failure is at the 
substantive level. The asymmetry is the credit-assignment rule, and the rule is what makes 
sustained empirical pressure on the framework difficult to apply across accumulated cases even 
when it would be reasonable to apply on any single one.
The exemption has costs. The first cost is the accumulation of elaborations. Each individual 
elaboration is locally reasonable: postulating dragging coefficients to accommodate Michelson and 
Morley was a reasonable response, given the framework’s prior commitments; postulating a 
particulate ontology to accommodate galactic rotation curves is a reasonable response, given the 
framework’s prior commitments; postulating anthropic selection to accommodate the 
cosmological constant is a reasonable response, given the framework’s prior commitments. The 
reasonableness of each elaboration, taken individually, is what makes the cumulative pattern hard 
to recognize from within the discipline. The pattern is visible only when the elaborations are taken 
together, and even then it is visible only against the backdrop of the structural argument the essay 
has been developing. From inside the discipline, the elaborations are the work of physics. From 
outside, the elaborations form a series whose direction is identifiable.
The second cost is the foreclosure of alternatives. A framework whose elaborations are protected 
from coherence testing cannot be displaced by a framework with superior coherence properties, 
because the displacement would require comparing the two frameworks against the empirical

===== PAGE 27 =====
record, and the comparison is the comparison the protected framework’s structural status 
forecloses. Alternative frameworks can be proposed; they have been proposed, in various forms, 
throughout the history of physics. What they cannot do, against a framework protected from 
coherence testing, is win a coherence-based contest. The contest cannot occur on those grounds 
because the protected framework’s grounds for retention are not coherence-grounds. The retention 
is grounded in consistency, in productivity in non-divergent regimes, in mathematical elegance, 
and in institutional continuity. Alternative frameworks must therefore win on the protected 
framework’s grounds, which they cannot, because the protected framework was constructed to win 
on those grounds and the alternatives, being alternatives, were not.
The third cost is the gradual disconnection of the framework from the empirical accountability the 
discipline claims for itself. A framework that has accumulated decades of unfalsifiable 
elaborations is not, in any operational sense, accountable to evidence at the framework’s own level. 
It is accountable to evidence at the level of its substantive predictions in non-divergent regimes; it 
is not accountable to evidence at the level of its foundational commitments. The discipline’s self-
presentation as empirically accountable continues to be supported by the substantive-predictions 
level. The structural reality at the foundational level differs from the self-presentation. The 
discipline operates, increasingly, as two enterprises: an empirically accountable enterprise at the 
level of substantive prediction within established regimes, and a consistency-prioritizing enterprise 
at the level of foundational commitment. The two enterprises share personnel, share institutional 
infrastructure, share a self-understanding as a single discipline. Structurally, they are not a single 
discipline. They are two enterprises operating under one name.
What the essay’s argument does not claim is that the structural situation can be resolved by an act 
of will. Physicists are not deluded about what they are doing; they are operating in a structural 
situation whose features the essay has tried to describe. The structural situation does not provide 
an obvious procedure for its own correction, because the procedure that would correct it—
questioning the framework when its empirical divergences accumulate—is precisely the procedure 
the structural situation does not have the resources to perform on itself. The framework’s 
exemption from coherence testing is not a policy that can be repealed by a journal editor or a 
funding agency or a famous physicist. It is a structural fact about how the two-layer system

===== PAGE 28 =====
operates. Resolving it would require a change in the operating structure, not a change in the 
dispositions of the people operating within the structure.
What the essay’s argument does claim is that the structural situation deserves more philosophical 
attention than it has received. The philosophy of physics has examined many aspects of the 
framework’s commitments—what kinds of entities the framework permits, what kinds of 
properties they can have, what kinds of relations they can stand in, what the framework’s 
interpretations imply about the nature of physical reality. What the philosophy of physics has 
examined less thoroughly is the structural question of why the framework’s commitments are not 
themselves subject to the empirical accountability the discipline claims for its substantive 
predictions. The question is not a question internal to physics; physics does not have a procedure 
for asking it. The question is a question for the philosophy of physics, because the philosophy of 
physics is the discipline whose subject matter is what physics does and how it does it. The 
asymmetry between substantive predictions and foundational commitments is part of what physics 
does, and how it does it, and the philosophy of physics is positioned to examine the asymmetry in 
a way physics is not.
The next section addresses what the examination might productively look like. The argument is 
not that alternative frameworks must be adopted, that any specific alternative is superior, or that 
the existing framework must be discarded. The argument is more modest: that the philosophy of 
physics must take seriously the question of what alternative mathematical frameworks would look 
like, because doing so is the only structural intervention that could correct the asymmetry the essay 
has identified. The intervention is not an intervention against physics; it is an intervention in the 
conditions under which physics can be empirically accountable at its foundations rather than only 
at its surface.
VI. The Necessity of Alternative-Framework Inquiry
The structural conclusion of the previous section establishes the diagnostic position of the essay. 
The present author’s recognition of this asymmetry arose during independent work developing an 
alternative axiomatic framework, cited later in this section; the asymmetry itself, however, stands 
independent of any specific alternative. The discipline of physics, in its current operating structure, 
has limited capacity to perform framework revision in response to the accumulation of empirical

===== PAGE 29 =====
divergences. The capacity is limited not because individual physicists lack intellectual seriousness 
but because the framework’s epistemic status as the medium within which substantive empirical 
claims become formulable places the framework outside the coherence standard the discipline 
applies to its substantive predictions. This is the structural situation the essay has named. What 
remains is to address what the philosophy of physics, as a discipline whose subject matter is what 
physics does and how it does it, might productively do in response.
The essay’s recommendation is that the philosophy of physics must take seriously the question of 
what alternative mathematical frameworks would look like, what their foundational commitments 
would be, and what physical theories formulated within them would predict. This recommendation 
is intentionally limited in scope. It does not propose that any specific alternative framework should 
be adopted by the discipline. It does not propose that the existing framework should be abandoned. 
It does not propose that physics is failing in any way correctable by an act of institutional will. The 
recommendation is that the alternative-framework question, currently treated as marginal or fringe 
by the mainstream of the discipline, must be elevated to a position commensurate with its structural 
importance.
The case for this elevation is not speculative. Work in the relational tradition has been proceeding 
for several decades, by working physicists from positions of disciplinary authority, with results 
that the broader discipline has not yet integrated into its self-understanding. The clearest 
contemporary statement of this work appears in Vidotto’s recent essay on the relational ontology 
of contemporary physics. 29 Vidotto, working in quantum gravity at Western Ontario in 
collaboration with Rovelli, observes that relationality is not a marginal interpretation favored by a 
small subset of philosophers; it is the deep structure operative across the entirety of contemporary 
fundamental physics. Classical mechanics, when examined from the perspective of modern 
symmetry theory, treats physical properties as relations between systems rather than as intrinsic 
features of objects. Gauge theories treat gauge symmetries not as redundancies to be eliminated 
but as handles through which systems couple to one another, with the couplings themselves being

What Vidotto’s analysis does, with particular precision, is identify the strength of the relational

perspectivalism in any sense that working physicists would find objectionable. The relations are 
among physical systems in interaction, not between observers and an unknowable substrate; 
conscious agents are a peculiar case among the different systems, not the central category.

defensive objection a skeptical reader could raise. An alternative-framework conversation does not 
require abandoning realism. It requires reconceiving what the locus of the real is. The reconception 
is well within the philosophical resources the discipline already has.

Vidotto’s diagnostic work does not, however, complete the alternative-framework conversation. 
Her contribution is to identify that relationality is the deep structure operative across contemporary 
physics. What she does not provide—and what her work does not attempt to provide—is the 
foundational axiomatic framework in which this relationality would be made primitive rather than 
derived as a feature observed across existing sub-disciplines. The relational ontology Vidotto 
documents is, on her presentation, already there in physics, awaiting recognition. The further step 
of constructing a foundational framework in which interaction is taken as the sole primitive, with 
all other physical categories derived from it, is work that her analysis identifies as needed but does 
not itself undertake. Several proposals exist for what such a foundational framework might look 
like.

The call to alternative-framework inquiry raises an objection that the essay must address directly. 
If the existing mathematical apparatus is exempted from empirical accountability in the manner 
the essay has documented, by what criterion is an alternative framework to be evaluated? The 
existing framework was constructed to fit the empirical record; any alternative that achieves 
coherence with the existing record will be evaluable against the same standard the existing 
framework already meets, and the existing framework will, in many cases, win such comparisons 
because it was constructed to win them. Other criteria—elegance, parsimony, conceptual clarity—
are available but are not themselves empirical criteria; appealing to them seems to abandon the 
empirical-accountability standard that motivated the call to alternatives. The objection is that the 
call to alternatives is bootstrapping: it depends on a criterion of evaluation that either reduces to 
the existing framework’s self-vindication or escapes empirical accountability altogether.
The objection is real and deserves a direct answer. The criterion by which alternative frameworks 
should be evaluated is the same criterion the existing framework is supposed to meet, applied at 
framework level rather than at substantive-theory level: the Lakatosian progressiveness criterion. 
An alternative framework has legitimate claim on the discipline’s attention if it can demonstrate 
three properties. First, empirical equivalence in established regimes—the alternative reproduces 
the existing framework’s successes wherever the existing framework operates well. Second, 
superior handling of divergent cases—the alternative addresses the cases where the existing 
framework requires elaborate accommodation, doing so with greater parametric economy or 
structural simplicity. Third, novel predictions—the alternative generates empirically 
distinguishable predictions in regimes where no current framework has been tested. These three 
criteria are not circular because they are not asking the alternative to justify itself by the existing 
framework’s standards. They are asking the alternative to meet the same standard the existing

===== PAGE 34 =====
framework was supposed to meet, applied at framework level rather than only at substantive-
theory level. The difference is that the standard is now being applied to the framework itself rather 
than only to the theories built within it.
The progressiveness criterion is necessary but not sufficient for the diagnostic the essay has 
developed. What the diagnostic specifically requires is alternative frameworks whose practitioners 
commit explicitly to the framework’s ansatz status—to its standing as a working hypothesis subject 
to revision in light of evidence, not as an established starting point exempt from further empirical 
challenge. This commitment is structural rather than mathematical; the same mathematical 
apparatus, held differently, becomes structurally accountable in ways it currently is not. An 
alternative framework that is empirically progressive in the Lakatosian sense but is held by its 
practitioners as a new fixed substrate would, over time, reproduce the structural situation the essay 
has documented in the current framework. The diagnostic is not solved by replacing one 
unrevisable substrate with another; it is solved by adopting a framework whose unrevisability is 
itself revised—by communities of practitioners who explicitly treat the framework as an ansatz 
that the empirical record may eventually require them to abandon. Ansatz status is not a confession 
of weakness. It is the acknowledgment of empirical accountability that the essay’s diagnostic 
identifies as currently absent at the framework level. Without it, even a progressive alternative 
becomes the next unfalsifiable substrate. With it, the framework is structurally accountable in the 
dimension the diagnostic identifies as currently exempted.
The criteria as stated leave open a question that should be addressed explicitly: multiple alternative 
frameworks may satisfy both progressiveness and ansatz commitment without uniquely 
identifying which alternative the discipline should adopt. This is a feature rather than a defect, and 
it lies along a spectrum of difference worth naming. At one end of the spectrum, alternative 
frameworks may differ only at the level of notation and convention while sharing identical 
ontological content—as Newton’s fluxional notation and Leibniz’s differential notation differed 
in convention while expressing the same calculus. At the other end, alternative frameworks may 
differ at the level of foundational formal apparatus while ultimately proving empirically 
equivalent—as Heisenberg’s matrix mechanics and Schrödinger’s wave mechanics differed in 
their mathematical structures and intuitive pictures while being shown by Dirac and von Neumann 
to be equivalent formulations of the same theory. Between these endpoints, alternatives can differ

===== PAGE 35 =====
at varying depths of structural commitment, with the empirical equivalence being more or less 
complete. The structural-preservation diagnostic the essay has developed applies most pressingly 
to frameworks at the Heisenberg-Schrödinger end of this spectrum and beyond, where the formal 
apparatus carries different ontological commitments. Frameworks at the Newton-Leibniz end do 
not really differ in their commitments; they differ in their expression of identical commitments. 
The discipline’s eventual adoption of Leibniz’s notation over Newton’s was driven by 
considerations of calculational convenience and pedagogical clarity, not by empirical 
superiority—and this is a legitimate basis for framework choice when the frameworks are 
empirically equivalent. Multiple correct alternatives may coexist; the discipline’s task is not to 
identify the uniquely correct one but to develop alternatives to the point where progressiveness 
comparison becomes possible, with the choice among empirically equivalent and structurally 
adequate alternatives being a secondary question to be settled on grounds the philosophy of physics 
has historically recognized as legitimate.
The closing observation is that the discipline’s empirical accountability at the foundational level 
depends on the inquiry being undertaken. A framework that is not subject to coherence with 
evidence at its foundational level can only be rendered accountable by the construction and serious 
evaluation of alternatives. Without alternatives, the framework’s relationship to evidence is 
asymmetric in the way the essay has documented; with alternatives developed to a comparable 
level of formal sophistication and empirical applicability, the framework can be evaluated against 
them on coherence grounds, and the structural exemption identified in the previous sections can 
be addressed. The alternative-framework inquiry is therefore not a competitor to the existing 
framework; it is the procedure through which the existing framework can become accountable in 
the dimension where it is currently not. This is the strongest reason the inquiry must proceed. It is 
also the reason the philosophy of physics, as the discipline positioned to take the inquiry seriously, 
has a structural responsibility to do so. The inquiry will not be performed by physics, because 
physics does not have the procedure to perform it on itself. It will be performed by the philosophy 
of physics, if it is performed at all, and the responsibility of the discipline is to recognize that this 
is its work.
The essay closes here. The argument has been that physics, despite its self-presentation as the 
paradigmatic empirical science, operates as a two-layer system in which the substantive theory

layer is subject to coherence with evidence while the foundational mathematical apparatus layer is 
subject only to internal consistency. The asymmetry has consequences that the essay has 
documented across five cases. The cases collectively demonstrate that the framework-preservation 
dynamic operates as a structural feature of contemporary physics, not as a series of unrelated 
puzzles. The structural feature has costs the discipline has not adequately reckoned with, including 
the accumulation of unfalsifiable elaborations, the foreclosure of alternative-framework inquiry 
on coherence grounds, and the gradual disconnection of the framework from the empirical 
accountability the discipline claims for itself at the foundational level. The response the essay 
recommends is that the philosophy of physics take seriously the existing alternative-framework 
research, of which the relational tradition documented by Vidotto and the foundational proposals 
in constructor theory, ontic structural realism, and the Interactionist Mathematical Paradigm are 
present examples—and that such alternatives be evaluated by the criteria the essay has specified: 
Lakatosian progressiveness applied at framework level, combined with explicit ansatz 
commitment. The work is being done. The discipline’s task is to recognize that this work is what 
addresses what the essay has named: the unfalsifiable substrate of empirical physics.
