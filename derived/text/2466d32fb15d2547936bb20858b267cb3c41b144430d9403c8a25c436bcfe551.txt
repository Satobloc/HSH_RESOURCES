Abstract 
Different areas of physics often employ very different microscopic concepts, variables, 
equations, and experimental interpretations while nevertheless exhibiting similar forms of 
mathematical organization. Green-function methods, quantum scattering, relativistic bound-
state equations, multiple-wave scattering, optical interference, resonant tunneling, gauge -
dependent transport, and quantum networks all contain examples in which propagation 
between states is modified by local interactions, closed paths retain phase information, and 
repeated processes generate large or infinite families of histories. This work proposes PIHR as 
a structural meta-theory for describing such common organization. PIHR separates four roles: 
propagation, interaction, holonomy or closed-path monodromy, and recursive closure. The 
framework does not replace the underlying physical theories and does not identify systems 
with different microscopic meanings. Instead, it introduces an abstraction layer in which 
domain-specific quantities are mapped to common structural roles and compared at that level. 
A first-return factorization theorem is established for linear recursive systems, showing when 
arbitrarily many repeated histories can be represented exactly by a finite internal resolvent. 
Dyson resummation, Lippmann–Schwinger scattering, Bethe–Salpeter resummation, Foldy–
Lax multiple scattering, Fabry –Pérot interference, quantum double -barrier tunneling, 
Aharonov–Bohm transport, and quantum graphs are then interpreted as distinct object-level 
realizations of a common meta-level organization. A three-arm multi-cycle quantum network 
provides a nontrivial quantitative test: the number of explicit histories grows exponentially 
with recursive depth while the exact representation remains three -dimensional. Classical 
controllability and observability criteria further determine when this finite representation is 
minimal. The resulting distinction between microscopic history complexity and structural 
realization complexity suggests that common architectures can exist across physical domains 
without implying equivalence of the underlying physics. PIHR is therefore proposed not as a 
new fundamental interaction or microscopic law, but as a testable meta-theoretical language 
for recurrent mathematical organization across physical theories and formalisms. 
Keywords: structural meta-theory; theoretical physics; Green functions; recursive systems; 
multiple scattering; quantum networks; holonomy; resummation; quantum graphs; minimal 
realization; PIHR 
1. Introduction 
Physics is divided into theories and formalisms that are distinguished by their physical 
objects, scales, dynamical laws, and experimental meanings. Quantum field theory,

===== PAGE 2 =====
nonrelativistic scattering theory, wave optics, mesoscopic transport, and network quantum 
mechanics are not interchangeable descriptions of the same physical system. Nevertheless, 
their mathematical formulations repeatedly contain related organizational motifs. 
In quantum electrodynamics, Dyson showed how repeated irreducible contributions can be 
reorganized into dressed quantities [1]. Schwinger’s Green-function formulation provided an 
operator language for propagation and field interactions [2], while Feynman’s space -time 
formulation represented amplitudes through contributions associated with alternative histories 
[3]. These developments established several of the recurrent structures that remain central to 
modern theoretical physics. 
The same organizational pattern appears in scattering and many -body problems. The 
Lippmann–Schwinger construction generates repeated propagation between potential 
interactions [4]. Foldy formulated multiple scattering of waves by distributed scatterers [5], 
and Lax developed a systematic multiple-scattering theory [6] together with its effective-field 
extension for dense systems [7]. Watson obtained a corresponding multiply scattered structure 
in the many-body problem [8]. These theories differ in physical interpretation, but each 
contains a finite set of elementary processes capable of generating arbitrarily long sequences. 
Relativistic two-particle physics provides another example. The Bethe –Salpeter equation 
organizes repeated propagation between irreducible two -particle interaction kernels [9]. 
Repetition also appears outside Green-function and field-theoretical language. Fabry and 
Pérot’s interferometer produces its characteristic response through repeated internal 
reflections [10]. In semiconductor physics, finite-superlattice tunneling was analyzed by Tsu 
and Esaki [11], and resonant tunneling through semiconductor double barriers was 
demonstrated by Chang, Esaki, and Tsu [12]. 
Closed trajectories introduce a further structural element. Aharonov and Bohm demonstrated 
that closed-path phase information associated with the electromagnetic potential can influence 
interference [13]. Byers and Yang connected multiply connected geometries with flux -
dependent quantum structure [14], and Aharonov –Bohm oscillations were subsequently 
observed in normal-metal rings [15]. Multi-channel transport through small rings can be 
formulated through scattering matrices [16], while the Fisher–Lee relation connects Green 
functions with transmission amplitudes [17]. Landauer’s transport formulation provides 
another foundational connection between transmission and conductance [18]. 
Quantum graphs make this structural recurrence especially explicit. Kottos and Smilansky 
showed how quantum dynamics on graphs can be represented in terms of propagation and 
vertex scattering [19] and developed periodic -orbit descriptions of their spectra [20]. 
Kostrykin and Schrader established general quantum-wire scattering on finite graphs and 
composition of graph scattering matrices [21]. Barra and Gaspard studied transport and 
dynamics on open graphs [22], while Kottos and Smilansky obtained exact scattering 
expressions for open chaotic graphs [23]. 
Closed-path information itself has several meanings. Wu and Yang formulated gauge 
structure using nonintegrable phase factors associated with paths [24]. Berry showed that 
cyclic adiabatic evolution generates geometric phase [25], and Wilczek and Zee extended this 
concept to non-Abelian holonomy [26]. Thus a closed cycle may carry ordinary dynamical 
phase, Abelian gauge phase, geometric phase, or a noncommuting matrix-valued holonomy, 
depending on the underlying theory.

===== PAGE 3 =====
A second body of mathematics is relevant to the present argument. Kalman showed that linear 
dynamical systems possess canonical structural decompositions [27] and developed the 
relation between state-space and input-output descriptions [28]. Ho and Kalman subsequently 
provided an effective construction of finite minimal realizations from input-output data [29]. 
These results establish that the complexity of a sequence of observable outputs need not equal 
the dimensionality of the smallest internal system capable of generating that sequence. 
Recursive representations in physics similarly permit infinite processes to be encoded through 
finite internal structures; the recursion method of Haydock, Heine, and Kelly provides a 
classic condensed-matter example [30]. 
These observations motivate a question different from the usual question posed within any 
one of these theories: 
 
Can recurrent forms of mathematical organization across physically different theories 
be described by a common theory at a higher structural level? 
 
The proposal developed here is that they can, at least for a restricted but broad class of linear 
recursive problems.

25. Limits of the Meta-Theory 
The framework has clear limits. 
First, PIHR currently addresses linear recursive systems most naturally. 
Second, a finite first-return state space may not exist. 
Third, systems with genuine long-term memory may require an expanding state space. 
Fourth, nonlinear recursion is not covered by Theorem 1. 
Fifth, continuum quantum-field problems can involve unbounded operators, ultraviolet 
divergences, renormalization, and asymptotic rather than convergent perturbation series. 
Sixth, an operator product may not possess a unique 𝑃/𝐼/𝐻 decomposition. 
Seventh, different structural decompositions may be possible for the same object-level theory. 
Eighth, a finite representation may fail to provide any practical simplification.

===== PAGE 23 =====
These are not secondary issues. They determine whether PIHR is a useful meta-theory or only 
a new notation. 
26. Falsifiability Criteria 
The proposal can be tested. 
PIHR becomes scientifically useful if: 
1. the definitions of 𝑃, 𝐼, 𝐻, and 𝑅 remain stable across domains; 
2. the same factorization rules apply without problem-specific modification; 
3. structurally equivalent systems can be identified before solving them independently; 
4. nontrivial finite or minimal representations can be constructed systematically; 
5. meta-level quantities such as 𝑑Y45 reveal information not obvious in the object-level 
formulation; 
6. the framework produces analytic or computational simplifications; 
7. or new deductions become possible from cross-domain structural equivalence. 
The program fails in its stronger form if: 
1. every example requires a new definition of the sectors; 
2. PIHR merely renames standard matrix multiplication; 
3. no structural invariants emerge; 
4. no useful reduction occurs; 
5. or physically unrelated systems can be called equivalent only through arbitrary choices 
of representation. 
These criteria make the proposal empirically and mathematically vulnerable rather than purely 
interpretive.

29. Discussion 
The central proposal of this work is a change of level. 
Traditional theoretical physics asks how a specific physical system evolves. 
PIHR instead asks:

theories?

The framework does not add another Hamiltonian, field, particle, interaction, or fundamental 
constant. 
Instead, it maps domain-level mathematical objects into a common structural language.

30. Conclusions 
We have proposed PIHR as a structural meta -theory for recurrent organization across 
different areas of physics. 
Its four labels denote: 
𝑃=propagation, 
𝐼=interaction, 
𝐻=holonomy or closed-path monodromy, 
𝑅=recursive closure. 
The central conceptual result is that these four roles are not symmetric. 
The primitive physical structure lies in 
(𝑃,𝐼,𝐻), 
while recursion acts upon their resulting first-return kernel. 
Thus

A first-return resummation theorem was established for PIH-factorizable linear systems:

The same meta-level architecture was then identified in:

• Lippmann–Schwinger scattering; 
• Bethe–Salpeter resummation; 
• Foldy–Lax multiple scattering; 
• Fabry–Pérot interference; 
• quantum double-barrier tunneling; 
• Aharonov–Bohm transport; 
• and quantum graphs. 
These systems remain physically different. 
Their commonality lies in structural organization. 
A three-arm multi-cycle quantum network provided a nontrivial quantitative example.

perspective. 
PIHR does not presently constitute a fundamental unification of physics. 
It makes the more limited and testable proposal that different physical theories can share 
recurring mathematical architectures and that these architectures can themselves 
become objects of theoretical study. 
If this program can be extended to more complicated networks, non -Abelian holonomies, 
interacting many-body systems, and finite closures of coupled Green-function hierarchies—
and if genuinely new results follow from the cross -domain structural representation—then 
PIHR could develop from a classification scheme into a substantive theory of common 
organization across physical theories.
