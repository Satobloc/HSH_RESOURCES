Abstract We investigate strong gravitational lensing by
two static black hole models (Model-1 and Model-2) within
the Effective Quantum Gravity (EQG) framework, charac-
terized by mass M and parameter ζ .F o rζ = 0, they reduce
to the Schwarzschild solution, and depending on the param-
eters, they describe black holes with an event and Cauchy
horizon (Model-1), a single horizon (Model-2), or no hori-
zons. Using supermassive black holes (SMBHs), Sgr A* and
M87*, as lenses and integrating theoretical predictions with
recent Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) data, we identify sig-
niﬁcant differences in lensing signatures due to quantum cor-
rections. For Model-1, the deviations of the lensing observ-
ables |δθ∞| of black holes in EQG from Schwarzschild black
hole, for SMBHs Sgr A* and M87*, can reach as much as
1.75 μas and 1.32 μas, while |δs| is about 30.12 nas for Sgr
A* and 22.63 nas for M87*. The ﬂux ratio of the ﬁrst image
to all subsequent packed images indicates that EQG black
hole images are brighter than their Schwarzschild counter-
parts, with a deviation in the brightness ratio |δrmag | reach-
ing up to 2.02. The time delays between the second and ﬁrst
images, denoted |δT2,1|, exhibit substantial deviations from
the GR counterpart, reaching up to 1.53 min for Sgr A* and
1159.9 min for M87*. The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT)
constraint onθsh of Sgr A* and M87*, within the 1-σ region,
limits the parameter ζ . Our analysis concludes that EQG
black holes are consistent with the EHT observations within
a ﬁnite parameter space.

1 Introduction
Gravitational lensing, a notable prediction of Einstein’s Gen-
eral Relativity (GR), has evolved into one of the most effec-
tive tools for studying astrophysical phenomena and testing
gravitational theories. Since Einstein ﬁrst considered light
bending around massive objects [1], the ﬁeld has developed
signiﬁcantly, leading to groundbreaking discoveries in cos-
mology [2] and astrophysics [ 3]. The deﬂection of light by
a gravitational ﬁeld, known as gravitational lensing, plays
a critical role in disclosing the nature of spacetime and the
distribution of mass in the universe.
Black holes, among GR’s most fascinating predictions,
provide a natural arena for studying gravity in its strongest
form. Developed by Einstein in 1915, GR has successfully
described a wide range of astrophysical phenomena and
passed numerous observational tests, from the solar system to
cosmology [4]. Among these predictions, black hole regions
of spacetime with gravitational ﬁelds so strong that not even
light can escape have garnered considerable interest. Non-
rotating black holes are represented by the Schwarzschild
metric [ 5], while rotating black holes are modeled by the
Kerr solution [6].
Despite its success, Einstein admitted that GR is incom-
plete at quantum scales, where it forecasts singularities at
black hole centres and during the Big Bang. These issues and
GR’s inability to reconcile with quantum mechanics moti-
vate the search for a more comprehensive theory of gravity,
particularly in strong-ﬁeld regimes. To address this, several
modiﬁed theories of gravity (MTG), such as Loop Quantum
Gravity (LQG) and string theory, have been proposed [7–10].
These theories offer quantum corrections to classical black
hole solutions, particularly near the event horizon, result-

The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) has recently pro-
vided us with the ﬁrst-ever images of supermassive black
holes, M87* [17] and Sgr A* [18], demonstrating the poten-
tial of direct observations to test the geometry of black holes
and the validity of GR. These images reveal the shadows of
black holes-corresponding to photon orbits trapped under the
intense gravitational ﬁeld-and have shown consistency with
the Kerr black hole solution predicted by GR [19,20]. How-
ever, deviations from Kerr geometry, such as those indicated
by quantum gravity, could alter the black hole shadow’s size
and shape and inﬂuence the associated gravitational lensing
effects [21–29].
Strong gravitational lensing, where light passes near a
black hole, offers precise tests of spacetime geometry. The
study of strong-gravitational lensing around black holes, par-
ticularly in the presence of quantum corrections, provides a
unique window into the fundamental nature of gravity. In the
classical regime, the bending of light near the photon sphere
has been well studied using methods such as the strong deﬂec-
tion limit [ 30], which provides analytical solutions for the
deﬂection angle near black holes. Darwin [ 31] ﬁrst investi-
gated the bending of light near a Schwarzschild black hole,
establishing the foundational framework for studying black
hole lensing. Following developments by Virbhadra and Ellis
[32,33] expanded these results, specifying relativistic images
formed by light passing close to the black hole’s event hori-
zon. Frittelli et al. [34] advanced analytical methods for solv-
ing the lens equation, which Bozza later enhanced with the
strong deﬂection limit (SDL) framework [ 30,35,36]. This
technique has been applied to different spacetimes, such as
Reissner–Nordström [37] and rotating black holes [ 30,38].
It has facilitated a deeper understanding of gravitational
lensing near black holes. The SDL has also been instru-
mental in studying black holes in modiﬁed gravity mod-
els [36,39–46]. Recent research has focused on lensing in
higher-curvature gravity theories [47–50], as well as modiﬁ-
cations to Schwarzschild spacetimes [51–57], revealing new
phenomena and offering crucial insights into quantum grav-
ity.
In modiﬁed gravity scenarios, quantum corrections to
black hole spacetimes can affect lensing observables like
deﬂection angles, photon spheres, and relativistic images.
Recent studies in higher-curvature gravity, LQG, and string
theory [ 21,48,58–65] show that quantum effects alter the
geometry near the event horizon, inﬂuencing black hole lens-
ing properties. For instance, quantum corrections can shrink
the lensing ring and change the photon orbits, leading to devi-
ations in the black hole shadow that can be distinguished
from classical GR solutions [ 60,66–68]. Comparing lens-
ing predictions in quantum-corrected spacetimes with high-
resolution EHT observations provides a method to test devi-
ations from GR and constrain quantum gravity. [18,69].
The Effective Quantum Gravity (EQG) represents one
approach, incorporating quantum corrections into classical
black hole solutions. Zhang et al. [ 70], within the Hamil-
tonian constraint approach to Effective Quantum Gravity
(EQG), introduced two types of static black hole models
that preserve general covariance, demonstrating that quan-
tum corrections can signiﬁcantly alter black hole geometry,
particularly near the event horizon. Liu et al. [71] further stud-
ied the light rings and shadows of these static black holes in
EQG, demonstrating that while quantum corrections do not
directly affect light rings, they inﬂuence the size and charac-
teristics of black hole shadows. It emphasizes the potential
of quantum-corrected black holes to exhibit new observa-
tional signatures. The quasinormal modes and shadows of
EQG black holes are studied in [72]. The gravitational lens-
ing effects of EQG black holes in both the weak and strong
ﬁeld limits are investigated in [73]. They ﬁnd that the quan-
tum parameter is analogous to the electric charge in the weak
gravitational lensing. In contrast, in the strong ﬁeld limit, the
effect of the quantum parameter is opposite to that of charges
in some other gravity theories. In this work, we focus on
spherically symmetric black holes in EQG [ 70], investigat-
ing the impact of quantum corrections on lensing observ-
ables. We aim to explore how these corrections inﬂuence the
gravitational lensing signature and discuss the prospects of
testing these predictions with observations like those pro-
vided by the (EHT) observations. We analyze these effects
using supermassive black holes like Sgr A* and M87* as
lenses. While the EHT may detect strong deﬂection from
EQG black holes, distinguishing them from Schwarzschild
black holes is challenging due to O(μas) deviations.
This paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 presents the
framework of EQG, introducing two static spherically sym-
metric black holes and brieﬂy discussing the horizon struc-
ture. Section 3 outlines the formalism of strong gravita-
tional lensing in the context of EQG black holes, provid-
ing a detailed analysis of the quantum corrections on lensing
observables, including deﬂection angles, shadows, and the
time delay between the ﬁrst and second images on the same
side of the source. In Sect. 4, we numerically analyze the
strong lensing observables by taking the supermassive black
holes Sgr A* and M87* as the lens. In Sect. 5, we discuss
the observational prospects for detecting these quantum cor-
rections and how current and future experiments, such as the
EHT, can be used to constrain EQG models. Finally, Sect.6
summarizes our ﬁndings and proposes directions for future
research.

5 EHT constraints on the observables
In 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) Collaboration
achieved a groundbreaking achievement by capturing the
horizon-scale image of the supermassive black hole M87*
[17,69,81]. With a distance of d = 16.8 Mpc and an esti-
mated mass of M = (6.5 ± 0.7) × 109 M⊙ , the EHT Col-
laboration determined that M87* has a compact emission
region with an angular diameter of θd = 42 ± 3μas. The
central ﬂux depression, which indicates the shadow of the
black hole, is at least a factor of 10. In 2022, the EHT
observed the supermassive black hole Sgr A* at the centre
of the Milky Way, revealing an image with a diameter ring
θd = 48.7 ± 7μas. This observation suggests a black hole
mass of M = 4.0+1.1
−0.6×106 M⊙ and a Schwarzschild shadow
deviation of δ =− 0.08+0.09
−0.09 (VLTI),−0.04+0.09
−0.10 (Keck).
These ﬁndings offer a valuable new approach to probing
strong ﬁeld gravity [ 18,82,84]. The EHT also calculated
an angular diameter of the emission ring for Sgr A* of
θd = (51.8 ± 2.3)μas, based on the previously estimated
mass M and DLS = 8.15 ± 0.15 kpc [18].
The horizon-scale images of these supermassive black
holes provide a novel theoretical framework for testing grav-
ity theories [13,18,50,85–97]. By analyzing the EHT obser-
vations of the shadows of Sgr A* and M87*, we can constrain
the deviation parameter ζ for the EQG black hole spacetime
in Model-1. Using the apparent radius of the photon sphere
θ∞, as a proxy for the angular size of the black hole shadow,
we can constrain this deviation parameter within the 1-σ con-
ﬁdence level.
Constraints from M87*: We ﬁnd that the Schwarzschild
black hole (ζ = 0) casts the largest shadow, with an angu-
lar diameter of θsh = 2θ∞ = 39.56069μas. This value
falls within the 1- σ region for a black hole with mass
M = (6.5 ± 0.7) × 109 M⊙ and distance DOL = 16.8M p c
[17,69,81]. Figure9 shows the angular diameterθsh as a func-
tion of ζ , with the black solid line representingθsh = 39 μas
for the EQG black hole spacetime corresponding to M87*.
When the EQG black hole spacetime metric is analyzed using
the EHT results for M87* within the 1-σ bound, the parame-
ter ζ is constrained to 0 <ζ ≤ 0.9M. Thus, based on Fig.9,
the EQG black hole spacetime could be a candidate for astro-
physical black holes within only a tiny part of the parameter
space.
Constraints from Sgr A*: The EHT observation employed
three independent algorithms, EHT-Imaging, SMILI, and
DIFMAP to determine that the mean measured value of
the shadow angular diameter lies in the range θsh ∈
(46.9, 50)μas, with a 1-σ interval of (41.7, 55.6)μas [18].
The angular diameter θsh ∈ (41.7, 55.6)μas falls within
the 1-σ conﬁdence region of the EHT observations of the
Sgr A* black hole shadow, constraining the parameter ζ to
0.0 ≤ ζ ≤ 4M for the EQG black hole spacetime. Thus,
within the ﬁnite parameter space, the EQG black hole space-
time is consistent with the EHT results for the Sgr A* black
hole shadow (see Fig. 9).
6 Conclusion
Recent developments in the EQG framework have led to
signiﬁcant modiﬁcations of black hole spacetimes through
quantum corrections. Zhang et al. [ 70] derived stationary
effective metrics while preserving covariance in spherical
symmetry. By retaining the kinematic variables and classi-
cal vector constraints, they formulated an arbitrary effective
Hamiltonian constraint Heff and a free function addressing
the gauge associated with the diffeomorphism constraint.

This approach yielded two families of effective Hamilto-
nian constraints, each characterized by a quantum parameter.
When these parameters vanish, the constraints reduce to their
classical forms, resulting in two quantum-corrected metrics
with distinct spacetime structures. Building on this work, we
explored strong gravitational lensing by these black holes
(Model-1 and Model-2), which generalize the Schwarzschild
black hole by introducing the parameter ζ . In our study,
we analyzed both Model-1 and Model-2 together. Model-
2 behaves similarly to Schwarzschild black holes, limiting
its novelty compared to Model-1, where quantum correc-
tions result in distinct deviations. A comparative discussion,
supported by tables and ﬁgures, has been included to high-
light these ﬁndings. Our analysis shows that quantum cor-
rections signiﬁcantly inﬂuence the lensing observables for
Model-1, while Model-2 remains indistinguishable from the
Schwarzschild solution.
We examined the impact of ζ on lensing coefﬁcients
(¯a, ¯b) and the deﬂection angle. For Model-1, the coefﬁcients
decrease with increasing ζ , while Model-2 shows no depen-
dence onζ . For a ﬁxed impact parameter, the deﬂection angle
αD is smaller for EQG black holes than for GR ones. Using
supermassive black holes M87* and Sgr A* as lenses, we
found that quantum corrections lead to deviations in key lens-
ing features, such as angular positions, image brightness, and
time delays. The brightness of the ﬁrst image increases, while
the time delays between subsequent images also increase.
Our results indicate that the angular position θ∞ and the
image separation s decrease as ζ increases, whereas the
brightness ratio rmag increases. The Einstein ring for EQG
black holes is smaller than that for the GR black hole.
We found that the relativistic image angular position θ∞
varies between 18 .4617μas ≤ θ∞(M87*) ≤ 19.782 μas
and 24.5725μas ≤ θ∞(Sgr A*) ≤ 26.3299μas. The devi-
ation |δθ∞| can reach 1.75μas for Sgr A* and 1.32 μas for
M87*. The time delays between the second and ﬁrst images,
/Delta1T2,1, show deviations from the predictions of GR, reaching
up to 0.7674 min for Sgr A* and 1159.9 min for M87*. EHT
observations place constraints on the EQG parameterζ , with
bounds 0 ≤ ζ ≤ 0.9M for M87* and 0 ≤ ζ ≤ 4M for Sgr
A*, indicating that the constraint for M87* is tighter.
In conclusion, our analysis shows that gravitational lens-
ing is a promising tool for testing quantum corrections in
EQG theory. The constraints derived from the EHT data sug-
gest that EQG black holes are consistent with current obser-
vations within a ﬁnite range ofζ . With future high-precision
observations, particularly from the forthcoming ngEHT,
these constraints could be further reﬁned, potentially dis-

Appendix B: EHT-imaging (Event Horizon Telescope
imaging algorithm)
The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) employs three imaging
algorithms-EHT-Imaging, SMILI, and DIFMAP.
These independently reconstruct black hole images using
the same dataset to minimize collective bias and enable com-
parative analysis. Each algorithm explores 103−105 param-
eter combinations, including weights for regularization func-
tions, mask sizes, station gain constraints, and noise budgets.
All three integrate low- and high-band data without ﬂagging
but differ in their initial models and exploration strategies
[98].
B.1 EHT-imaging (Event Horizon Telescope imaging
algorithm)
EHT-Imaging reconstructs high-resolution black hole images
from VLBI data.
Purpose: Produces high-ﬁdelity images of black hole
shadows and photon rings, e.g., the M87 black hole.
Methodology:
Visibility Data: Processes complex Fourier analysis
from Earth-based radio telescopes.
Image Reconstruction: EHT-Imaging uses regular-
ized maximum likelihood (RML) methods, combin-
ing prior knowledge with regularization techniques
like MEM and CLEAN for producing smooth images.
Calibration: Reﬁnement of a simple geometrical ini-
tial model, and optimization of hyperparameters.
Regularization: To prevent overﬁtting, techniques
like total variation (TV) minimization are applied.
Final Image: High-resolution images of the black
hole’s shadow, revealing detailed features such as
photon rings.
Key Techniques: Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE),
Bayesian Inference, and Regularization.
Applications: Imaging black hole shadows (e.g., M87*
and Sgr A*) and accretion disk structures.
B.2 SMILI (sparse modeling imaging library for
interferometry)
SMILI enhances image reconstruction accuracy using statis-
tical methods, particularly with sparse interferometric data.
Purpose: Improves reconstruction accuracy by incorpo-
rating statistical models by focusing on sparse data sce-
narios.
Methodology:
Initial Model: Starts with basic structures like a point
source or Gaussian disk and reﬁnes them iteratively
via gradient descent.
Bayesian Framework: Maximizes posterior probabil-
ity by integrating noise characteristics of data with the
model assumptions.
Data Fitting: Adjusts parameters to the best-ﬁt observed
data while preserving consistency with the prior
information.
Regularization: Added constraints (e.g. smoothness
or sparsity) to avoid overﬁtting and ensure recon-
structed features, like ring morphology.
Iterative Reﬁnement: Uses methods like Markov
Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to reﬁne
images and estimate uncertainties in the model.
Key Techniques: Bayesian Inference, MCMC, and Reg-
ularization.
Applications: High-resolution imaging, artifact reduc-
tion with low signal-to-noise ratio, and imaging of black
hole accretion disks and jets.
B.3 DIFMAP (Difmap imaging algorithm)
DIFMAP specializes in imaging, calibration, and analysis of
VLBI data.
Purpose: Used for reconstructing and data analysis of
VLBI observations, focusing on compact sources such
as quasars and black holes.
Methodology:
Visibility Data Processing: Calibrates phase and
amplitude from complex visibility data.
CLEAN Deconvolution: The algorithm subtracts iter-
atively components from raw observed data, and
hence creates a cleaner image of the source.
Model Fitting: Model-ﬁtting techniques in which
Gaussian components are ﬁtted to observed data, sep-
arating different components of observed sources like
shadows and accretion disks in black holes.
Imaging Algorithms: Employs various imaging algo-
rithms such as Multi-Frequency Synthesis (MFS) for
wide-band VLBI data.
Self-Calibration: Enhances image quality for low-
SNR data by iteratively correcting phase and ampli-
tude.
Key Techniques: CLEAN, Model Fitting, and Self-
Calibration.
Applications: Imaging black hole accretion disks, jets,
and detailed structures of radio sources.
