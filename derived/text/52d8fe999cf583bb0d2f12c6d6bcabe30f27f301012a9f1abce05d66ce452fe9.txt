ABSTRACT
Thehydrogen-bonddonorabilityofseleniumhasbeeninvestigatedbystudyingbenzeneselenol
dimers and the complex of benzeneselenol with dimethyl sulﬁde (S(CH3)2) using matrix-
isolation infrared spectroscopy and electronic-structure calculations. The structures and rela-
tive stability of the complexes were characterised computationally, while their spectroscopic
signatureswereexaminedundercryogenicAr-matrixconditions.FormationofSe–H ⋅⋅⋅ Seand
Se–H⋅⋅⋅ ShydrogenbondswasestablishedfromthecharacteristicredshiftoftheSe–Hstretch-
ing vibration mode of benzeneselenol, supported by comparison with simulated vibrational
spectra. Several benzeneselenol dimer conformers with closely spaced stabilisation energies
were predicted. The calculated vibrational frequencies of these four conformers were assigned
to the experimentally observed Se–H stretching bands at 2276 and 2290 cm−1. These bands
correspond to a red shift of 75 and 61 cm−1 compared to the benzeneselenol monomer, and are
assigned to the trans and cis conformer pairs, respectively. For the [PhSeH-S(CH3)2] complex,
a new band appeared at 2223 cm−1 upon annealing, corresponding to a red shift of 128 cm−1,
conﬁrmingtheformationofaSe–H⋅⋅⋅ Shydrogenbond.Thisworkprovidestheﬁrstimpression
of the Se–H⋅⋅⋅ Se hydrogen bond interaction in benzeneselenol dimer and the Se–H⋅⋅⋅ S
hydrogen bond interaction in [PhSeH-S(CH3)2].
1. Introduction
Hydrogenbondingisoneofthemostimportantnon-covalentinteractionsandplaysafundamentalroleinchemistry,
biology, supramolecular chemistry, interstellar chemistry, and biophysics [1–9]. In the chalcogen series, oxygen- and
sulfur-centred hydrogen bonding interactions have been extensively investigated, where oxygen and sulfur act both
as hydrogen bond donors and acceptors [10–17]. In contrast, selenium-centred hydrogen bonding interactions remain
comparatively less explored despite the growing importance of selenium-containing compounds in chemistry and
biology [18, 19]. Selenocysteine, often referred as the 21st amino acid, contains a selenol (Se–H) functional group
and is present in several human proteins [20, 21]. The Se–H functional group is also present in selenomethionine.
Organoselenium compounds exhibit remarkable versatility and participate in a wide range of transformations with
applications extending from organic synthesis to materials chemistry and biology [18, 19]. Selenium-containing
compounds have also attracted considerable attention because of their biological activities, including anticancer,
antibacterial, antiviral, and antioxidant properties [20, 22, 23]. Owing to the unique electronic properties of selenium,
selenium chemistry continues to emerge as an active area of investigation in biochemistry, ligand chemistry, and
semiconductor science [18, 19, 24]. Understanding selenium-centred non-covalent interactions is therefore important
for elucidating the structure, stability, and reactivity of selenium-containing molecular systems.
Recent investigations have primarily focused on the hydrogen bond acceptor ability of selenium. Several experi-
mental investigationshave establishedthe formationof hydrogenbonds in whichselenium servesas ahydrogen-bond
acceptorinvariousmolecularcomplexes.TheﬁrstexperimentalevidenceforO–H ⋅⋅⋅ Sehydrogenbondinginteraction
wasreportedusingmolecularjetspectroscopyexperiments[25]withphenolasthehydrogenbonddonorandSe(CH 3)2
as the hydrogen bond acceptor. The complexation-induced red shift in the OH stretching frequency (ΔOH) of phenol
was found to be 240 cm−1. More recently, hydrogen-bonded complexes of alcohols with Se(CH3)2 were investigated
usingroom-temperaturegas-phaseFTIRspectroscopy[26].Theobserved ΔOHvaluesforCH 3OHandC 2H5OHwere
113and124cm −1,respectively.Interestingly,nearlyidenticalredshiftswereobservedforagivendonormoleculewith

2. Experimental and Computational details
All the experiments were performed under matrix-isolation conditions and analysed using Fourier-transform
infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Details of the experimental setup have been described elsewhere [43]. Benzeneselenol
(PhSeH,ofpurity >97%)anddimethylsulﬁde(S(CH 3)2),ofpurity99%)wereprocuredfromSigma-Aldrichandused
withoutfurtherpuriﬁcation.High-purityargongas(99.999%)waspremixedwithvapoursofPhSeHandS(CH 3)2 ina
1 L stainless steel mixing chamber. Several gas-phase concentrations were initially examined to optimise the spectral
quality,minimiseself-associationofthemonomers,andmaximisethesignal-to-noiseratio.For(PhSeH) 2experiments,
optimal experimental conditions were achieved for a 1:2000 mixture of PhSeH:Ar. For the experiments of [PhSeH-
S(CH3)2]complex,optimalconditionswereobtainedfor2:1mixtureofPhSeH:S(CH 3)2withdilutionratioswithArof
approximately 1:3000 and 1:6000 with respect to PhSeH and S(CH3)2, respectively. The prepared gas mixtures were
deposited through an eﬀusive nozzle (1.58 mm diameter) onto a polished KBr window maintained at 16 K using a
closed-cycle helium cryostat. Sample deposition was carried out using a dosing valve at approximately 46 mmol h−1
for180minutesand210minutesforPhSeHand[PhSeH-S(CH 3)2]mixture,respectively.Theargonmatrixcontaining
isolatedPhSeHwasannealedfor120mintofacilitatetheformationof(PhSeH) 2,whereasPhSeH-S(CH 3)2 matrixwas
annealed for 60 min to promote the formation of [PhSeH-S(CH3)2] complex. Annealing was carried out by gradually
increasing the temperature of the matrix from 16 to 35 K, after which the matrix was cooled back to 16 K, and the
infrared spectra were recorded again. All FTIR spectra were recorded at 16 K using a Tensor II spectrometer (Bruker
Optics)equippedwithaDLaTGSdetectorandaKBrbroadbandbeamsplitter.ThefullIRspectrumofPhSeHhasbeen
presentedinESIFigureS1.Thespectrawererecordedintherangeof400-4000cm −1 withaninstrumentalresolution
of 1 cm−1. The spectrometer and sample compartment were continuously purged with dry nitrogen gas to eliminate
spectral interference from atmospheric CO2 and H2O.
Electronic structure calculations for the monomers and the corresponding complexes were performed using the
Gaussian 16 programme package [44] using the PBE-D3 functional in conjunction with 6-311++G(2d,2p) level.
All the coordinates were fully relaxed to scan the dimer potential energy surface of (PhSeH)2 and [PhSeH-S(CH3)2]
complex. Harmonic vibrational frequency calculations conﬁrmed that all optimised structures correspond to minima
on the potential energy surface. The binding energy (BE) of each complex was calculated as the diﬀerence between
the energy of the complex and the sum of the energies of the isolated monomers. The binding energies were further
corrected for harmonic zero-point vibrational energy (ZPVE) and basis set superposition error (BSSE) using the
counterpoise method [45], which yields the corresponding stabilisation energies (D0). The stabilisation energies of
the complexes are listed in ESI Table S1 along with all the energy values. Quantum theory of atoms in molecules
(QTAIM)andnaturalbondorbital(NBO)analyseswereperformedusingtheAIMAll[46]andNBO7.0[47]program
packages, respectively, to characterise the nature of the non-covalent interactions in the complexes. The second-order
perturbationstabilisationenergy( ΔE(2)
ij )associatedwithcharge-transferinteractionswasobtainedfromNBOanalysis.
Symmetry-adapted perturbation theory calculations at the SAPT2 [48] level were performed using the PSI4 program
package [49] to partition the total interaction energy of the complexes into electrostatic, induction, dispersion, and
exchange-repulsion contributions.
3. Results and discussion
Electronic structure calculations have been performed to manually scan the potential energy surface of (PhSeH)2
dimer and 1:1 complex of [PhSeH-S(CH3)2]. The results of theoretical calculations are described ﬁrst, followed by
those of the matrix isolation IR spectroscopy experiment. Finally, experimental IR spectra have been assigned to the
corresponding hydrogen-bonded conformers using calculated geometries and simulated vibrational frequencies.

5. Conclusion
The present study provides the ﬁrst spectroscopic evidence for Se–H⋅⋅⋅ Se and Se–H⋅⋅⋅ S hydrogen-bond
interactions, as revealed by matrix-isolation infrared spectroscopy and supported by electronic-structure calculations.
The formation of (PhSeH)2 and the [PhSeH-S(CH3)2] complex was established from characteristic changes in the
Se−H mode. The potential energy surface scan revealed multiple minima corresponding to diﬀerent conformers of
(PhSeH)2. The lowest-energy structures are stabilised by a combination of Se–H⋅⋅⋅ Se hydrogen bond and⋅⋅⋅ 
interactions,withfourcloselylyingconformersseparatedbyonly1.33kJ/mol,whereasotherconformersareprimarily
stabilised by Se–H⋅⋅⋅  interactions. The experimentally observed red shifts of 75 and 61 cm−1 for theSe−H mode
provide spectroscopic signatures of the trans and cis dimer pairs, respectively, which are stabilised by Se–H⋅⋅⋅ Se
hydrogenbondinteractions.The[PhSeH-S(CH 3)2]complexisstabilisedbyaSe–H ⋅⋅⋅ Shydrogenbond,asindicated
by a stabilisation energy of 24.19 kJ/mol and a substantial red shift of 128 cm−1 in the Ar matrix. The nature of
these interactions is further supported by quantum-chemical analyses. The presence of bond critical points along the
Se–H⋅⋅⋅ SeandSe–H⋅⋅⋅ Sbondpathsconﬁrmstheexistenceofhydrogenbondinteractions,whilethecorresponding
electron-density characteristics indicate that the interactions are predominantly non-covalent in nature. NBO analysis
shows greater charge transfer for the Se–H⋅⋅⋅ S interaction than for the Se–H⋅⋅⋅ Se interaction. The EDA analysis
revealsthat(PhSeH) 2 ispredominantlydispersion-driven,whereasthe[PhSeH-S(CH 3)2]complexpossessesrelatively
largerelectrostaticandinductioncontributions.Furthermore,thespectroscopicandtheoreticalresultsdemonstratethat
seleniumcanactasahydrogen-bonddonorthroughtheSe–HgroupandthatSe–H ⋅⋅⋅ SeandSe–H⋅⋅⋅ Sinteractions
can contribute signiﬁcantly to the structure and stabilisation of molecular complexes.
