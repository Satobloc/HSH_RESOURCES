We develop a statistical-mechanical theory of water in unsaturated soil whose outcome is a con-
tinuum field equation for the pore-occupancyg(r,x,t)∈[0,1]—the fraction of pores of radiusrthat
are water-filled at positionxand timet. The theory is built by passing through three scales. At
themicroscale, single inter-pore water transfers are set by Hagen–Poiseuille ratesκ(r)and a driving
potentialΦ(r,r ′)—the difference of the pore-class chemical potentials, taken here in its capillary–
gravitational form but open to adsorptive, osmotic, or thermal refinement without change to the
theory. Averaging over a representative volume gives amesoscalemaster equation whose gain and
loss terms relax the occupancy toward the equilibrium stepg eq =H(r ∗−r)fixed by chemical-
potential minimization. Contracting the volume to a point promotesgto a field and yields the
continuumbalance∂ tg+∇·F=C[g]−E−T, withC[g]the redistribution operator and∇·F
the transport divergence—the central equation of the theory, of which everything else is a limit, a
moment, or a boundary resolution.
The kinetic equation is an Onsager gradient flow: it descends the Gibbs free energy with a
state-dependent mobility, withdF/dt≤0a theorem for the isothermal, unforced system and mass
conservation as its zeroth moment. A single dimensionless group, the pore-resolved Damköhler
numberDa(r,x) =τ redis(r)/τforcing(x), organizes the behavior and unifies phenomenologies long
modelled separately. A Chapman–Enskog reduction identifies Richards’ equation as the quasi-static
(Da→0) limit—with the matric potentialψand the hydraulic conductivityK(θ)emerging only
there,Kvanishing below the percolation threshold; capillary-bundle and critical-path conductivity
models are its diagonal and spectral limits, and dynamic capillary-pressure models its one-moment
approximation. Hysteresis is the holonomy of a forcing bundle over the space of boundary histories,
a geometric phase rather than per-pore bistability, with a falsifiable loop-area lawH∝I 2 that
no rate-independent model reproduces. Preferential flow is what the same equation does where
Da≳1, so the long-standing dichotomy between Richards-equation and preferential flow becomes
a continuous,Da-controlled crossover within one framework. Out of the quasi-static limitg(r)is
the irreducible state variable and no scalar potential characterizes the system. All inputs—f(r),
C(r,r′), ¯L,¯τ—are geometric properties of the pore network, measurable from micro-CT images and
calibrated against no macroscopic retention or conductivity data.
I. INTRODUCTION
The dynamics of unsaturated porous media is inher-
ently irreversible [1]. Under realistic forcing, the system
does not evolve through a sequence of equilibrium states,
but through a succession of non-equilibrium configura-
tions. Capillary hysteresis is the most familiar manifes-
tation of this irreversibility: identical macroscopic states
such as the same water and energy content can corre-
spond to different internal configurations depending on
whether the system was reached by wetting or drying,
slowly or rapidly [2, 3].
Another long-standing difficulty in unsaturated flow
is the coexistence of two seemingly distinct behaviors.
When wetting is moderate or absent, water redistribu-
tion is slow, capillary-controlled, and close to local equi-
librium; flow is dominated by matric forces and is reason-
ably described by the classical continuum model known
∗ riccardo.rigon@unitn.it
as Richards equation [4–6]. In contrast, during intense or
rapid wetting events, water preferentially occupies larger,
well-connected pores, bypassing finer pores that would be
favored by equilibrium thermodynamics. This dual be-
havior has been emphasized in the seminal critiques of
Richards’ equation by Germann and Beven [7–9], who
argued that preferential flow and bypass mechanisms vi-
olate the assumptions underlying single-domain formula-
tions.
These observations have motivated the development
of dual-domain and dual-permeability extensions of
Richards’ equation, in which the pore space is parti-
tioned into interacting fast and slow domains [10]. While
successful in many applications, such approaches intro-
duce additional state variables and exchange terms phe-
nomenologically, without explaining how the two flow
regimes emerge from a common physical description. A
complementary, Lagrangian line of work represents soil
water as an ensemble of particles of unequal mobility [11].

From a physical perspective, the key issue is that
macroscopic variables such as water content are coarse-
grained descriptors. Many distinct pore-scale configu-
rations correspond to the same macroscopic state, yet
differ in connectivity, accessibility, transport efficiency,
and energy content. When forcing is slow compared to
internal relaxation processes, such as film flow, vapor
transport, and interfacial rearrangement, the system re-
mains close to equilibrium and capillary ordering domi-
nates [12]. When forcing is rapid, relaxation cannot keep
pace, and water occupies geometrically accessible path-
ways rather than thermodynamically optimal ones. The
same porous medium therefore exhibits qualitatively dif-
ferent behavior depending on the relative timescales of
forcing and relaxation. These dynamics generated the
phenomenon known as hydraulic hysteresis [13, 14] which
is not explained by Richards equation and usually treated
with ad hoc methods [15].
These considerations motivate a shift in perspective, in
whichthreeelementsplaycomplementaryroles. Thermo-
dynamics defines the equilibrium targets. Network topol-
ogy constrains which microscopic rearrangements are ge-
ometrically admissible. Relaxation dynamics governs the
rate at which the system can move through configura-
tion space. When relaxation is fast relative to forcing,
the system tracks equilibrium and classical matric flow
emerges; when forcing is fast, relaxation is incomplete,
flow bypasses the smallest pores, and preferential path-
ways appear. The transition between the two behaviors
iscontinuousanddynamicallycontrolled, ratherthanim-
posed by the model structure.
The theory developed here explicitly tracks this ir-
reversible evolution through non-equilibrium states and
naturally unifies these behaviors within a single formal-
ism. Although motivated by water flow in porous media,
the framework is not specific to capillarity. It provides a
general description of systems in which local thermody-
namic equilibria exist, but macroscopic behavior emerges
from constrained, history-dependent transitions between
them. Similar structures arise in plastic deformation,
magnetic hysteresis, and glassy dynamics, where irre-
versibility results from the interplay between thermody-
namic driving, geometric constraints, and finite relax-
ation times [16–18]. Phenomenological understanding,
together with over a century of measurements [2, 5, 19],
points to two distinct scales: an internal one, whose dy-
namics is governed by the topology and geometry of the
soil medium [20], and a larger one dominated by trans-
port. The natural mathematical representation of such a
two-scale system is a fiber bundle [21].
The remainder of this paper formalizes this perspec-
tive using tools from statistical physics and explores its
implications for transport and upscaling in porous me-
dia. Hysteresis, memory, rate dependence, and preferen-
tial flow emerge naturally as consequences of irreversible
non-equilibrium evolution, rather than as ad hoc modifi-
cations of equilibrium theory.

Drying mechanisms produce distinct pore emptying se-
quences.
•Capillary drainage(the laboratory idealization):
Gas invades fromthe boundary, constrained by net-
work topology. Emptying proceeds from largest ac-
cessible pores to smallest. The accessibilitya(r)is
controlled by gas-phase percolation.
•Stage I evaporation(atmosphere-limited,
Sec. VIIID): Water at the surface, preferen-
tially in smaller pores, where it redistributed after
infiltration, is removed by atmospheric demand.
The liquid network replenishes the surface through
capillary flow. Pore emptying is controlled by
the atmospheric boundary condition, not by the
air-phase topology [35].
•Stage II evaporation(diffusion-limited): The liquid
network disconnects from the surface. Water va-
porizes within the soil, preferentially in larger pores
(higher vapor pressure at lower capillary pressure),
then diffuses upward as vapor. This creates a pore
emptying sequence opposite to capillary drainage:
large pores empty by vaporization even when the
gas phase has no hydraulic access to them.
•Root uptake(plant-mediated): Roots extract water
throughtheroot–soilinterface, accessingporesthat
are hydraulically connected to the root network.
This can remove water from pores that are inac-
cessible to both the gas-phase boundary and the
surface evaporation front, producing yet another
emptying sequence.

more strongly-held configurations (more negativeψ)

water in smaller pores (lower energy, more negativeψ)

VIII. THE FORCING BUNDLE
The Chapman–Enskog reduction of Sec. VII collapsed
the kinetic equation onto Richards’ equation in the quasi-
static limitDa→0. The non-equilibrium content of the
theory—hysteresis—livesintheoppositedirection: inthe
dependence ofg(r,t)on thehistoryof the boundary forc-
ing, not on its instantaneous value. This dependence is
already contained in the dynamics of Eq. (42); to expose
it explicitly we recast that history dependence as a fiber
bundle over the space of forcing protocols, whose curva-
ture is the wetting–drying commutator[W,D]and whose
holonomy around a closed forcing loop is the hysteresis
loop itself (§VIIIF).
When a REV is subjected to time-varying boundary
forcing—rainfall, drainage, evapotranspiration—its fill-
ing distributiong(r,t)evolves along a path that depends
on the forcing sequence, not just on its current value.
This path dependence is invisible to the equilibrium re-
tention curveθ(ψ), which assigns a single occupancy to
each water content; capturing it requires carrying the
configuration as a functional of the forcing history, which
is what the bundle below does.

A. Hysteresis is geometry, not bistability
The first payoff concernshowthe medium remem-
bers its wetting history. Classical hysteresis theories—
independent-domain models [3, 13, 23] and their Preisach
formulation [16]—represent the pore space as a collection
of bistable units (hysterons), each with fixed wetting and
drying thresholds, and reproduce the primary loop and
the scanning curves by superposition over the unit den-
sity. They are descriptively successful, but theypositthe
two ingredients that matter: the per-unit bistability and
the rate-independence of the loop.
The kinetic theory supplies both as consequences
rather than assumptions. Bistability is not attached to
individual pores; it emerges from the non-commutativity
of the wetting and drying generators,[W,D]̸= 0
(Sec. VIIIF), together with accessibility. A closed
wetting–drying cycle is a loop in forcing space, and the
configuration’s failure to return is the holonomy of that
loop—the scanning curve of soil physics is a geometric
phase, not a bookkeeping of latched hysterons. A two-
class worked example [26] makes this explicit: precipi-
tation fills both classes non-selectively [Eq. (66)] while
drainage empties the large class first [Eq. (67)], so af-

X. CONCLUSIONS
We have derived a continuum theory of unsaturated
soil water from the bottom up. Its outcome is a single
local balance [Eq. (42)],
∂tg(r,x,t) +∇·F=C[g]−E−T,
for the pore-occupancy fieldg(r,x,t)—the fraction of
pores of radiusrthat are water-filled atx. The the-
ory is built by passing through three scales and is best
read as that passage. At themicroscale, single inter-pore
transfers are governed by Hagen–Poiseuille ratesκ(r)and
the capillary–gravitational driving potentialΦ. At the
mesoscale, averaging over a representative volume turns
these into gain and loss operators whose difference is the
redistributionC[g], relaxing the occupancy toward the
equilibrium stepg eq =H(r ∗−r). At thecontinuum
scale, contracting the volume to a point promotesgto
a field and the inter-volume exchange to the transport
divergence∇·F, giving Eq. (42). Everything else in the
paper—Richards’ equation, the conductivity, hysteresis,
preferential flow—is a limit, a moment, or a boundary
resolution of this one equation.
The continuum theory unifies phenomenologies that
have long been modelled separately. Richards’ equation
is its quasi-static (Da→0) Chapman–Enskog reduc-
tion, with the matric potentialψand the conductivity
K(θ)emerging only in that limit, where local equilibrium
makes the mapg→θ→ψone-to-one; out of the limit
g(r)is the irreducible state variable and no scalar poten-
tial exists. Capillary-bundle and critical-path conductiv-
ity models are its diagonal and spectral limits; dynamic
capillary-pressure models are its one-moment approxima-
tion. Hysteresis is the holonomy of the forcing bundle—a
geometric phase, not per-pore bistability—and preferen-
tial flow is what the same equation does where the lo-
cal Damköhler numberDa(r,x)exceeds unity. The sin-
gle dimensionless groupDa =τ redis/τforcing organizes the
whole:Da≪1gives Richards’ equation,Da∼1gives
rate-dependent hysteresis,Da≳1gives preferential flow.
Whether Richards’ equation applies is therefore a prop-
erty of the soil–forcing combination at each point, not of
the soil alone.
Two features make the construction more than a re-
formulation. It isthermodynamically grounded: Eq. (42)
descends the Gibbs free energy as an Onsager gradient
flow,dF/dt≤0(Appendix D), with mass conservation
as the zeroth moment. And it isconstructive: every
ingredient—f(r),C(r,r ′), ¯L,¯τ, and henceκ,Φ,Da—
is a geometric property of the pore network, measurable
from micro-CT without calibration against retention or
conductivity data. The capillary–gravitational form of
Φused throughout is a deliberate simplification, not a
limitation: because the theory uses the driving poten-
tial only through its values, the single point of entry
for new physics isΦitself. Adding disjoining, osmotic,
or thermal terms toΦcarries the theory to adsorptive
films, saline soils, and coupled heat transport while leav-
ing the operator algebra, the gradient-flow structure, and
the Chapman–Enskog reduction unchanged.
Structurally, Eq. (42) is the porous-media analogue of
the Boltzmann equation (Table I):g(r)↔f(v), redis-
tribution↔collision integral, molecular chaos↔the
mean-field closure, and Chapman–Enskog→Richards
mirroring Chapman–Enskog→Navier–Stokes, down to
theH-theorem and the breakdown at sharp fronts (the
analogue of shocks). The same skeleton supports the
classical analogies with magnetic hysteresis (Preisach–
Bertotti) and plasticity [16–18], now made precise:θ c
is a yield surface, trapped water a plastic strain, and
hysteresis the holonomy of a state-dependent connection.

K. Statistical-mechanical interpretation
Theg(r,t,x)framework performs a mean-field approx-

vidual pores, wetting and drying involve rapid Haines
jumps, thermal fluctuations, and pore-scale disorder. By
grouping all pores of radiusrwithin the REV, individ-

stochastic noise cancels, leaving the deterministic kinetic
equation.

under averaging. The Damköhler number determines

individual filling events is frozen intog(r).

Prediction.During rapid infiltration, the pore-filling
