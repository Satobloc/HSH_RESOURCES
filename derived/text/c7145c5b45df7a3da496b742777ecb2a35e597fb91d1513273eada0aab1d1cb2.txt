Abstract. The purpose of these notes is to expose the structure, general strategy, and the main
ideas of the proof in [3]. The core part of the proof is a purely combinatorial cutting algorithm
for certain graphs called molecules. We first explain the steps reducing to this algorithm, and then
explain the algorithm. These parts are essentially independent of each other.

We give an exposition of the ideas and proof in [3]. The overall strategy consists of (a) proof of two
key formulas (KF1)–(KF2); (b) reduction (using the two key formulas) to a purely combinatorial
cutting algorithm for a certain class of graphs called molecules; and (c) construction of the cutting
algorithm. These parts are essentially independent of each other, and we will explain each of them
in these notes.

• In Section 2, we are presenting the proof of (KF1)–(KF2) in a superficially different way

The role of molecules. In the above induction process, M′ records the collision history (of
suitable sets of particles) in [0 , (ℓ− 1)τ], and Mℓ records the collision history in [( ℓ− 1)τ,ℓτ ], so
M = M′∪ Mℓ records the collision history on [0,ℓτ ]. Intuitively, this molecule M is a topological
reduction of the actual particle trajectories. More precisely (see Definition 2.2):
• The collisions and overlaps are represented by the C-atoms and O-atoms in M, which
are abstract atoms;
• The linear pieces of the trajectories (between two adjacent collisions or overlaps) are
represented by the edges in M, which are either abstract bonds connecting two atoms, or
abstract ends sticking to one atom;
• The trajectory of each particle is represented by a set of atoms and bonds forming a
particle line.
In the proof of (KF1)–(KF2) (Section 2), these intuitions will be very important in understanding
the cluster expansions and how they translate to the molecule language. For example, the collisions
within each cluster are just the C-atoms forming a C-molecule (Definition 2.1), and the overlaps
between the clusters are just the O-atoms joining the C-molecules, see Definition 2.2.

second and third parts of the proof. This is because these proofs are based on the key notion of
cutting, which instantly breaks the above correspondence. We can still define cutting at the level
of (abstract) molecules, where cutting a molecule reduces it into two smaller molecules, but they

2.1.2. Step 1: Cluster expansions and C-molecules. We start by performing the cluster expansion4,
which naturally leads to the notion of C-molecules. This is the simplest kind of molecules that
only contains C-atoms (for collisions), which occur as topological reductions/abstractions of particle
trajectories, counting only collisions. Intuitively, each collision is turned into an abstract C-atom,
and each linear piece of trajectory (between two consecutive collisions, or before the first or after
the last collision) is turned into an abstract edge (either a bond or bottom end or top end).
The formal definition of C-molecules is as follows, see Figure 1 for an illustration:
Definition 2.1 (C-molecules). A C-molecule is a graph whose vertices are called C-atoms. We
also have edges which include bonds connecting two atoms (which are called top edge at the
lower atom and bottom edge at the higher atom; note one of the two atoms is drawn higher than
the other), and ends at one atom only (which may also be top or bottom). Each atom has exactly
2 top edges and 2 bottom edges (see the picture on the right in Figure 1). We divide the set of all
edges into finitely many disjoint chains, such that each chain starts from a bottom end and keep
going up until reaching a top end. Each such chain is called a particle line; for each atom n and
each top edge and bottom edge at n, we say they are serial is they belong to the same particle line.

3. Part B: Reduction to the algorithm
From now on, equipped with (KF1)–(KF2), the remaining goal is to estimate the right hand
side of (KF2), i.e. PI(M). Note that everything is now completely expressed in terms of
molecules M and associated notions (see for example the definition of I(M) in (2.16)). As
such, from now on we will only use notions of molecules and forget everything in the
picture of particles, collisions trajectories etc. In particular:
• Instead of “collisions” and “overlaps” we will talk about C- and O-atoms; instead of the
number of “recollisions” we will talk about the circuit rank (i.e. number of independent
cycles) in the molecule M.
• In Section 2 we have identified (physical) particles with (abstract) particle lines in molecules,
and used the same notion p for both of them. For now on, we will not refer to any physical
particles and p will only refer to the particle lines defined in Definition 2.1.
• The main combinatorial properties of M that will be used in the proof below are (a)–(d)
stated at the end of Section 2.1.6, which are also completely stated in terms of molecular
notions such as particle lines.

Now, we start with a multi-layer molecule M that satisfies the key properties described in (a)–(d)

Before starting, let us first recall several useful facts about molecules and cutting:
• We often refer to M′ ⊆ M as a (sub-)molecule by regarding any bond between M′ and
M\M′ as a free end in M′. Also, once we cut out any elementary component S, then we
will ignore it for the rest of the cutting algorithm and consider the molecule M\S only.
• An atom m is a parent (resp. child) of another n if m is connected to n by a bond with
m being above (resp. below) n. We say, m is a descendant (resp. ancestor) of n if it is
obtained by iteratively taking parents (resp. children) starting from m. Note that this is
a monotonic process, i.e. one cannot get back to the start by iteratively taking parents or
iteratively taking children.
• If we cut any atom n as free, then this introduces a bottom fixed end at each parent of n (if
exists), and a top fixed end at each child of n (if exists). Recall also that each atom has at
most 2 parents and at most 2 children.
4.1. The two-layer case. It turns out that, most of the ideas in the cutting algorithm for general
molecules M, are already contained in a simplified 2-layer case, where we assume that
(1) M has no O-atoms, and we ignore all the degeneracies (cf. Section 4.4);
(2) M is a 2-layer molecule M = MU∪ MD with an “up layer” MU and a “down layer” MD;
(3) MU and MD are trees without fixed end, and no atom in MD is parent of an atom in MU;
(4) The number of atoms in MU that are connected to an atom in MD by a bond, is at least ρ.
The two-layer molecules can be viewed as those appearing in the calculation of the cumulants
EH(2τ), the first nontrivial case beyond the short-time regime. Moreover, the treatment of the
general ℓ-layer case is not fundamentally harder than the 2-layer case, and only requires one extra
step of layer selection, see Section 4.4. In Figure 10, we list some example of two-layer molecules
that will feature in our discussions below.
4.2. The trichotomy. The treatment of the two-layer molecules defined in the previous section is
really an interpolation between three extreme scenarios, leading to a key trichotomy in the proof.
Each of the three scenarios will be represented by a toy model below, which is defined in terms of
further properties of the layers MU and MD, and each requires a different cutting strategy.
The classification into the three scenarios is based on the following two considerations:

4.3. Toy models I–III. We describe the strategy for each toy model. For more detailed description
of the algorithm with proofs, see [4].
4.3.1. Toy model I. The idea for toy model I is to apply a naive algorithm in MU, where we
always cut the lowest atom in MU, followed directly by a greedy algorithm in MD to extract{33}
components whenever they appears in MD. The heuristic algorithm is described as follows and is
depicted in Figure 11:
Definition 4.1 (Algorithm for Toy Model I). We define the following algorithm for toy models I.
(1) At each time, choose a lowest atom n in MU, and cut n as free.
(2) If this generates a pair of adjacent degree-3 atoms in MD, then we cut them as free, giving
a{33} elementary molecule (in what follows, we shall simply refer to this situation as the
“appearance of a{33}” in MD). For technical reasons, we also need to include the case of a
pair of degree-3 atoms in MD separated by one degree-4 atom in MD, which we cut together
as a {343} molecules (that can be further cut into a {3} molecule and a {33} molecule),
but this can be ignored for now. Repeat until no {33} (or{343}) molecule exist in MD.
(3) Go back to (1) and cut the next lowest atom in MU, and so on.
(4) When all atoms in MU have been cut, then cut MD one atom at a time, such that: if any
cutting causes the appearance of a {33} (or{343}) molecule in MD then go to (2) and
remove them, then cut the next atom, and so on.
For the precise description of this algorithm, as well as the proof that it yields (4.1), see Section
1.1 of [4]. Here, we content ourselves with the illustration of this algorithm by an example as
described in Figure 11, and making a few remarks about the intuition behind it:

(1) The reason we always cut a lowest atom in MU, is to guarantee that MU does not contain
any top fixed end (a top end that is fixed) in the whole process (this is because, cutting any lowest
atom, which has no child in MU at the time it is cut, will not generate any top fixed end in MU).
This property, as well as similar ones, will play an important role below; we refer to them as
monotonicity properties. The significance of the monotonicity property is to guarantee that there
cannot be any non-elementary molecules produced in the process. In fact, the only non-elementary
molecules of one atom are those of deg 1 or deg 2 with both top and bottom fixed ends, but they
cannot be produced under the monotonicity assumption.
(2) A drawback of the naive algorithm of cutting lowest is that, if the lowest atom happens to
have deg 4, then we will get a bad {4} molecule. However, it can be shown that for toy model
I, MU can have at most one lowest atom 10 (see Figure 12 and [4] for a proof). This allows us to
control the loss of{4} molecules.
(3) The overall idea is to (i) keep cutting lowest atoms in MU to guarantee monotonicity, and
thus guarantee that MU does not produce any “forbidden” non-elementary molecule (we do not
intend to gain from MU), and (ii) extract gain from MD whenever possible. In the end all the
bonds between MU and MD lead to gains, and all such gains are captured in MD.
4.3.2. Toy model I plus. Toy model I plus is viewed as an extension of toy model I, and the strategy
is similar; in fact, the greedy algorithm in MD, i.e. step (2) in the algorithm for toy model I, is
exactly the same for toy model I plus. The difference occurs in MU, as follows:
A key feature for toy model I is that MU has at most one lowest atom, which reduced the number
of bad{4} molecules, see (2) in Section 4.3.1 above. Now for toy model I plus, the MU may have
many lowest atoms, so a naive “cutting lowest” algorithm will not work. Instead, we shall avoid
deg 4 atoms and choose a lowest deg 3 atom n each time. This leads to a number of changes:
(i) if a deg 3 atom n is not lowest (it is only lowest among deg 3 atoms, which means it may have
children which are deg 4), then cutting it will create top fixed ends in MU. However, this top fixed
end will only occur at the children (and subsequently descendants) of n, which now has no bottom
fixed ends (since these children nodes must have deg 4 before n is cut, by definition). Denote by
Sn the set of its descendants (including n).
(ii) Subsequently, we can cut all descendants of n, going from high to low , and this maintains
the property that no bottom fixed end occurs at any descendants of n. Therefore, the monotonicity
property in Section 4.3.1 (1) is now replaced by the following: there is no top fixed end outside the
set Sn\{n} and no bottom fixed end inside the set Sn\{n}, where Sn is the set of descendants of n.
This idea leads to the following modification of the naive “cutting lowest” atom in MU (i.e. Step
(1) in the algorithm of Toy Model I), which is (essentially) theUP algorithm. Note that it preserves
the new version of monotonicity described in (ii) above, and thus does not create “forbidden” non-
elementary components. Moreover, it also minimizes the number of bad {4} molecules.
Definition 4.2 (The UP Algorithm). We define the following UP algorithm.
(1) Each time choose a lowest atom n among all deg 3 atoms (if there are no degree-3 atom,
choose a lowest degree 4 atom), and let Sn be the set of descendants of n.
(2) Cut all atoms in Sn as free one by one, each time cutting a highest atom m. If at any point,
m has a parent m+ or a child of m− that is also of degree 3, we cut {m, m±} together as a
{33} molecule.

4.4. Reduction to toy models. Finally, we briefly discuss the extra pieces that need to be added
to the above toy models, in order to treat the general case:
The O-atoms. In reality, the molecule M may contain O-atoms. This complicates the definition
of cutting, when the role of bonds is generally replaced by those of ov-segments. The latter are a
chain of O-atoms separating two C-atoms in a given particle line. We then need to translate the
relevant notions, such as parent/child and components, into ov-parent/ov-child and ov-components
etc., associated with the ov-segments. This translation, while tedious, will not cause any essential
change to the proof.
Layer selection. To reduce from multi-layer to 2-layer case, we need to perform one key step of
layer selection. Start with a layer ℓU with sℓU =|r(MℓU )| being largest. By the proof in Section
3.1, all the particle lines in r(MℓU ) are connected to each other via layers between 1 and ℓU; then
choose the highest layer ℓD, such that sufficiently many of these particle liness are connected to
each other via layers betweenℓD andℓU. In this way, most recollisions can be located to happen at
layer ℓD, so we can simply isolate layers ℓU and ℓD (with certain sets between ℓU andℓD attached
to ℓU) which already contain enough information, to reduce to the 2-layer case.
Layer refining. In the above, we have assumed each single layer of M is a tree/forest. In reality,
if some layer contains a cycle, we can divide this layer into two thin-layers, such that each of them
has no cycle. In general, we can divide each layer of M into at most (Γ + 1) thin layers, where
Γ is the recollision truncation threshold, such that each cluster in each layer contains at most Γ
cycles/recollisions (this can be stated purely in terms of molecules). This then allows us to perform
the same layer selection and toy model analysis as above.
Degeneracies. The degeneracies occur when the vector ze = (xe,ve) for certain edges e, which
may appear as fixed ends of {33} molecules, satisfy certain extra restrictions, which affects the
gain from{33} molecules. To deal with such degeneracies, our strategy is as follows: if there are
few degeneracies (compared to ρ), then they are negligible and we just proceed with the usual
algorithm; if there many of them, we perform a much simpler alternative cutting sequence where
most atoms are cut as {3} molecules, while the many extra restrictions will lead to extra gains in
integration, which will be sufficient.
Pre-processing. Finally, the cutting process associated with layer selection may lead to fixed ends
in the resulting 2-layer molecule; similarly, if 0 < #2conn≪|X| for the 2-layer model, we also need
to first cut these 2-connections which introduces fixed ends. To deal with these fixed ends, we need

an extra pre-processing step to first get rid of any {33} (and{343} molecules) in MD. This is a
straightforward process, see [3], Definition 11.8–Proposition 11.9 for more details.

The main enemy in the above statement is when a small group of particles within distance O(ε) of
each other produce many recollisions within time O(ε). To handle this, we need a new ingredient,
namely the absolute upper bound on the number of collisions given the number of particles, which
is proved by Burago-Ferleger-Kononenko. Basically, if Γ is sufficiently large, then these Γ + 1 rec-
ollisions must involve sufficiently many particles, with each new particle providing a{33}-molecule
in a suitable algorithm. The latter algorithm is inspired by that in Sections 10–11 in [3] to extract
the needed gain from this single cluster with many recollisions. See [3], Section 13.
The fA analysis in [3], Section 12. As explained in (2.13), the expansion of the fA is in
terms of molecules with only one root particle. Such molecules might not be trees, but if they are
not, then one can apply some basic algorithms (namely the UP algorithm) in order to prove that
they are lower order. As such, one can reduce to the case when all the molecules in (2.13) are trees.
Furthermore, a cancellation first observed in [1], shows that molecules that are not binary trees
cancel completely. The contribution of the remaining leading order molecules to (2.13), given
by binary trees, can be mapped directly order-by-order to the time τ iterates of the Boltzmann
equation with initial data given by fA((ℓ− 1)τ). By induction, this allows us to approximate
fA(ℓ′τ) by n(ℓ′τ) for all 1≤ℓ′≤ℓ, where n(t) is the solution of the Boltzmann equation.
