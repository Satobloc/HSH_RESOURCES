The Architecture Principle 
A Structural Foundation for Green-Function Theory 
Borros Arneth, Philipps University Marburg, Justus Liebig University Giessen, Germany, 
borros.arneth@staff.uni-marburg.de 
 
Abstract 
Green functions occupy a central position throughout modern mathematical physics. They 
describe propagation in differential equations, quantum mechanics, quantum field theory, 
condensed-matter systems, statistical mechanics, inverse problems, and graph the ory. 
During the development of Green-function theory, a rich mathematical hierarchy has 
emerged, including Hamiltonians, Green functions, Dyson equations, self -energies, 
Luttinger–Ward functionals, and thermodynamic potentials. Although these mathematical 
structures have been developed independently for different purposes, they fit together with 
remarkable coherence. The present work proposes the Architecture Principle, according 
to which these objects are successive mathematical representations of a more primitive 
entity, termed a Green-Function Architecture. Every admissible Green function is 
interpreted as one representation of an underlying transport architecture characterized by 
propagation, interaction, holonomy, and recursive closure. These four structural sectors are 
summarized by a reduced architectural coordinate tuple (𝑎,𝑏,𝑐,𝑑), which serves as a 
compact descriptor of architectural complexity. Rather than replacing established Green-
function theory, the proposed framework provides a structural interpretation unifying 
graph theory, spectral graph theory, quantum graphs, inverse p roblems, topology, and 
recursive Green-function constructions within a common mathematical language. The 
work establishes the mathematical foundations of Green-Function Architecture Theory, 
formulates precise representation principles, proves reduced struc tural classification 
results, and identifies the principal open mathematical problems required for a complete 
reconstruction theory. 
Keywords: Green functions; mathematical physics; graph theory; quantum graphs; 
reconstruction theory; spectral graph theory; topology. 
 
1. Introduction 
Green functions have become one of the universal mathematical tools of theoretical 
physics. Since the pioneering work of George Green in 1828, Green-function methods have 
been applied to boundary-value problems, differential equations, quantum mechanics, 
quantum field theory, condensed-matter physics, statistical mechanics, inverse problems, 
and graph theory [1–9].

During the last century, Green-function theory has evolved far beyond the original concept 
of a propagator. Modern formulations include Hamiltonian theory, Dyson equations, self-
energy formalisms, Luttinger–Ward functionals, Green functions on graphs, spectral graph 
theory, and recursive diagrammatic expansions [4 –18]. Despite this remarkable 
mathematical richness, these structures are usually introduced independently and for 
different physical purposes. 
The present work is motivated by a simple but fundamental question: 
Why does Green-function theory possess such a remarkably coherent mathematical 
hierarchy? 
Rather than considering Green functions as isolated analytical kernels, we propose that 
they should be regarded as representations of a deeper structural object. This leads to the 
central hypothesis of the present work. 
Architecture Principle. 
The primitive mathematical object of Green-function theory is not the Green function itself 
but a Green-Function Architecture describing the global organization of transport. Green 
functions, self-energies, functional representations, and statistical descriptions arise as 
successive mathematical representations of this underlying architecture. 
This viewpoint does not modify the established mathematical formalism of Green-function 
theory. Instead, it proposes a different logical ordering of its principal mathematical 
objects. 
The resulting framework, termed  Green-Function Architecture Theory (GFAT) , 
synthesizes ideas originating from graph theory, graph Laplacians, quantum graphs, 
spectral graph theory, algebraic topology, inverse graph problems, and recursive Green-
function methods into a common architectural language.

3. The Architecture Principle 
The central hypothesis of Green-Function Architecture Theory is that the hierarchy of 
Green-function theory is itself a representation hierarchy. 
Instead of beginning with the Hamiltonian, we introduce a more primitive object, 
𝒜,	
 
called a Green-Function Architecture. 
The Green-Function Architecture represents the complete structural organization of 
transport before any analytical representation is chosen. 
The Architecture Principle therefore proposes the following ordering: 
𝒜⟶𝐻⟶𝐺⟶Σ⟶Φ⟶Ω.	
 
Within this viewpoint, Hamiltonians, Green functions, self-energies, and thermodynamic 
potentials are interpreted as successive mathematical representations of the same 
underlying architecture. 
The Green function is therefore no longer regarded as the primitive mathematical object. 
Instead, it becomes one representation of a deeper transport structure. 
This interpretation is compatible with several established mathematical frameworks. 
Graph Laplacians describe transport through weighted graphs [13–15]. 
Quantum graphs describe Green functions by sums over propagation paths and vertex 
scattering processes [16–18]. 
Inverse graph problems demonstrate that graph structure may often be reconstructed from 
boundary or spectral information [19–21]. 
Recursive Green-function constructions organize perturbative expansions through families 
of Feynman graphs and Hopf-algebraic recursion [22–25]. 
These developments strongly suggest that Green functions already contain substantial 
structural information beyond point-to-point propagation. 
The Architecture Principle elevates this observation to the level of the primitive 
mathematical object.

4. Green-Function Architectures 
We now introduce the fundamental definition of the theory. 
Definition 1 (Green-Function Architecture). 
A Green-Function Architecture is the ordered triple 
𝒜=(Γ,𝑈,𝐺),	
 
where 
• Γ denotes a directed transport graph, 
• 𝑈 denotes the family of transport operators acting on the graph, 
• 𝐺 denotes the associated Green-function representation. 
The graph describes the transport topology. 
The transport operators describe propagation through the graph. 
The Green function provides the analytical representation of the resulting transport process. 
Unlike conventional Green-function theory, where the Green function itself is regarded as 
the fundamental object, the present framework regards the architecture as primitive and the 
Green function as one of its mathematical representations. 
This interpretation immediately suggests that the structural complexity of a Green -
Function Architecture should be describable independently of any particular analytical 
representation. 
The remainder of the present work develops such a reduced structural description based on 
four primitive architectural sectors. 
5. The Four Primitive Architectural Sectors 
The central hypothesis of Green-Function Architecture Theory is that every Green-
Function Architecture possesses four independent structural components describing 
distinct aspects of transport organization. 
Rather than introducing these sectors axiomatically, we motivate them by observing that 
modern Green-function theory already separates naturally into four complementary 
mathematical structures.

===== PAGE 6 =====
6	
The first concerns propagation itself. Green functions describe transport between points, 
while graph Laplacians and quantum graphs describe transport through networks [13–
18]. 
The second concerns interaction. In many-body theory interactions are represented by 
the self-energy, while in graph-based descriptions they correspond to scattering vertices 
or branching points [4–12,16–18]. 
The third concerns global topology. Green functions depend not only on local 
propagation but also on the existence of closed transport cycles, graph topology, 
homology classes, and spectral properties of the underlying transport network [25]. 
The fourth concerns recursive closure. Dyson equations, recursive Green-function 
methods, Hopf algebras of Feynman graphs, and Dyson–Schwinger equations all 
demonstrate that Green-function theory possesses an intrinsic recursive organization [22–
24]. 
Accordingly, we define four primitive architectural sectors. 
Propagation 
The propagation sector contains the transport paths of the architecture together with the 
corresponding transport operators. It describes how information propagates through the 
transport graph. 
Interaction 
The interaction sector contains the structural organization of branching and merging 
events. It describes how different transport channels communicate through interaction 
vertices. 
Holonomy 
The holonomy sector describes global topological organization. Closed transport cycles, 
independent loops, cycle spaces, and related topological invariants belong to this sector. 
Recursive Closure 
The recursive sector describes structural feedback. Recursive graph insertions, strongly 
connected components, recursive transport, and graph self-similarity belong to this 
sector. 
These four sectors are proposed as independent structural components of the architecture.

7. Reduced Structural Classification 
The coordinate tuple possesses two important mathematical properties. 
Theorem 1 (Reduced Completeness). 
Within the reduced four-sector model, the tuple 
(𝑎,𝑏,𝑐,𝑑)	
 
completely classifies Green-Function Architectures up to reduced architectural 
equivalence. 
Proof. 
Reduced architectural equivalence is defined by equality of the four primitive structural 
functionals corresponding to propagation, interaction, holonomy, and recursive closure. 
Consequently, 
(𝑎,𝑏,𝑐,𝑑)!=(𝑎,𝑏,𝑐,𝑑)"	
 
if and only if the two architectures possess identical values of all four structural 
functionals. 
Therefore, they are reduced architecturally equivalent. 
Conversely, reduced architectural equivalence immediately implies equality of the 
coordinate tuple. 
Hence the tuple completely classifies reduced Green-Function Architectures. 
 
 
A second result concerns minimality. 
Theorem 2 (Reduced Minimality). 
The coordinate tuple

Proof. 
Suppose one coordinate is removed. 
If the propagation coordinate is omitted, architectures differing only in propagation

Hence the four-coordinate system is minimal. 
 
These theorems establish the mathematical consistency of the reduced architectural 
description. They do not prove that every Green-Function Architecture in nature is 
completely described by four sectors; rather, they show that within the reduced GFAT 
model, the chosen coordinates form a complete and minimal classification.

9. The Architecture Representation Principle 
The principal hypothesis of Green-Function Architecture Theory is that the hierarchy of 
modern Green-function theory is a hierarchy of mathematical representations rather than 
a hierarchy of independent mathematical objects. 
Accordingly, we formulate the following principle. 
Architecture Representation Principle 
The Green-Function Architecture is the primitive mathematical object. The principal 
mathematical structures of Green-function theory arise as successive representations of 
this architecture. 
The proposed representation hierarchy is 
𝒜⟶(𝑎,𝑏,𝑐,𝑑)⟶Π⟶𝐻⟶𝐺⟶Σ⟶Φ⟶Ω. 
 
Each level represents a different mathematical description of the same underlying 
architecture. 
The first mapping assigns the reduced architectural coordinates to the complete 
architecture. 
The second mapping constructs the corresponding Architectural Multiplicity.

The Hamiltonian then provides an operator representation of the architecture. 
The Green function represents propagation within this Hamiltonian description. 
The self-energy represents interaction effects, while the Luttinger–Ward functional

Finally, the thermodynamic potential summarizes the statistical properties of the 
corresponding ensemble. 
The Architecture Principle therefore does not replace the mathematical hierarchy of 
Green-function theory. Instead, it proposes that the hierarchy itself originates from one 
deeper structural object. 
 
10. Relation to Existing Mathematical Frameworks 
One important feature of Green-Function Architecture Theory is that it is compatible with 
several well-established branches of mathematics. 
Graph theory provides the natural description of transport graphs and connectivity [13–
15]. 
Graph Laplacians provide Green functions through inverse or pseudoinverse operators 
[13–15]. 
Quantum graph theory represents Green functions as sums over propagation paths 
connected by vertex scattering matrices [16–18]. 
Spectral graph theory relates graph structure to spectral invariants and transport operators 
[13–15]. 
Algebraic topology describes global transport organization through cycle spaces, Betti 
numbers, and homology groups [25–28]. 
Inverse graph problems investigate the reconstruction of graph structure from Green-
function or boundary information [19–21]. 
Recursive Green-function methods, Hopf algebras, Dyson equations, and Dyson–
Schwinger recursion describe recursive organization within perturbative quantum field 
theory [22–24]. 
Rather than competing with these mathematical frameworks, Green-Function 
Architecture Theory seeks to unify them by interpreting each as describing one aspect of 
the same underlying architecture.

Within this interpretation, 
• graph theory describes propagation, 
• self-energy theory describes interaction, 
• topology describes holonomy, 
• recursive Green-function theory describes structural closure. 
The four architectural sectors therefore emerge naturally from existing mathematics 
rather than being introduced independently. 
 
11. Principal Open Mathematical Problems 
Although the reduced architectural framework developed in the present work is 
mathematically consistent, several important questions remain open. 
Problem 1 
Construct a universal reconstruction map 
ℛ:𝐺⟶𝒜. 
 
At present, reconstruction has been established only for restricted graph-based settings. 
 
Problem 2 
Prove the universal Four-Sector Decomposition Theorem. 
Specifically, determine whether every admissible Green-Function Architecture admits a 
unique decomposition into propagation, interaction, holonomy, and recursive closure. 
 
Problem 3 
Derive the Architectural Multiplicity from graph theory. 
The present work proposes the Architectural Multiplicity as a structural invariant. 
A rigorous derivation from transport-path enumeration, graph invariants, spanning 
forests, graph zeta functions, or related combinatorial structures remains to be developed.

12. Discussion 
The Architecture Principle proposes a structural interpretation of Green-function theory 
rather than a replacement of its established mathematical formalism. 
Modern Green-function theory already possesses an exceptionally rich hierarchy 
consisting of Hamiltonians, Green functions, Dyson equations, self-energies, Luttinger–
Ward functionals, and thermodynamic descriptions. 
The present work proposes that these objects should be interpreted as successive 
representations of one underlying Green-Function Architecture. 
This viewpoint naturally unifies concepts originating from graph theory, quantum graphs, 
algebraic topology, inverse graph problems, spectral graph theory, and recursive Green-
function methods. 
The reduced architectural coordinates 
(𝑎,𝑏,𝑐,𝑑) 
 
provide a compact description of the structural organization of the architecture, while the 
Architectural Multiplicity is proposed as the corresponding scalar invariant. 
Unlike conventional classification schemes, which classify physical observables, Green-
Function Architecture Theory classifies the transport architecture itself.

The resulting framework establishes a new structural viewpoint from which Green-
function theory may be understood. 
 
13. Conclusions 
The present work introduces the Architecture Principle as a structural foundation for 
Green-function theory. 
Instead of regarding Green functions as primitive mathematical objects, the proposed 
framework introduces Green-Function Architectures as the underlying structural entities 
from which Green functions arise. 
The architecture is characterized by four primitive structural sectors corresponding to 
propagation, interaction, holonomy, and recursive closure. 
These sectors are summarized by the architectural coordinate tuple 
(𝑎,𝑏,𝑐,𝑑), 
 
which provides a complete and minimal description within the reduced architectural 
theory. 
The Architectural Multiplicity is introduced as the principal scalar invariant associated 
with the reduced architecture. 
The central contribution of the present work is therefore not a modification of Green-
function theory itself but a new interpretation of its mathematical organization. 
Green functions, self-energies, functional representations, and thermodynamic quantities 
are viewed as successive representations of one common architectural object. 
This perspective suggests that the remarkable coherence of modern Green-function 
theory reflects an underlying structural unity rather than a collection of independent 
mathematical constructions. 
Whether the remaining open problems—particularly universal reconstruction, unique 
four-sector decomposition, derivation of the Architectural Multiplicity, and the 
Architecture Representation Theorem—can be solved remains an open question. 
Nevertheless, the Architecture Principle establishes a coherent mathematical framework 
and defines a precise research program for investigating the structural foundations of 
Green-function theory.
