Continuous-Frequency Characterization of the Temperature Coefficient of Phase 
Shift of Photoreceiver in Spaceborne Gravitational Wave Detection
Abstract: Phase stability of photoreceiver front-end circuits is important in precision 
heterodyne interferometry, including space-borne gravitational wave detection and 
intersatellite laser ranging. Conventional characterization schemes only measure the 
temperature coefficient of phase shift (TCPS) at a single discrete frequency, whereas 
practical photodetectors work within a wide bandwidth such as from 5 MHz to 25 MHz. 
How to accurately acquire the frequency-dependent TCPS over the full operating 
bandwidth remains an open technical challenge. In this work, a frequency-continuous 
TCPS measurement method combining temperature modulation and frequency 
sweeping is proposed. The phase-frequency responses of the device under test are 
measured at different steady-state temperatures using a vector network analyzer for 
frequency sweeping, and the TCPS is obtained by phase differencing between adjacent 
temperature points. The method is experimentally validated over 1–50 MHz using a 
bipolar-transistor-based transimpedance amplifier as a representative photoreceiver 
front-end. The measured TCPS exhibits clear frequency dependence and reaches an 
extremum of −7.4 ± 0.2 mrad/K at 19.8 ± 0.2 MHz. Monte Carlo simulations 
considering circuit-parameter uncertainties show consistency with the measured 
extremum frequency and magnitude. The proposed method provides a useful tool for 
evaluating thermally induced phase drift in photoreceiver front-ends for precision laser 
interferometry and other related areas.
Keywords
Photoreceiver front-end; Transimpedance amplifier; Heterodyne interferometry; 
Temperature coefficient of phase shift; Frequency-domain measurement
1. Introduction
High-precision laser interferometry and optoelectronic phase readout techniques 
are widely used in space-based gravitational wave detection, inter-satellite laser ranging, 
precision displacement measurement, and optical sensing [1-7]. Specifically, for space-
based gravitational wave detection, inter-satellite laser interferometers detect 
gravitational waves in the low-frequency band of 0.1 mHz~1 Hz. In such systems, the 
physical quantity to be measured is typically converted into phase variation of 
heterodyne beat-note signals. The long-term stability of phase readout directly 
determines the measurement sensitivity of the system [2]. For example, in space-based 
gravitational wave detection, inter-satellite laser interferometry requires highly stable 
phase measurements of MHz-level heterodyne beat-note signals, and consequently, 
phase noise in the optoelectronic readout link directly affects the system performance 
[8, 9]. Therefore, the phase stability of the optoelectronic readout chain is a critical issue 
in the design and performance evaluation of precision heterodyne interferometric 
systems.

In a laser heterodyne interference measurement link, the beat-note optical signal 
generated by interference must be converted into an electrical signal by a photoreceiver 
before it can be processed by the subsequent phase readout system [8]. As the front-end 
circuit of the photoreceiver, the transimpedance amplifier (TIA) converts the weak 
photocurrent generated by the photodiode into a usable voltage signal. Its 
transimpedance gain, bandwidth, phase-frequency response, and noise characteristics 
affect both the amplitude stability and phase stability of the beat-note signal [10, 11]. 
In practice, temperature fluctuations can cause drifts in the feedback resistance and 
capacitance, the small-signal parameters of active devices, and parasitic parameters, 
thereby changing the phase-frequency response of the TIA and affecting the final output 
phase [12, 13]. To quantitatively describe the sensitivity of the circuit phase to 
temperature variations, the temperature coefficient of phase shift (TCPS) is commonly 
introduced, which is defined as the output phase variation induced by a unit temperature 
change. Within a small temperature variation range, thermally induced phase noise can 
be estimated from the product of the ambient temperature noise and the TCPS.
Previous studies have investigated temperature-related phase drift in 
photoreceivers, TIAs, and phase readout systems. In 2014, Gerberding equivalently 
simulated temperature-induced circuit-parameter variations by changing the feedback 
resistance of a TIA, and theoretically analyzed the resulting change in the phase-
frequency response. The results showed that, at an operating frequency of 20 MHz, an 
approximately ±2% variation in a 10 kΩ feedback resistance could induce an output 
phase shift of about 10 mrad [14]. In 2017, Fernández Barranco et al. experimentally 
investigated the photoreceivers used in the GRACE Follow-On inter-satellite laser 
interferometry system. By applying temperature perturbations to the photoreceiver and 
measuring the corresponding phase variation of the beat-note signal, they obtained the 
experimental TCPS. The measurements showed that, at an operating frequency of 16 
MHz, the single-channel TCPS was approximately 4 mrad/K, whereas the differential 
TCPS was approximately 100 μrad/K [15]. In 2024, Feng et al. used a phasemeter to 
apply local temperature modulation to temperature-sensitive devices and 
synchronously measured the temperature and phase variations, thereby obtaining the 
TCPS of several devices at a single operating frequency [16].
The above studies indicate that temperature-to-phase drift is a critical concern in 
high-precision optoelectronic readout links, and the TCPS is an important parameter 
for evaluating circuit thermal stability and low-frequency phase noise. However, 
existing analyses and experimental results mostly focus on specific operating 
frequencies, typically providing the TCPS at a single frequency. In heterodyne 
optoelectronic readout links, the beat-note signal frequency is generally not fixed but 
varies over a certain range. Therefore, the response characteristics over the entire 
operating bandwidth need to be considered. A TCPS obtained at only a single frequency 
cannot fully reflect the thermally induced phase response of the photoreceiver front-
end over the target frequency range, nor can it identify the frequency regions and 
extremum features where the temperature-induced phase response is more sensitive.
Based on this, a method is proposed for measuring the TCPS using a vector 
network analyzer (VNA) for frequency-sweeping combined with temperature

2.2. TCPS Measurement Method
Based on the above definitions, a VNA is employed to perform swept-frequency 
phase measurements on the TIA under test. The VNA outputs a swept-frequency signal 
over a specified frequency range while simultaneously measuring the phase-frequency 
response of the device. By repeating these measurements at multiple steady-state 
temperature points, the phase-frequency responses 𝜑(𝑓,𝑇1), 𝜑(𝑓,𝑇2), 𝜑(𝑓,𝑇3), 𝜑
(𝑓,𝑇4), and 𝜑(𝑓,𝑇5) are obtained. The TCPS curves 𝑆𝑇,1(𝑓), 𝑆𝑇,2(𝑓), 𝑆𝑇,3(𝑓), and 
𝑆𝑇,4(𝑓) are then calculated from the phase-frequency differences between adjacent 
temperature points.
Through this approach, the frequency response of the DUT's TCPS across the target 
frequency band can be derived. Compared with point-by-point measurements using a 
phasemeter, this method does not require signal configuration, phase locking, and phase 
readout to be performed separately at each frequency point. Instead, the phase data over 
the entire frequency band are obtained directly from the swept phase-frequency 
response, thereby improving the convenience and efficiency of frequency-domain 
TCPS measurements. Meanwhile, to avoid additional phase drift introduced during 
temperature transitions, the phase-frequency response at each temperature point is 
acquired only after thermal equilibrium has been reached.

3. Model of the DUT
To validate the proposed frequency-domain measurement method for the TCPS, a 
discrete-transistor-based TIA was selected as a representative photoreceiver front-end 
for experimental investigation. Traditional TIAs based on integrated operational 
amplifiers feature simple structures and mature applications. However, in MHz-level 
weak-light heterodyne detection scenarios, their bandwidth, noise, and stability are 
typically constrained by the operational amplifier’s input noise, input capacitance, gain-
bandwidth product, and feedback network parameters [17, 18]. In contrast, a discrete-
transistor-based TIA offers greater design flexibility, allowing key design parameters 
such as the collector current and transconductance of the input transistor, as well as the 
feedback network parameters, to be adjusted according to specific requirements.
The transistor-based TIA circuit used in this work mainly follows the discrete 
heterojunction bipolar transistor (HBT) photoreceiver scheme proposed by Fernández 
Barranco et al. [15]. Designed for inter-satellite laser interferometry, this scheme uses 
HBTs to construct the transimpedance front-end and achieves low input-referred 
current noise and a large bandwidth in the MHz-level heterodyne frequency band. 
These results indicate the potential of transistor-based TIAs for high-precision laser 
interferometric optoelectronic readout links [15]. Meanwhile, the operating state of a 
transistor-based TIA depends on device parameters and bias conditions, and its phase-
frequency response is highly sensitive to temperature variations. Therefore, this circuit 
was selected as the experimental device for this study. We measured its phase-
frequency responses under different temperature conditions to obtain the frequency 
response of the TCPS and to further evaluate the feasibility and repeatability of the 
proposed frequency-domain measurement method.
In the experiment, the transistor-based TIA under test used a feedback resistance 
of R F=55 kΩ. The VNA swept the frequency from 1 MHz to 50 MHz with 1000 
sampling points. Five steady-state temperature points were selected. For each 
temperature point, seven complex frequency-response traces were recorded and 
averaged in the complex domain before extracting the phase-frequency response. An 
external temperature probe positioned near the DUT recorded the temperature data at a 
sampling rate of 1 Hz. The steady-state temperature recorded by this probe was used as 
the circuit temperature in the calculation of the TCPS.

4. Experimental Results
The TCPS curves are obtained from the difference between the phase-frequency 
responses measured at different steady-state temperature points. Therefore, the stability 
of the temperature points directly affects the calculation of the TCPS. During the 
experiment, an external temperature probe was placed near the DUT to record the actual 
temperature at the circuit location. Fig. 4. shows the temperature variation recorded by 
the external temperature probe during the experiment, where the gray shaded regions 
indicate the time windows used for steady-state temperature statistics and phase-
frequency response acquisition.
Since the time required to reach thermal equilibrium differs among set temperature 
points, the stabilization time for each point varies. Based on the temperature data 
recorded by the external temperature probe, steady-state statistical windows with small 
temperature variations were selected within each temperature plateau. The mean 
temperature within each window was used as the actual steady-state temperature of the 
corresponding temperature point, and the standard deviation was used to represent the 
temperature fluctuation at that point. In the subsequent calculation of the TCPS, these 
actual steady-state temperature values were used rather than the chamber set 
temperatures or the internal closed-loop control temperatures.
The five temperature points were 27.456(8) °C, 29.103(2) °C, 30.784(7) °C, 
32.541(6) °C, and 34.318(4) °C. The temperature fluctuations within each steady-state 
statistical window were small, indicating good stability of the temperature near the 
DUT during the phase-frequency response acquisition. These measurements can 
therefore be used for subsequent calculation of the TCPS.

The above results indicate that temperature variations induce changes in the phase-
frequency response of the DUT. However, these changes are not simple frequency-
independent offsets; rather, they are frequency-dependent. Therefore, measuring the 
phase variation with temperature at a single frequency point cannot fully capture the 
thermally induced phase response of the circuit across the entire target frequency band. 
To accurately characterize the temperature-dependent phase behavior of the device, 
frequency-domain measurements and analysis over the target band are necessary.

5. Discussion
The TCPS calculated based on nominal parameters can be used to analyze the 
frequency-response characteristics of the DUT. However, actual circuit components, 
such as resistors, capacitors, and small-signal transistor parameters, may deviate from 
their nominal values. These parameter uncertainties further affect the phase-frequency 
response and the TCPS. Therefore, a point-by-point comparison between the nominal 
model and experimental results is insufficient. To evaluate the influence of parameter 
uncertainties on the frequency response of the TCPS, a Monte Carlo simulation was 
conducted based on the complete circuit model [19, 20].
In the Monte Carlo simulation, random perturbations were applied to the model 
parameters affecting the phase-frequency response, centered on their nominal values. 
The perturbation ranges were set as ±10% for the small-signal transistor parameters, ±5% 
for the bias currents, ±10% for the post-stage amplifier parameters, and ±10% for the 
temperature coefficients. All parameters were sampled randomly within their respective 
predefined ranges according to a uniform distribution [21].
For each set of randomized parameters, the phase-frequency responses at different 
temperature points were recalculated, and the corresponding TCPS curves were 
obtained using the phase-difference method between adjacent temperature points. After 
1000 random simulations, the statistical distribution of the TCPS at different 
frequencies was obtained. To reduce the influence of extreme random samples, the 5% 
- 95% interval was adopted to represent the distribution range of the Monte Carlo 
simulation results.
Fig. 7. Monte Carlo simulation results of the TCPS curves under parameter uncertainties.
The experimentally measured TCPS curve is further compared with the Monte 
Carlo simulation results. As shown by the results, the experimental curve and the 
simulated frequency-response distribution exhibit similar overall trends, both showing 
a pronounced extremum near 20 MHz. Considering the effects of parasitic parameters 
in the actual circuit, deviations of device parameters from their nominal values, non-
uniform temperature distribution, and the difference between the temperature recorded 
by the external temperature probe and the actual junction temperature of the devices, 
the circuit-level model cannot be expected to reproduce the experimental curve point 
by point over the entire frequency range. Therefore, this work mainly selects the

Based on the combined experimental and simulation results, the proposed 
measurement method, which utilizes swept phase-frequency responses and overall

measurements, this method not only provides the temperature-induced phase sensitivity 
at specific frequencies, but also enables the identification of the distribution 
characteristics of the TCPS with frequency, sensitive frequency bands, and extremum 
locations. For MHz-level heterodyne optoelectronic readout links, such frequency-
domain information facilitates a more comprehensive assessment of thermally induced 
phase noise and provides guidance for subsequent circuit parameter selection and 
thermal stability design.

In this study, a frequency-domain measurement method for the TCPS based on 
VNA-swept phase-frequency response and overall temperature modulation is proposed. 
In this method, the phase-frequency responses of the photoreceiver front-end under test 
are measured at different steady-state temperature points, and the TCPS curves over the 
target frequency range are obtained by phase differencing between adjacent 
temperature points. Compared with conventional single-frequency measurement 
methods, the proposed method can directly obtain the frequency-continuous 
characterization of the TCPS, thereby providing more complete experimental 
information for evaluating the thermal stability of broadband optoelectronic readout 
chains.
A discrete-transistor-based TIA was used as a representative photoreceiver front-
end, and frequency-domain measurements of the TCPS were carried out over 1-50 MHz. 
The experimental results show that the TCPS of the circuit under test exhibits clear 
frequency dependence and reaches an extremum near 20 MHz. The measured 
extremum frequency is 19.8 ± 0.2 MHz, and the corresponding extremum magnitude is 
−7.4 ± 0.2 mrad/K, indicating that the proposed method can effectively extract the 
sensitive frequency region and key characteristic parameters of the thermally induced 
phase response of the circuit.
Furthermore, an AC transimpedance transfer-function model of the circuit under 
test was established, and Monte Carlo simulations were performed to analyze the 
influence of parameter uncertainties on the temperature-to-phase-shift coefficient. The 
experimental results show good agreement with the Monte Carlo simulation results in 
terms of the key features of extremum frequency and extremum magnitude, indicating 
that the established model can provide a reasonable explanation for the experimentally 
observed frequency-dependent behavior and extremum region. The proposed method 
can provide a reference for thermally induced phase-drift evaluation, phase-noise 
budget analysis, and thermal-stability design of photoreceiver front-ends in high-
precision laser interferometry, optoelectronic readout chains, and broadband 
heterodyne detection systems.
