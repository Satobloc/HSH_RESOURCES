Design and Experimental Demonstration of a Conduction 
Cooling System for an S-band High-gradient Cryogenic Copper 
Accelerator Cavity
Min Yang 1, Quantang Zhao 1,2,*, Peng Yu 1,2, Ruikang Ma 1,3, Chihao Ni 1,2, Xiaoxiao Yuan 1, Jiao Xu 
1,2, Hongwei Zhao 1,2
1 Institute of Modern Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Lanzhou 730000, China
2 School of Nuclear Sciences, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing 100049, China
3 School of Physics, Xi’an Jiaotong University, Xi’an 710049, China
4 School of Nuclear Science and Technology, Lanzhou University, Lanzhou 730000, China
Abstract: 
Cryogenic operation of high-gradient radiofrequency (RF) linear accelerators enables higher 
acceleration gradients and lower breakdown rates, which are critical for compact hadron 
therapy systems. This study presents a conduction-cooled cryogenic system operating at 77 K 
for an S-band high-gradient copper traveling-wave accelerator dedicated to ion beam 
acceleration. The system integrates a custom vacuum cryostat with a Gifford-McMahon cold 
head providing 250 W cooling capacity at 77 K. To ensure efficient heat transfer, an optimized 
multi-link design was implemented to minimize thermal contact resistance, supported by 
detailed thermal analyses. Flexible bellows accommodate cryogenic contraction and cavity 
alignment, while heaters maintain temperature stability at 77 ± 1 K. Experimental validations, 
including cooldown processes, steady-state regulation, and high-power RF testing up to 10 MW, 
confirmed stable 77 K operation with ± 1 K control accuracy under dynamic RF power loading.

1. Introduction 
Hadron therapy provides precise dose delivery in cancer treatment, but its clinical 
application is limited by the large size and high cost of conventional accelerator facilities  [1], 
Compact linear accelerators (linacs) are preferred to reduce the facility scale and cost [2]. To 
further shrink the linac size, the development of high-gradient normal-conducting copper 
accelerating structures is required. Among various frequency bands, S-band 
technology remains the most mature route to achieve the necessary acceleration gradients for 
therapeutic linacs. 
Most studies on S-band high-gradient copper accelerators focus on room-temperature 
operation, including geometric design and optimization [2], novel fabrication techniques [4] and 
high-power tests [5]. Although cryogenic performance of S-band structures has been 
investigated for electron beams [6], cryogenic systems at X- and C-bands have also been 
developed for compact X-ray free electron lasers [7], Colliders [8] and RF photoguns [10] ; 
however, these are almost exclusively applied to electron beam. To the best of our knowledge, 
no cryogenic system operating at 77 K has been engineered for ion beams. Cooling oxygen-
free high conductivity (OFHC) copper to cryogenic temperatures decreases surface resistance. 
This increases the cavity shunt impedance (ZTT) and intrinsic quality factor (Q 0) [11], and 
reduces RF power loss [13]. Low temperatures also increase copper hardness, which reduces

surface deformation and raises the RF breakdown threshold to allow higher accelerating 
gradients [11]. Motivated by these physical effects, our group previously reported on the design, 
fabrication, and tuning of a cryogenic high-gradient traveling-wave cavity for a compact 
carbon-ion therapy accelerator [15]. However, bridging the gap between a standalone cavity and 
a fully integrated cryogenic module requires addressing critical engineering challenges, 
particularly regarding thermal stability under dynamic RF heating and structural integrity 
during cool down. Based on a comprehensive literature survey, no conduction-cooled cryogenic 
system operating at 77 K has been reported for an S-band copper traveling-wave accelerator 
dedicated to ion beam acceleration. 
Two methods are typically used to cool high-gradient copper structures: liquid nitrogen (LN) 
immersion and cryocooler-based conduction cooling. LN immersion [11] provides good cooling 
rates and temperature uniformity, but it requires large dewars, transfer lines, phase separators, 
and complex safety systems, which are not practical for hospitals. Conduction cooling [16] is 
simpler and more compact. However, previous conduction cooling designs were mostly built 
for small test cavities. They cannot handle the dynamic heat load or provide the temperature 
stability needed for a full-scale S-band traveling-wave accelerator module. In copper cavities, 
even minor temperature fluctuations cause thermal expansion or contraction, leading to RF 
resonant frequency detuning and a drop in acceleration performance. To address these 
challenges, we developed a full-scale conduction-cooled cryogenic system for the accelerator.
In this work, we designed and fabricated an S-band high-gradient traveling-wave accelerator 
using conduction cooling [17], and performed its first high-power RF test to verify the cooling 
scheme. Based on these efforts, this paper presents the development and validation of a 
cryogenic system, which maintains the accelerator cavity at 77 ± 1 K. Section 2 describes the 
cryogenic design, including temperature control, sensor placement, and sealing at the cavity-
cryostat interfaces. Sections 3 and 4 present the thermal and mechanical analysis. Section 5

2. Overview of the Accelerator parameters and Cryogenic system
The S-band high-gradient traveling-wave accelerator cavity [15] described in this paper is a 
negative-harmonic structure operating at 2856 MHz, designed to achieve 60 MV/m at 77 K 
with 8.2 MW RF input power. Under an RF input power of 10 MW (2 𝜇s pulse width, 5 Hz 
repetition rate; hereafter 2 𝜇s, 5 Hz), the cavity dissipates 58.6W average heat load. Following 
fabrication and tuning to meet the target electric field distribution, a dedicated conduction-
cooling cryogenic system was developed to maintain the cavity at 77 ± 1 K under both no-load 
and high-power condition.
Although the conduction cooling layout draws on established practices from 
superconducting RF cavities systems [18], the present system operates at significantly higher 
temperatures (77 K vs. 4.2 K) and must accommodate distinct dynamic heat loads characteristic 
of normal-conducting copper structures. Accordingly, all mechanical components were 
redesigned for the specific thermal and operational requirements of this S-band accelerator. Fig. 
1 shows a three-quarter cross-sectional view of cryogenic system, which was comprised of five 
functional subsystems: accelerator cavity, conducting cooling modules, heating modules, two 
independent vacuum systems, and a temperature regulation system. The primary design 
objective was to maintain stable 77 ± 1 K operating via cryocooler-based conduction cooling. 
Flexible thermal links played a critical role in the heat transfer process; their design was 
required to achieve high thermal conductance, convenient assembly and effective vibration 
isolation.

The cavity and cryogenic chamber were equipped with independent vacuum systems to 
satisfy their respective vacuum requirements. The cavity featured two dedicated RF ports (input 
and output waveguides) and two beam ports (inlet and outlet). To ensure system reliability, 
these ports extended externally from the cryogenic chamber to the outer wave guide system and 
Faraday cup. Maintaining the cavity integrity within a vacuum design target of < 9×10 -7 Pa, 
rendered the sealing design of the chamber-accelerator joints a significant technical challenge. 
To address this, metal-sealed CF flanges were adopted for static interfaces, while edge-welding 
bellows were integrated at dynamic joints to compensate for differential thermal contraction 
during cooldown.
Cavity temperature regulation was achieved through a dynamic balance between active 
cooling (provided by the cryocooler cold head) and active heating (via cartridge heaters). 
Temperature sensors were strategically deployed to monitor spatial temperature distributions at 
critical locations during cooldown and steady-state operation. In addition to the aforementioned

3. Thermal analysis 
To verify thermal performance, two distinct thermal link design schemes were proposed and 
comparatively analyzed (Fig. 2). The cavity was precision-machined from oxygen-free high-
conductivity copper (OFHC Cu; Residual Resistivity Ratio ≥ 300). Configuration (a) adopted 
a single-link (OFHC Cu) scheme with indirect mechanical coupling：four 1 mm-thick semi-
cylindrical shells were tightly wrapped around the cavity outer surface, and two 1-mm-thick 
long plates were bolted to connect the shells to the end bar of the thermal link. This link 
consisted of a 5-mm-thick circle plate, and multiple braid straps with the end bar. The circle 
plate was rigidly bolted to the cold head baseplate, while the straps end bar was fastened to the 
long plates via bolted joints. In contrast, configuration (b) employed a distributed multi-link 
scheme to evaluate the effect of path multiplicity on heat transfer efficiency. A thermal bar (16 
mm ×  15 mm cross-section) was integrally brazed to the top of the cavity. This configuration 
was derived by splitting the overall conductive path of configuration (a) into multiple parallel 
segments for comparison. While the simulation model of configuration (b) assumed three 
idealized braid straps per segment, the as-built assembly was upgraded to four segments, each 
incorporating a double-layer fine copper wire braid architecture. This modification was 
implemented to provide additional margin against manufacturing tolerances and to achieve 
higher effective conductance within the same envelope. Consequently, the effective conduction 
path length and contact resistance per segment in the final application differs from those in the 
simulation model; specific correlation details are provided in the as-built configuration section 
(Fig. 8). Each segment was individually bolted to discrete positions on the thermal bar.

Based on these idealized models, thermal performance was evaluated for both configurations 
using the design models described above, accounting for natural convection on the ambient-
exposed outer wall and radiative heat load on the vacuum-facing inner wall of the chamber. 
Material properties for OFHC Cu and 304 stainless steel were obtained from NIST open data 
[20]. In the single-link design (Figs. 2(a) and 3(a)), maintaining the cavity at 77 K required a net 
cooling capacity of 36.0 W at the cold head. Under this load, the copper plate top temperature 
stabilized at 53 K, resulting in a 24 K temperature drop across the link. This large gradient arose 
from the high cumulative thermal resistance of the concentrated heat transfer path, which 
necessitates a substantial driving temperature difference to transport the full cavity heat load to 
the cold head.

only 23.2 W of net cooling power to maintain the same 77 K cavity temperature. The copper 
plate top stabilized at 64 K, reducing the link temperature drop to 13 K. By distributing the 
thermal load across multiple parallel interfaces, this design significantly lowers the effective 
thermal resistance, thereby eliminating unnecessary overcooling of the cold head while reliably 
extracting the intrinsic heat leakage from the cavity.

The assembly was optimized into a compact configuration with overall dimensions of 600 mm 
×  300 mm ×  300 mm. The chamber was fabricated from 316L stainless steel (yield 
strength: 170 MPa) [19] with a 10 mm wall thickness; reinforcing ribs were welded on the front 
and bottom surfaces to enhance stiffness. The applied load comprised atmospheric pressure (1 
atm) and gravitational weight of related components: cold head (18 kg), cavity (13.4 kg), and 
bellows (7 kg). With a safety factor 1.5, the allowable stress and deformation limits were set at 
113 MPa and 1.5 mm, respectively [22]. Finite element analysis results indicated that the 
maximum equivalent stress was 10.4 MPa and the maximum deformation is approximately 0.02 
mm, both satisfying the design criteria with adequate margin.

5. System assembly
The assembly sequence of the cryogenic system is illustrated in Fig. 7. First, the cryogenic 
chamber and its supporting platform were positioned (Fig. 7(a)). The chamber was adjusted 
along the x, y, and z axes - coarsely via the platform and finely adjusted relative to the 
waveguide system - to achieve the alignment within 1 mm. Second, the accelerator cavity and 
custom waveguide were pre-assembled as an integrated subassembly (Fig. 7(b)). Third, the 
cooling-heating subsystem (CHS) - comprising a cold head (KDE300SA, 250W @ 77 K), four 
flexible OFHC braid straps (5 mm ×  45mm cross-section area, which is slightly different 
with former design), three cartridge heaters (HT-100, 100 W each), and bellows assemblies - 
was independently pre-assembled and functionally validated (Figs. 7(c) and Fig. 8). 
Subsequently, the CHS was integrated onto the chamber to isolate vibration (Fig. 7(d)) and its 
flexible straps were connected to the cavity. The cavity was suspended using two loop-slung 
304 stainless steel wire ropes (∅ 2 mm) configured in loops, while four small bellows above the 
chamber enabled fine vertical positioning of the cavity (Fig. 7(e)). Additionally, two slightly

6. Cooling test 
After assembly and leakage testing finished, the integrated system was subsequently 
evacuated by the pumping system. The cavity sealed with copper gaskets achieved a vacuum 
level of 2 × 10 -7 Pa, whereas the cryogenic chamber sealed with elastomer O-rings and copper 
gaskets reached 1.5 × 10 -5 Pa at room temperature; this discrepancy is attributed to the higher 
permeation and outgassing rates of the elastomeric seals. Subsequently, cooldown tests were 
performed. Prior to activating the helium compressor (KDC6000), the water chiller was started 
to pre-cool the circulating water to 15 °C. The high-pressure helium gas (~2.3 MPa) from the

In this test, eight PT100-BO platinum resistance temperature sensors (range: 20-325 K,

This cooling test reduced the cold head temperature from 298 K to ~30 K, and the cavity 
temperature to ~70 K. Throughout this process, the flange temperature was approximately 20 
K higher than that of the cavity, which indicated a good thermal isolation. The chamber inner 
wall temperature decreased by only ~3 K due to natural convection. To stabilize the cavity at 
77 K, three cartridge heaters were deployed: two connected in series (providing up to 200 W 
total with adjustable current), while the third was regulated via the supplier’ s default PID 
Settings. Through manual iterative adjustments based on temperature sensor feedback, an 
optimal control strategy minimizing cooldown time was established:

Under this steady-state test protocol, RF power was stepped from 0 MW to 10 MW (2 𝜇𝑠, 
5 Hz) with dwell times of 30 min at 0 MW and 1 MW, followed by 1 hour at each of 2, 4, 6, 8, 
and 10 MW. During the zero-RF-power baseline period, the measured temperature drop across 
the as-built four-segment thermal link was 8 K, confirming that the fabricated assembly 
provides sufficient intrinsic thermal conductance to meet the design requirement for stable 77 
K operation under the actual experimental conditions. As RF power increased, the cold head 
temperature decreased from 69 K to 47 K, while the cavity temperature remained stable at 77 
± 1 K throughout the entire power sweep. At the maximum power level of 10 MW, the 
accelerating gradient reached 66 MV/m. These results demonstrate effective cavity temperature 
regulation under dynamic RF loading. Furthermore, Fig. 14 shows a clear linear trend between 
RF input power and cold head temperature under stable cavity operation (77 ± 1) K. The 
empirical linear relationship (𝑇𝐶𝐻 =  ― 2.13 ∙ 𝑃𝑅𝐹 +68.35) provides a practical calibration tool: 
operators can anticipate the cold head temperature shift based on RF power settings, enabling 
proactive thermal management without requiring knowledge of absolute heat loads. Compared 
with the simulation baseline established in Fig. 4, the experimental data exhibits a highly 
consistent linear slope, confirming that the thermal load dynamics were accurately predicted. 
Notably, the measured cold head temperatures are consistently higher across the entire power 
range, showing a parallel upward shift of approximately 4.79 K. This offset is attributed to the 
geometric and contact resistance differences between the actual strap used in the assembly and 
the idealized strap modeled in the simulation, which slightly increased the overall thermal 
resistance.

This study established a validated engineering reference for a 77 K conduction-cooled 
cryogenic system designed for an S-band high-gradient copper accelerator cavity, specially 
tailored for compact ion accelerators. While prior works have explored cryogenic cavity 
structures in isolation, the present contribution lies in the integrated system-level validation of 
a cold head, a multi-link configuration, heaters, bellows and ropes. Thermal simulations 
evaluated heat transfer efficiency during cooldown from ambient to 77 K under no-load 
conditions for two candidate configurations, based on which the preferred design was selected 
for subsequent RF-powered thermal analysis. Parallel structural analysis informed design 
refinements to mitigate thermally induced deformation during this cooling phase. Guided by 
these design and simulation results, precision assembly procedures were implemented to 
minimize interfacial thermal resistance and ensure mechanical alignment. Subsequent cooling 
tests validated the simulated cooldown performance and demonstrated stable cavity 
temperature regulation at 77 ± 1 K under RF loads up to 10 MW (66 MV/m). Collectively, this 
integrated workflow establishes a verified engineering basis for conduction-cooled high-
gradient copper accelerator cavity, enabling industrial-scale development. Future efforts will 
focus on: (1) assessing long-term reliability under sustained high-power cycling; and (2) 
refining autonomous temperature regulation strategies for unattended operation. These 
developments aim to bridge the gap between laboratory validation and clinical translation.
