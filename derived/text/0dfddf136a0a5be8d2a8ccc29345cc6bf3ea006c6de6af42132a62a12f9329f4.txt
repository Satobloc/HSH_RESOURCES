Simulating large-scale free-surface flow by coupling a localized 3D fluid
solver to a cheaper 2D surface model has long faced a mismatch in wave
dynamics: efficient 2D wave models used in graphics are typically either
linear or non-dispersive. These models are fast, simple, and accurate for calm,
small-amplitude seas, but coupling them to strongly nonlinear 3D solvers
produces visible reflections and artifacts at the 2D–3D interface. We address
this problem by introducing a nonlinear and dispersive 2D wave model
based on the canonical Zakharov formulation. Its Hamiltonian structure
enables canonically consistent two-way coupling, allowing information to

1 Introduction
Simulating large-scale free-surface flow remains a long-standing
computational bottleneck in graphics, whether the scene is a sea-
plane splashing down on the open ocean, a battleship plowing
through heavy seas, or a submarine breaking the ocean surface.

A fully three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes (NS) simu-
lation captures every regime of interest, including splashes, breaking
waves, and fluid–solid coupling, but its memory and compute costs
grow prohibitive once the domain spans hundreds of meters. A
practical remedy is to confine the expensive 3D solver to a small
embedding region and model the surrounding open ocean with a
much cheaper 2D surface theory [Chentanez et al. 2015; Huang et al.
2021; Schreck and Wojtan 2022; Stomakhin and Selle 2017; Thürey
et al. 2006] in order to propagate far-field waves efficiently. The
success of this 2D–3D decomposition rests on the quality of the 2D
model and the fidelity of the coupling between the two solvers.
Within this 2D–3D decomposition, a key obstacle to practical,
high-fidelity coupling is that graphics currently lacks an efficient
2D wave model that is simultaneouslynonlinear and dispersive:
none retains the wave–wave interactions whose strength grows
with wave steepness (nonlinearity) while allowing waves of differ-
ent wavelengths to travel at their correct, wavelength-dependent
speeds (dispersion). The two wave theories that dominate the litera-
ture both bypass the nonlinear surface dynamics. Airy wave theory
[Airy 1845; Canabal et al. 2016; Schreck et al. 2019; Tessendorf 2014]
linearizes the free-surface boundary conditions around the flat state,
retaining the correct dispersion 𝜔 2 =𝑔𝑘tanh (𝑘ℎ) but discarding
every nonlinear interaction that gives rise to scenarios like the steep-
ening of Stokes wavetrains. The discarded terms scale with wave
steepness, so for the gentle ambient swell that Airy solvers typically
animate, the linearization costs little and buys great speed and sim-
plicity. The shallow-water equations (SWE) [Chentanez and Müller
2010; Kass and Miller 1990; Layton and van de Panne 2002] carry
nonlinear depth-averaged advection but collapse the dispersion re-

distinguishes a real ocean from a sloshing tank, a sacrifice that is
acceptable in the transport-dominated shallow flows SWE was built
for, but not in open water. Lagrangian alternatives that have gained
traction in graphics, e.g., wave particles [Yuksel et al. 2007], wave
packets [Jeschke and Wojtan 2017], wavefront tracking [Jeschke
and Wojtan 2015], and water-surface wavelets [Jeschke et al. 2018],
all transport their primitives along linear ray paths and inherit the
same linearization. Each of these trade-offs is defensible in its in-
tended regime. Once strongly nonlinear waves dominate, however,
these models lose accuracy, whereas our method makes the oppo-
site trade-off, sacrificing a little speed and stability in exchange for
greater nonlinear accuracy and coupling fidelity.
Coupling a localized 3D NS solver to such a linear 2D wave
model creates precisely such a strongly nonlinear setting. The 3D
solver is strongly nonlinear by construction, so waves reaching the
embedding-box boundary exhibit nonlinear effects such as crest
steepening and amplitude-dependent dispersion. A linear 2D model
cannot represent any of these, so the two solvers disagree on the
very content of the surface state at the interface. The disagreement
shows up as spurious reflections at the seam and visible surface
artifacts. We therefore identify the absence of a nonlinear and dis-
persive 2D wave model in graphics as a key obstacle to a 2D–3D
coupling pipeline that is at once practical, accurate, and largely free
of interface artifacts. The one prior nonlinear route, the boundary-
element method (BEM) [Huang et al. 2021], recovers the nonlinear
free-surface potential flow at the PDE level, but discretizes it on
a Lagrangian surface mesh, and pays for its nonlinear accuracy
with per-step surface tracking/remeshing, denseO(𝑁 3) boundary
interactions, and high implementation complexity.
We address this limitation by introducing a 2D Eulerian wave
model that is at oncenonlinearanddispersive, and we show that
its underlyingHamiltonianstructure is what makes the two-way
coupling to a 3D NS solver natural. The wave model evolves the
canonical Zakharov pair [Zakharov 1968]: the surface elevation𝜂
and the surface value𝜓 of the velocity potential. HereHamiltonian
means that(𝜂,𝜓) form a canonical pair in the sense of classical
mechanics: they evolve like position and momentum under the total
wave energy, the Hamiltonian (Section 3). We advance the Zakharov
system using the Craig–Sulem expansion of the Dirichlet–Neumann
operator (DNO)𝐺(𝜂) [Craig and Sulem 1993]. This reduces every
nonlinear correction to FFTs and pointwise products on a regu-
lar grid atO(𝑁 2 log𝑁) cost. In practice, we retain this expansion
through second or third order (Section 3). The same DNO machinery
enables two-way coupling: it lifts(𝜂,𝜓) to depth-resolved boundary
velocities for the 3D solver and, in reverse, reconstitutes a canonical
wave state after relaxing𝜂toward the 3D surface.
On standalone 2D wave-propagation benchmarks, our wave model
alone lowers the mean wave-height error by1.7× (deep water) to
2.4× (shallow water) over the strongest linear baseline (Airy with
dispersion kernels) and by3to5 × over SWE and BEM, while run-
ning more than10 3× faster than BEM; the price of this accuracy is a
modestly more expensive and slightly less stable wave step than the
linear baselines, a few milliseconds per substep in absolute terms
(Table 3). Once coupled to the 3D NS solver, a dispersion-matching
test in which a wave packet radiates from the 3D box across the seam
and a single-boat experiment in which a hull radiates a Kelvin wake
through the seam together show that our coupled pipeline yields
symmetric wave fields with minimal seam artifacts, whereas prior
coupling schemes exhibit varying degrees of asymmetric propaga-
tion, spurious reflection, or visible 2D–3D inconsistency (Section 8);
on the single-boat scene, our solver also runs more than4× faster
than a GPU NB-FLIP simulation of the same domain, i.e., the bound-
ing box of the entire visible water region. We further run a suite of
large-scale scenarios, a battleship in heavy seas, a surfacing subma-
rine, two chasing boats, and a seaplane landing on the open ocean, in
which the 2D–3D interface remains largely free of visible artifacts.

2 Related Work
2.1 2D simulation
SWE.The SWE, a simplified form of the Navier–Stokes equations,
originated in engineering physics [Crapper 1984; Le Méhauté 2013;
Stoker 2019] and were first introduced to computer graphics by Kass
and Miller [1990]. Layton and van de Panne [2002] introduced an
implicit semi-Lagrangian scheme to improve stability and support
larger time steps. Chentanez and Müller [2010] extended SWE to
handle arbitrary slopes and depths. Later works enhanced SWE by
adding surface tension [Wang et al. 2007], reducing numerical dissi-
pation while ensuring stability via implicit Newmark integration
[Angst et al. 2008], and improving efficiency using a collocated grid
and a simplified SWE formulation [Lee and O’Sullivan 2007].
Airy Wave Theory.Airy wave theory [Airy 1845; Birkhoff 2015;
Dean and Dalrymple 1991], popularized in computer graphics by
Tessendorf [2001], synthesizes ocean height fields by evolving wave
spectra according to dispersion relations. Some implementations
augment linearly evolved waves with geometric displacement or
post-sharpening [Stomakhin and Selle 2017; Tessendorf 2001]; we
distinguish this geometric nonlinearity from the dynamical non-
linearity in our wave evolution. Horvath [2015] further improved
spectral realism using an advanced empirical model, and proposed
an artist-friendly parameterization scheme to facilitate intuitive con-
trol over ocean wave elongation. Beyond Tessendorf’s formulation,
Stam [2001] proposed a compact FFT-based fluid solver widely used
in 2D surface simulations. Nielsen et al. [2013] fit artist-created wave
fields to spectral parameters for consistent high-frequency synthesis.
Later, Tessendorf [2014] presented theeWavemodel, which provides
a time integration in both Fourier and convolution forms, enabling
more flexible boundary treatments. Dispersion-kernel approaches
[Canabal et al. 2016] handle solid boundaries via convolution and
shadow masks. More recently, Schreck et al. [2019] introduced a fun-
damental solution-based method using Green’s functions to capture
wave–obstacle interactions in open domains without grids.
High-Order Spectral Method.Starting from the canonical Hamil-
tonian formulation of Zakharov [1968], the high-order spectral
(HOS) method was independently introduced by Dommermuth and
Yue [1987] and West et al. [1987] to evolve nonlinear surface waves
on a flat reference grid via a Taylor expansion of the Dirichlet–
Neumann operator, with Craig and Sulem [1993] casting the expan-
sion in the explicit operator form used in modern HOS solvers. Such
interior-to-boundary reductions via Dirichlet–Neumann (Steklov–
Poincaré) operators also appear in graphics, including extrinsic
shape analysis [Wang et al . 2018], boundary-first conformal flat-
tening [Sawhney and Crane 2017], and boundary-only physics-
based skinning [Gao et al. 2014]. Subsequent work extended HOS
to directional sea states [Bateman et al. 2001], variable bathymetry
[Guyenne and Nicholls 2008], and large-scale open-ocean simula-
tion [Ducrozet et al . 2016]. We adopt the HOS expansion as the
engine of our 2D wave solver.
Other Wave Simulation.A variety of approaches have explored
alternative physical representations to better capture dispersion,
boundary interactions, and visual detail. These include wave parti-
cles [Yuksel et al. 2007], wave packets [Jeschke and Wojtan 2017],
wavefront tracking [Jeschke and Wojtan 2015], and water surface
wavelets [Jeschke et al . 2018]. Hybrid models combine different
regimes, such as SWE with Airy theory [Jeschke and Wojtan 2023],
or augment height fields with particles for breaking waves [Thurey
et al. 2007]. Others enhance detail as a post-process, like wave curves
on deforming surfaces [Skrivan et al. 2020].
2.2 3D simulation
Early works in 3D fluid simulation for graphics [Foster and Fed-
kiw 2001; Foster and Metaxas 1996; Stam 1999] primarily adopt
Eulerian frameworks, but often suffer from numerical dissipation.
Pure Lagrangian approaches, such as smoothed particle hydrody-
namics (SPH) [Ihmsen et al. 2014; Müller et al. 2003], require costly
neighbor searches and struggle to enforce incompressibility. Hybrid
Lagrangian-Eulerian methods such as Particle-in-Cell (PIC) [Harlow
1964] alleviate some issues but still exhibit dissipation. To address
this, the Fluid-Implicit Particle (FLIP) method was introduced to
graphics by Zhu and Bridson [2005], building on Brackbill et al .
[1988], significantly reducing dissipation. To improve efficiency,
Narrow Band FLIP (NB-FLIP) [Ferstl et al. 2016] and extended NB-
FLIP [Sato et al. 2018] were later proposed. Furthermore, several
approaches [Ando and Batty 2020; Losasso et al. 2006, 2004; Nielsen
and Bridson 2016; Wang et al. 2025a; Zhu et al. 2013] have employed
adaptive techniques to resolve fine-scale fluid features while main-
taining computational efficiency for large-scale scenes. Recently,
flow-map-based methods [Chen et al. 2025a, 2024, 2025b; Deng et al.
2023; He et al. 2026; Li et al. 2024, 2026, 2025a,b; Nabizadeh et al .
2022; Sun et al. 2024, 2025; Wang et al. 2026, 2024, 2025b; Zhou et al.
2024] and the Coadjoint Orbit FLIP [Nabizadeh et al . 2024] have
reduced numerical dissipation and better preserved vortices.
2.3 Coupled 2D and 3D simulation
Thürey et al. [2006] coupled shallow water with a 3D solver us-
ing the Lattice Boltzmann method. Nielsen and Bridson [2011] im-
proved art-directability by introducing guide shapes to constrain
high-resolution liquid simulations to low-resolution inputs. Chen-
tanez et al. [2015] combined SWE, SPH, and a 3D Eulerian solver,
enabling efficient simulation but lacking accurate wave dispersion.
Huang et al. [2021] addressed this by coupling BEM [Da et al. 2016;
Huang and Michels 2020] with FLIP for more realistic ocean–ship
interaction, though BEM’s mesh operations are computationally
expensive and limit scalability. The fluxed animated boundary (FAB)
method [Stomakhin and Selle 2017] allows artistic control but only
supports one-way coupling (the 3D simulation cannot affect the

3.3 Boundary conditions via even extension
The FFT machinery above is intrinsically periodic; closed boundaries
(a tank wall, a coastline, the perimeter of a pond) require additional
care. We support reflecting and replicating boundaries through an
even extension of the wave domain. Given the physical state(𝜂,𝜓)
on a rectangle of resolution(𝑀,𝑁), we form

the FFT cost increases only by a constant factor of four, and the
boundary treatment carries no asymptotic overhead.
