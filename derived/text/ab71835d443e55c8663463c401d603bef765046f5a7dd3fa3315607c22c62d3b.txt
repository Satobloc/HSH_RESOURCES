Belonging in a discipline or department is an important aspect of people’s
persistence in that discipline or department, especially for undergraduate
students. Previous research has shown that increasing student feelings of
belonging in a department beneﬁts all students and is even more valuable
for women, racialized people, and other underrepresented groups.
However , helping students to develop feelings of belonging can be di!cult
and complex. This article presents data from a larger , primarily qualitative
case study that was conducted at a research-based university in Canada. It
examines how a group of faculty, sta", and students worked toward
building community and cultivating belonging in their physics
department. The aspects of this process that the professor and student
participants experienced, and the challenges and rewards encountered,
are described and explained here. Some of the strategies that professor
participants undertook were relatively easily accomplished using what we
describe here as a “classical” paradigm; the analogy is to the traditional
physics culture. One example of a strategy employed under the classical
paradigm is hosting a mentoring program for physics majors. Other
strategies were quite challenging for many professor participants and
required a paradigm shift toward more progressive views, which, following
the analogy, we call a “quantum” paradigm. An example is believing and
behaving as though students with B grades belong and can succeed in
physics. We suggest that the paradigm shift from classical (traditional
thinking) to quantum (progressive) is an important barrier that members
of the physics community must recognize and overcome to fully address
student participation and experiences in physics, although some changes
were still possible without making the paradigm shift. The study found a
new di"erence between feeling welcome and feeling a sense of belonging
among students, as well as a similar di"erence among faculty members.
We present possible steps that physics faculty and sta" may consider
along the road to recruitment and retention in physics, which are notable
for departments and instructors interested in improving student
outcomes, departmental sustainability, and equity, diversity, and inclusion.

I. INTRODUCTION
Belonging means the degree to which one feels valued, secure, and supported
within a group [1–3]. Improving student feelings of belonging in the physics
community can have many positive e"ects, such as higher grades [4], greater
persistence in degree programs [5–8], greater self-e!cacy [9], and improved
physical and psychological well-being [1]. By cultivating belonging, we aim to
include everyone who would like to participate in the physics community, which
would build diversity and long-term sustainability in our discipline. This type of
change has long eluded us in physics, while similar disciplines have made
substantial recent improvements [10–13]. Reasons for our lack of progress
include that many physics faculty may not know how they can increase students’
sense of belonging [9,14], they may believe that they do not inﬂuence student
persistence at all [7], or they may not be comfortable implementing strategies
presented in the literature without support [15,16].
This paper presents results from part of a larger case study [17] conducted in
one university physics department in Canada where a small group of faculty and
sta" attempted to increase undergraduate persistence, diversity, and
engagement. The focus of this work is on how both faculty and student
participants described their thoughts and experiences related to who belongs
and how belonging can be achieved in a physics department.
Participants in this study included faculty, sta", and students in a physics
department, and the intent was to investigate and improve upon student
feelings of belonging as a means of improving retention in the department’s
undergraduate programs. Interestingly, however , some ﬁndings emerged about
the faculty and sta" perceptions of their own belonging in physics. Therefore,
the results include discussions of belonging that relate to both student and
professor experiences.
The study focused on how student success and belonging could be improved
upon at the departmental level because departments are an appropriate unit for
attempting such changes [15]. Course-level success and belonging are also
valuable and were encouraged as part of this work, but they were secondary to
the overall goal of the project, which was to increase student retention, diversity,
and engagement in the department.
II. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
Much recent research in physics education has emphasized the importance of
building a sense of belonging in physics communities [4,13,18–20] along with
bolstering people’s physics identities [3,9,21]. These two concepts are strongly
interrelated, and both exist precariously within a messy, complex ecosystem [22]
that remains harsh, especially for women [23–24], racialized people [25–27], and
other underrepresented groups [28]. Members of these groups often leave or
avoid physics because they do not feel they belong in the ﬁeld, for a variety of

A. Physics identity, self-e!cacy, and belonging
Physics identity has been described as the feeling of being a “physics person”
[29]. Persistence in physics graduate programs and careers is a"ected by physics
identity [5,29], and gender and race gaps in physics identity have existed for
many years [27,29]. There are many inﬂuences on physics identity, including self-
e!cacy and recognition by peers and authority ﬁgures [29]. People’s physics
identities are constructed over many years, starting long before they arrive on
university campuses. This creates a daunting challenge for anyone hoping to
improve student feelings of belonging in physics: it involves changing people’s
deeply held beliefs about themselves. Improving students’ sense of belonging
has been shown to positively a"ect both the persistent gender gap [1,9,30,31]
and the racial imbalance [25,26,32,33] in physics.
1. Faculty e"ects on belonging
University physics faculty can greatly inﬂuence physics identity through implicit
and explicit recognition of students as capable of success in physics [3,4,12],
although they may not realize that they hold this power [7]. Recognition of
student e"orts and abilities [12,21,34] and creating supportive learning
environments [9,19] are the two most important methods by which physics
professors, instructors, and teaching assistants can build student physics
identities and belonging. But crucially, both of these methods require an
underlying belief that, as educators, we can and do inﬂuence student
experiences of physics.
Faculty beliefs about teaching and supportive departmental cultures are known
to have important e"ects on student belonging [7,16,35,36]. Unfortunately, the
traditional physics culture implicitly discourages student belonging and
supportive teaching.
B. The classical paradigm
Introductory physics courses have long been known as “gatekeeper” courses [37]
; that is, they are designed and carried out with the goal to “weed out” large
numbers of students [38], in what most physics community members incorrectly
believe is a meritocratic system [39,23]. This makes sense in a culture of elitism.
The underlying belief that physics is only for the best and brightest students
aligns with the practice that most introductory physics courses are still being
taught traditionally, using lectures as the sole type of instruction [16,39,40] and
assuming that those who are talented enough will succeed without support.
Importantly, the stereotype that physics is only for “brilliant” people is widely
held in society, not only by physicists [10,11]. Combined with the societal
association of brilliance with maleness and whiteness [11,41], this creates a large
and unspoken barrier to broadening participation in physics.

The classical paradigm is the term we use in this study for the set of beliefs about
how traditionally taught courses are necessary in physics to maintain the ﬁeld’s
elite status. This implies restricting progress in physics courses to only the top
tier of students. The classical paradigm is based on the assumption that using
traditional teaching methods ensures that only the most capable students will be
allowed to persist in physics and that those who discover that they are capable
will naturally choose to remain without further action from anyone in the physics
community. By deﬁnition, then, the classical paradigm creates an elitist culture
and contributes greatly to low enrolment.
C. The quantum paradigm
In her 1990 book [42], Sheila Tobias investigated elitism in the academic cultures
of physics and chemistry. She wrote,
We need to enlarge what has hitherto been considered the natural pool of
recruits to science and be willing to o"er new kinds of students a welcome and a
chance for success. … We are obliged to think and think hard not just about who
does science and why, but who doesn’t do science, and why not. To this end, we
should examine, as a beginning construct, the student on the ‘second tier’.
The term “second tier” refers to students who are capable and interested in
physics and chemistry but were pushed out of the ﬁelds during their
undergraduate years due to elitist cultures where only “top tier” students were
welcomed. This excludes many students who might contribute greatly to the
ﬁeld but whose grades fall just short of the highest tier , or who do not consider
themselves brilliant. Many of these students identify as women and/or as part of
other underrepresented groups [10–12].
In undergraduate physics, students learn and adopt a shift in how the universe
functions when they advance from classical physics to quantum physics. This is
usually di!cult, but it results in a wider and more accurate perspective of how
the universe works. Similarly, we argue in this paper that some physics
educators undergo a shift in pedagogical understanding from the classical
paradigm described above to the quantum paradigm, which is a set of beliefs
about how inclusive teaching and a culture of belonging can be used in physics
to improve outcomes for the majority of students while still ensuring that they
learn the same material and are appropriately challenged. The quantum
paradigm requires a belief that many, if not all, students can succeed in physics,
and that, to do so, they need and deserve our support.
Many previous scholars have pointed out that elitism and meritocracy are the
source of the physics enrolment and diversity problems [23,43–45]. Some have
discussed this as a cultural problem [19,31,42,43,45,46]. Here, we build on these
understandings to discuss the problem as a thought experiment: the need to
shift from the classical paradigm, including its elitism and the resulting low
enrollments, to the quantum paradigm, based on promoting belonging and
broader participation.

D. Welcoming vs belonging
Belonging is well deﬁned in the literature as a sense that one’s presence is
valued and that one is safe and secure [1–3]. Welcoming has not yet been as
clearly deﬁned and is only mentioned tangentially in the literature as of this
writing. The meaning of “welcome” in this paper is “to greet hospitably and with
courtesy or cordiality” or “to accept with pleasure the occurrence or presence of”
[47]. Therefore, welcoming people into a community is an initial step toward
helping people feel that they belong. That is,
welcoming is on the continuum towards belonging. … You can only belong
through other human beings. … People who only feel welcomed leave, and
people who belong stay [48].
The distinction between welcome and belonging is new to the physics education
literature. For the purposes of this research, we assume that the two terms are
interpreted by participants and readers according to their commonly understood
deﬁnitions as outlined above.
Much research is extant on physics students’ identities and sense of belonging,
but the same is not true for physics professors. The only information currently
available for professors is about how they can build their students’ physics
identities. No references were found to professors’ own physics identities or
sense of belonging during the literature search for this study.
E. Change theories
1. Professional learning communities
Contextualized research on how exactly a physics department should go about
increasing belonging has been missing from the literature [15,20,49,50].
However , previous authors have stated that, to reduce participation gaps and
improve outcomes for all students, physics instructors should engage in ongoing
professional development about inclusive instructional strategies [4,9,20]. One
way to deliver these types of change for professors is through professional
learning communities [51], which o"er collegial encouragement in addition to
pedagogical strategies [52]. Such groups o"er a venue for professors to examine
their beliefs about teaching and learning, to consider how these beliefs a"ect
and are a"ected by their interactions with students, and to support each other .
This paper describes part of a larger case study [17], focusing on the work that a
small group in a certain physics department, which we here call The Committee,
did to build community and increase student feelings of belonging. The research
question for this paper is,
How did members of the physics department describe their experiences and
perspectives on The Committee’s work to increase student retention, diversity,
and engagement?

2. First- vs second-order change
Substantial literature has been published in physics education calling for cultural
change, without widespread success to date [19,31,42,43,45]. One explanation
that has been o"ered is based on the concept of ﬁrst-order versus second-order
change [15,53].
First-order changes are surface level; they leave underlying social norms and
belief systems unquestioned. An example is adding demonstration experiments
to a class to illustrate concepts and generate interest. This can work well toward
its intended goal, but it does not change the teacher-centered learning
environment or require students to learn actively.
Second-order changes are much more challenging because they involve
fundamental changes to the status quo. In the physics culture, believing that all
students can succeed and that student-centered learning is valuable is a second-
order change because elitism and lecture-only teaching are central to the
traditional physics culture. Second-order changes require long-term
commitment and expert support of the type that can be o"ered through
ongoing professional development for faculty and sta" [20,49] or via
professional learning communities [15,50,54].
In this research, we examine the shift from the classical paradigm to the
quantum paradigm in a physics department. This involves a shift in thinking
about the types of changes that are required to improve belonging and retention
in physics. Within the classical paradigm, ﬁrst-order changes are su!cient to
achieve these goals, while the quantum paradigm requires deeper , second-order
changes that promote inclusive teaching and a supportive environment.
F. Qualitative case study methodology
This research is grounded in a primarily qualitative case study methodology for
two reasons. First, taking steps to build student physics identities and feelings of
belonging in most physics departments constitutes a signiﬁcant cultural shift
because it means moving away from traditional concepts such as meritocracy
and lecture-only teaching, which are still prevalent [9,16,38]. Cultural change,
paradigm shifts, physics identity, and belonging are deeply human, context-
dependent experiences that may be better understood using qualitative
research methods. Second, research [15,20,54] has pointed to the importance of
university departments, rather than whole institutions, as the key unit for
improving student success. A university physics department, or a small group
within such a department, is an appropriate choice for an in-depth, instrumental
case study [55].

C. Data saturation
During the research, several of the professor and student participants inquired
as to whether su!cient data were being gathered for the purposes of the study.
When they learned that twelve professors and hundreds of students had
participated, and that over 35 h of interviews had been conducted, they
universally expressed that this was more than adequate to create a picture of
their department. This perspective aligned with my own, since I (L. S. C.) had
continued to recruit participants until I assessed that data saturation [57] was
likely. The professor participants had between 0 and 25 years of teaching
experience, some were tenured and permanent, and some were sessional.
Between them, they taught almost every physics student in the university. The
student participants had completed between 0 and 12 semesters of
undergraduate courses. Most were in ﬁrst-year physics courses, but many were
in upper-year classes, approximately reﬂecting the population of undergraduate
physics students. The population of student participants was slightly more
heavily weighted toward upper-year students than the actual departmental
population because the number of students in introductory classes was
overwhelming, as it is in most Canadian physics departments.

D. Analysis
The data corpus was primarily qualitative and included over 1000 pages of
transcripts and ﬁeld notes. The analysis followed standards for qualitative
analysis [58] and included the following phases [58]:
1) Familiarization with the data, involving reading, rereading, and noting ideas.
2) Initial (open) coding to determine what is being said in each piece of data and
across the whole data corpus.
3) Axial coding. Searching for themes among the codes, relating and collating
codes. This was done using code frequency counts, code meanings, and the
comparative method [57].
4) Reviewing and relating themes, checking whether the themes accurately
represent the data, creating an initial concept map.
5) Solidifying and deﬁning themes, continuing to ensure the overall story is told
accurately.
6) Reporting the results, choosing representative examples, relating to the
literature and the research questions.
I (L. S. C.) performed the bulk of this iterative analysis; I collaborated with and
was advised by K. M. in phases 4, 5, and 6. In addition, themes were recorded in
the ﬁeld notes as they arose during the research, which is recommended in
qualitative research [49,59], especially during a project of this length.
Throughout the analysis phase, codes and the data corpus were reexamined and
revised as needed.
Every e"ort has been made to ensure the authenticity of the research as
recommended by qualitative research scholars. Authenticity is the qualitative
research parallel to validity in quantitative research. For example, this study’s
data were examined and compared across time periods, participant groups, and
data types (qualitative and quantitative, transcripts and ﬁeld notes, surveys and
interviews) to achieve crystallization of the codes and themes [60]. This was done
while including and carefully considering all data, including the dissenting voices
rather than ignoring outliers [61]. Transferability has been accomplished to the
extent possible via a thick, rich description of the study department [55] using
maximum variation of participants across demographics, and su!cient data
collection [62]. Reﬂexivity and an audit trail [62] provided further opportunities to
ensure the integrity and reliability of the research.

were analyzed to determine whether and how they reinforced or refuted the
qualitative data. Survey participation rates were low, at between ﬁve to ten
percent of enrolled students each semester; however , they were high enough to
obtain minimum conﬁdence intervals of 90% and margins of error less than 10%
for ﬁve out of the six surveys. These participation rates were not a signiﬁcant

V. DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS
The physics department in this case study had many positive features, including
an active interest in improving the retention, diversity, and engagement of
students in their classes, a successful mentoring program, and a high rating
from students in feelings of being welcome. If readers do not believe that their
own departments already possess these attributes, this research suggests
beginning with developing them, as the rest of this study’s results built on the
extant culture of the department. Creating conditions on the left side of Fig. 1
lays the foundation for the development of the ideas further to the right and
increases the likelihood of sustainable change. Participants’ journeys did not
always move linearly from left to right on the ﬁgure, but establishing or
strengthening the classical approaches described in the Results section and
listed in Table V is important for readers hoping to begin or continue moving
into the quantum paradigm, building community and addressing student
retention.

Welcome Belonging
The study data demonstrated a new di"erence between students’ feelings of
welcome and of belonging, with belonging being more di!cult to achieve. This
lack of belonging was worse for female students compared to their male
colleagues. We recommend that interested members of the physics community
consider how they can help all students, but especially women and other
underrepresented students, not only to feel welcome but also to feel that they
belong in physics. This means providing a caring community that believes each
student has something of value to contribute to physics.
Importantly, the sense of not belonging, and its gender divide, extended to
professor and sta" participants in this study. The shared, yet isolating, feeling of
not being a real physicist, among people who would certainly be considered by
the public to be physicists, demonstrates the fragility of physics identities and
the insidiousness of the elitism in physics. The tacit belief that one must follow a
speciﬁc educational and career path, or that one must consistently demonstrate
brilliance without support, to fully belong in the physics community, is a myth
based on a traditional, elitist physics culture that many continue to deny exists
[26,35].
If the sense of belonging in physics is so di!cult to achieve, does this explain
why so few people choose to pursue physics degrees? Until we, as a physics
community, can o"er an environment in which all interested people can feel that
they belong, it is disingenuous and harmful to continue to ask why retention is
so low, and why there are not more women or members of other
underrepresented groups in physics.
Belonging is a critical human need [65], important for the development of strong
physics identities [1,21], which in turn leads to greater persistence in physics.
This is especially true for women, racialized people, and members of other
underrepresented groups [1,28,30,33]. Therefore, improving students’ and
professors’ feelings of belonging in physics may be critical to diversifying and
sustaining the discipline. This study conﬁrms, however , that improving belonging
in physics is complex and di!cult. It requires a fundamental, quantum shift in
how members of the physics community think and act.
The participants in this research found signiﬁcant success in building community
on several levels: among students, among faculty and sta", and across the whole
department. This conﬁrms prior research [6–8,43]. Community building was
relatively easy to implement in this study, at least as far as classical paradigms
would allow, and it should be a priority for any physics department wishing to
increase, maintain, or diversify enrollments. Participants were generally
enthusiastic about encouraging and supporting second- and upper-year
students, and about supporting each other .

Including ﬁrst-year students in the physics community was more controversial,
but neglecting the needs of ﬁrst-year students has been shown to be
detrimental not only to those students but also to departments [66–68]. This
study and others [3,42,69] demonstrate that many students succeed in ﬁrst-
year physics with grades just short of excellent, and they would be interested in
pursuing physics degree programs if they were minimally supported.
Encouraging such students, perhaps through one-on-one conversations, with
messages from faculty, or via community events, is an important area for future
growth. These ideas can be implemented with a nominal investment of time and
resources and may help students consider , perhaps for the ﬁrst time, that they
could belong in physics and do a physics degree.
The idea of supporting and encouraging students was, however , di!cult for
some professor participants to accept. Their reluctance was based on one or
more of the following: (i) the belief that those who are smart enough to succeed
in physics will do so without support, (ii) a gap in their understanding of how to
appropriately o"er that support, or (iii) a belief that professor’s actions did not
a"ect student persistence at all. These ideas are part of a traditional, classical
physics teaching paradigm. The literature is clear that professors profoundly
a"ect student experiences in physics, either positively or negatively, whether
they intend to or not [9,12,19]. Disregarding or minimizing this e"ect is an
example of the traditional physics culture creating an unnecessary barrier to
belonging, sustaining elitism in our ﬁeld. Study participants who did attempt to
encourage and support students had some understanding of the quantum
paradigm and were generally more open to second-order changes. One way that
participants’ traditional beliefs were addressed in this study was through
professional development in a professional learning community, which
additionally provided a source of support and belonging for the professors
themselves.
This research suggests a reason why increasing student enrollment in physics is
di!cult and complex: There is a limit to improving student recruitment and
retention if classical methods such as those listed in Table V are the only
approaches used. Just as in classical versus quantum physics, some problems are
better tackled using a quantum paradigm, which here means a fundamental
change toward more progressive, inclusive ideas of education. This includes
acknowledging that professors’ actions and beliefs critically inﬂuence student
experiences, both positively and negatively. Coming to this understanding is a
second-order , quantum change that requires members of the physics community
to accept that our elitist, classical culture is ﬂawed; it creates barriers that
prevent more people from pursuing physics. The classical physics culture drives
away the same people that we claim to want to attract and keep in physics.
Acknowledging this problem and accepting responsibility for changing it are the
ﬁrst, di!cult steps in the journey to resolving it and making room for everyone
who wants to participate in physics.

This paper represents a part of a larger study; for greater detail and context,

The research was primarily qualitative in nature, which means the results may
not directly transfer into di"erent contexts. The study was conducted according
to best practices in qualitative research [62], including close attention to
authenticity, crystallization from multiple data sources, and an audit trail.
Participation in the research was su!cient to make the assertions in this paper .
Within the bounds of the research ethics permissions obtained for the study, the
number of participants was maximized. Greater participation, however , may have
deepened or broadened the results. Similarly, a longer time frame for data
collection may have expanded and strengthened the study’s conclusions.
The quantitative data from student surveys that we have presented should be
interpreted with caution for several reasons. For example, the survey questions
used have not been validated. However , the wording of the questions was similar
to parts of the validated survey used in Ref. [3]. Further , no inferences were
made in this study using solely quantitative results; these were used only in
combination with the substantial qualitative data.
The data for this study were collected from September 2018 to December 2019,
just prior to the global COVID-19 pandemic. Many aspects of university
education and academic communities have changed since that time. However ,
the messages of this research remain relevant postpandemic and may be even
more important for racialized students [70–72]. As we now work to rebuild our
departmental communities and cultures, we have an opportunity to include
more people in physics.
VII. CONCLUSION
This study’s participants made progress toward building community among
students, among professors, and across the whole department as a means of
increasing their enrollments and diversity. Improving the student experience was
one aspect of this progress, including e"orts to create a coherent curriculum
map, making improvements to the second-year program, and increasing access
to the department for ﬁrst-year students. Another part of the community
building e"orts focused on student recruitment and retention, including a
mentorship program, undergraduate research experiences, one-on-one
interactions, and supporting the so-called second tier of students. Participating
in community building helped some participants move from welcoming people
into physics to encouraging greater feelings of belonging. This type of work aims
to strengthen and diversify membership in the physics community for the long
term, but this research conﬁrmed prior work [14,15,49,50] in that the changes

developments for the physics culture [3,9,12,27]. The challenge of making this

that the traditional physics culture creates. Even if members of the physics
culture accept the need for change, making the shift toward belonging and a
community of support may be daunting, especially for those who do not already
feel a sense of belonging themselves. We suggest that readers interested in
making the types of changes in this research should seek out a community of
support for themselves among colleagues in physics, science, education, and
other university departments.

belonging in physics for students, and a similar lack of belonging among
professors. Understanding this gap, and the associated challenge of shifting
paradigms in the physics culture from classical to quantum, will help increase
recruitment, retention, persistence, and diversity and will move the physics
culture toward wider support and inclusion for all.
