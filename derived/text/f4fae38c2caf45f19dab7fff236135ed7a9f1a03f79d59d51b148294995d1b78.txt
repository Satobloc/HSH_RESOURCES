Mathematical Reality and Physical Actuality 
Resolving the Ontological Controversy in Quantum Foundations 
 
 
Juliet Zhong 
Independent Researcher, London, United Kingdom 
ORCID: 0009-0006-5099-3671 
 
 
Abstract 
 
At the 2025 Helgoland centenary conference, Anton Zeilinger and Alain Aspect exchanged a 
philosophically consequential disagreement. Zeilinger asserted that there is no quantum world 
and that the quantum state is nothing more than information; Aspect replied that quantum states 
are real and objective. A subsequent Nature survey of over 1,100 physicists confirmed that this 
disagreement is structural and persistent. A previous study (Zhong, 2026a) demonstrated that 
this divergence cannot be resolved within a single -layer ontological framework. The present 
paper completes that argument by identifying the precise conceptual distinction the earlier 
analysis left open: the wavefunction is mathematically real and objectively defined, but it 
describes the probability structure of possible physical outcomes rather than a simultaneously 
existing classical reality. Measurement outcomes belong to the domain of physically actualised 
facts. The Zeilinger position captures an essential feature of the wavefunction domain; the 
Aspect position captures an essential feature of the outcome domain. Their apparent 
contradiction arises from the conflation of mathematical reality with physical actuality —a 
conflation all five major interpretations of quantum mechanics share. The formal inter face 
between these two levels is expressed by the staged mapping | ψ⟩ → Cᵢ → Tμν(Cᵢ) → G μν 
(Zhong, 2026b), which specifies the conceptual relationship between quantum probability 
structures and classical physical descriptions. This two -level definition dissolves the 
ontological controversy in quantum foundations. 
Keywords: quantum mechanics interpretation; wavefunction ontology; mathematical reality; 
physical actuality; measurement problem; Zeilinger –Aspect divergence; ontological 
stratification; quantum gravity interface

1. Introduction: The Dispute and Its Stakes 
In June 2025, the physics community gathered on the German island of Helgoland to mark one 
hundred years of quantum mechanics. The exchange that crystallised the occasion was brief. 
Anton Zeilinger, co-recipient of the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics, stated: "There is no quantum 
world." Alain Aspect, joint recipient of the same prize for his landmark Bell inequality tests, 
replied: "I disagree." A Nature survey of more than 1,100 physicists, published on 30 July 2025, 
subsequently confirmed that this disagreement is not a personal philosophical difference but a 
structural and unresolved division within the research community (Gibney, 2025). One 
hundred years after the birth of quantum mec hanics, physicists agree on the mathematical 
formalism and disagree, profoundly, on what that formalism describes. 
A previous study (Zhong, 2026a) argued that this disagreement cannot be resolved within a 
single-layer ontological framework —that is, within any framework that assumes the 
wavefunction and measurement outcomes belong to the same type of being and must be 
accommodated within a si ngle set of ontological categories. That paper further argued that a 
minimal ontological stratification, distinguishing the domain of quantum states from the 
domain of measurement outcomes, is a necessary condition for rendering the Zeilinger and 
Aspect positions simultaneously coherent. It did not, however, provide a precise 
characterisation of what distinguishes the two domains or a formal account of the interface 
between them. 
The present paper completes that argument. Its central claim is that the Zeilinger –Aspect 
divergence arises from a specific conceptual conflation: the conflation of mathematical reality 
with physical actuality. The wavefunction is mathematically real and o bjectively defined—it 
is not a subjective probability assignment, not a mere calculational device, and not an 
expression of observer ignorance. But it describes the probability structure of possible physical 
outcomes for a single system, not a collection o f simultaneously existing classical realities. 
Measurement outcomes, by contrast, are physically actualised facts: definite, observer -
independent, and characterised by the non -local correlations that Aspect’s experiments have 
established. Zeilinger is correct about the wavefunction domain; Aspect is correct about the 
outcome domain. The two positions are not contradictions. They are accurate descriptions of

===== PAGE 3 =====
two different aspects of the same physical situation, which have been forced into apparent 
conflict by the assumption that they must occupy the same ontological level. 
The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reconstructs the Zeilinger and Aspect positions with 
precision, identifying exactly what each physicist is responding to in the quantum mechanical 
formalism. Section 3 argues that all five major interpretations documented in the Nature survey 
share a common conceptual conflation: the conflation of mathematical reality with physical 
actuality, in one direction or the other. Section 4 develops the distinction between mathematical 
reality and physical actuality as the c onceptual key to dissolving the divergence. Section 5 
introduces the staged mapping | ψ⟩ → Cᵢ → Tμν(Cᵢ) → G μν as the formal expression of the 
interface between the two levels, drawing on the companion paper on quantum gravity (Zhong, 
2026b). Section 6 addresses objections and clarifications. Section 7 concludes. 
2. What Each Physicist Is Responding To 
2.1 Zeilinger and the Informational Character of the Wavefunction 
Zeilinger’s position is a response to genuine and well-established features of the wavefunction 
as it appears in the quantum mechanical formalism. The wavefunction ψ is defined on the 
configuration space of a physical system—an abstract, high-dimensional mathematical space, 
not the three -dimensional space of ordinary physical experience. It assigns probability 
amplitudes to possible measurement outcomes, not definite values to physical properties. When 
a measurement is performed and an outcome is obtained, the wavefun ction is updated: what 
was a superposition of possible outcomes becomes, in effect, a representation of the one 
outcome that was realised. The wavefunction, in Zeilinger’s reading, is therefore essentially 
informational: it encodes what can be predicted about measurement results, not what is the case 
independently of any measurement (Zeilinger, 1999; Fuchs, Mermin and Schack, 2014). 
This reading is grounded in the mathematical structure of the theory. The wavefunction does 
not assign definite values to position and momentum simultaneously; it assigns probability 
distributions. It does not describe a particle at a location; it describe s the probability that a 
particle will be found at a location if a measurement is performed. These features are not 
artefacts of interpretation; they are features of the mathematical object. The informational 
reading is a natural and defensible response to them. 
Where Zeilinger’s position becomes philosophically contentious is in its further inference: that 
because the wavefunction is informational in character, there is no quantum world—no mind-

===== PAGE 4 =====
independent domain of physical reality to which the wavefunction corresponds. This inference 
moves from the correct observation that the wavefunction does not describe definite classical 
properties to the stronger conclusion that there is nothing for the w avefunction to be about. It 
is this inference, as the present paper argues, that is mistaken. 
2.2 Aspect and the Objectivity of Quantum Correlations 
Aspect’s position is an equally well-grounded response to a different set of features of quantum 
mechanics. His experimental work in the 1980s, building on Bell’s 1964 theoretical analysis, 
produced the first convincing demonstration that quantum correlations violate Bell inequalities 
(Aspect, Grangier and Roger, 1982; Bell, 1964). This result establishes that the statistical 
predictions of quantum mechanics cannot  be reproduced by any theory that assumes both 
locality and realism in the classical sense: th at particles have definite properties prior to 
measurement, and that those properties are determined by factors confined to their immediate 
vicinity. 
For Aspect, this experimental legacy grounds a realist commitment. The correlations between 
entangled particles are objective, reproducible, and non-local. They cannot be explained as the 
product of pre -existing local properties —Bell’s theorem rules that out. They cannot be 
explained as the joint updating of subjective beliefs —the correlations obtain whether or not 
any agent is present or aware of them. Something in the world must account for these 
correlations, and that something must be non-local in character. The wavefunction, or whatever 
the wavefunction describes, must have ontological weight: it must correspond to something 
real, not merely to an observer’s bookkeeping. 
This position is equally well -supported. The Bell experiments are among the most precisely 
confirmed results in the history of physics, and the non -local correlations they establish are a 
structural feature of quantum mechanics that no purely epistemic int erpretation can fully 
account for without invoking some form of objective, non -local physical structure. Aspect is 
correct that the outcomes of quantum measurements, and the correlations between them, are 
objectively real. 
2.3 The Structural Asymmetry 
What is philosophically significant about the exchange is that both physicists are responding 
accurately to genuine features of the same theory, and those features do not belong to the same 
level of description. Zeilinger is responding to the character of the wavefunction as a

===== PAGE 5 =====
mathematical object: its probabilistic, contextual, and high -dimensional character. Aspect is 
responding to the character of measurement outcomes and their correlations: their definiteness, 
reproducibility, and objective non -locality. The conflict arises n ot because one physicist is 
wrong but because both are correct about different aspects of quantum mechanics, and the 
single-layer ontological assumption forces those Aspect’s onto a single canvas where they 
appear to contradict each other. 
The question the present paper pursues is whether this conflict is genuine —whether one 
position must be abandoned—or whether it is an artefact of a shared conceptual conflation. The 
answer, it will be argued, is the latter. 
3. The Common Conflation of the Five Major Interpretations 
The 2025 Nature survey documented the distribution of interpretive preferences across five 
major positions: the Copenhagen interpretation (favoured by 36% of respondents), many -
worlds (15%), Bohmian mechanics (7%), spontaneous collapse theories (4%), and e pistemic 
or QBist approaches (17%), with a substantial minority selecting other formulations. Each of 
these interpretations represents a sophisticated and serious attempt to account for the full range 
of quantum mechanical phenomena within a coherent conce ptual framework. None, this 
section argues, succeeds—and the pattern of failure is the same in each case: the conflation of 
mathematical reality with physical actuality, in one direction or the other. 
The Copenhagen interpretation, in its standard formulation, holds that the wavefunction 
represents the probabilities of measurement outcomes and that asking about the state of a 
system between measurements is unanswerable in principle. It combines elements  of both the 
Zeilinger and Aspect positions —taking the wavefunction as non -referential while treating 
measurement outcomes as real —without providing a principled account of the relationship 
between them. The result, as Bohr’s defenders acknowledge, is a systematic refusal to answer 
the question of what the wavefunction is about (Bohr, 1935). That approximately 29% of self-
described Copenhagen adherents in the survey simultaneously believe the wavefunction 
represents something real illustrates the internal i nconsistency: the interpretation is used as a 
practical tool without its philosophical content being clearly apprehended (Gibney, 2025). 
The many-worlds interpretation takes the wavefunction with maximum ontological seriousness: 
it treats the wavefunction as the totality of physical reality, evolving unitarily at all times, with 
all branches equally real (Everett, 1957). This accommodates t he Aspect intuition that the

===== PAGE 6 =====
wavefunction is ontically real but at the cost of generating a severe problem at the other end: if 
all branches are real, the appearance of a single definite outcome in any given branch requires 
explanation within a framework that treats all outcomes as si multaneously existing. The 
classical world of definite facts becomes a secondary, emergent phenomenon within a 
fundamentally quantum ontology. Many-worlds mistakes the full mathematical structure of the 
wavefunction for the full ontological structure of physical reality. 
Bohmian mechanics assigns definite trajectories to particles, guided by a real pilot wave (Bohm, 
1952). It is explicitly psi -ontic and accommodates Aspect’s realism. But it achieves this by 
introducing a wavefunction defined in the configuration space of all particles—not in ordinary 
three-dimensional physical space—and by treating this high-dimensional mathematical object 
as a real physical field. The ontological status of a field defined in a space that is not the space 
of physical experience remains phil osophically opaque. Bohmian mechanics mistakes a 
mathematical structure for a physical entity without clarifying what kind of entity could exist 
in configuration space. 
Spontaneous collapse theories modify the Schrödinger equation to introduce a physical 
collapse mechanism (Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber, 1986). They are empirically testable in 
principle and represent the most honest attempt to provide a physical account of t he domain 
transition between quantum superposition and definite classical outcomes. But they add a 
stochastic collapse term that is physically unexplained, introduced specifically to solve the 
measurement problem. The collapse mechanism is an ad hoc mathem atical modification that 
gestures at a physical process without identifying one. 
Epistemic and QBist interpretations hold that the wavefunction encodes an agent’s degrees of 
belief about measurement outcomes, not a description of physical reality (Fuchs, Mermin and 
Schack, 2014; Caves, Fuchs and Schack, 2002). They handle the Zeilinger dimension with 
elegance but face a severe difficulty with the Aspect dimension: if the wavefunction is purely 
subjective, what accounts for the objective, non -local correlations between distant entangled 
particles? The correlations cannot be attributed to pre-existing local properties—Bell’s theorem 
rules that out—and cannot be fully explained as the joint updating of different agents ’ beliefs 
without some shared objective physical structure. Epistemic interpretations mistake 
mathematical instrumentalism for ontological adequacy. 
The pattern of failures is not random. Each interpretation fails specifically at the point where it 
must account for phenomena from one domain using the conceptual resources of another. The

===== PAGE 7 =====
failure is always at the same structural location: the interface between the domain of quantum 
probability structures and the domain of physically actualised facts. This repeated failure at the 
same point is precisely what would be expected if the conflati on of mathematical reality with 
physical actuality is the source of the difficulty. 
4. Mathematical Reality and Physical Actuality: The Conceptual Key 
4.1 The Distinction 
The central conceptual distinction required to dissolve the Zeilinger –Aspect divergence is the 
following: mathematical reality and physical actuality are not the same thing, and quantum 
mechanics describes both, at different levels. 
Mathematical reality, as used here, refers to the objective, mind -independent character of the 
wavefunction as a mathematical object. The wavefunction is not a subjective probability 
assignment, not an expression of observer ignorance, and not a mere calculational device. It is 
a real description of a real feature of a physical system: the complete probability structure of 
possible outcomes for that system under specified conditions. This probability structure is not 
created by the observer; it is a propert y of the system, determined by its physical constitution 
and its history of interactions. It is objective in the same sense that any mathematical 
description of a physical situation is objective: it is the same for all observers, it evolves 
according to de terministic laws (the Schrödinger equation), and it generates precise 
quantitative predictions that can be confirmed or disconfirmed by experiment. 
Physical actuality, by contrast, refers to the domain of definite, realised physical facts: the 
definite position of a particle after measurement, the definite correlation between two entangled 
particles after their joint measurement, the definite reading of a detector. These are facts in the 
full classical sense: they are either the case or not the case, they are observer-independent, and 
they are the objects to which general relativistic descriptions of spacetime —the Einstein field 
equations—apply. Physical actuality is the domain of what has happened, not of what could 
happen. 
The wavefunction belongs to the domain of mathematical reality. It describes what could 
happen—the full probability structure of possible outcomes —for a system that has not yet 
undergone the relevant physical interaction. Measurement outcomes belong to the  domain of 
physical actuality. They describe what has happened —the definite result of a physical 
interaction that has already occurred.

===== PAGE 8 =====
These two domains are not competing descriptions of the same moment. They are descriptions 
of different aspects of the same physical situation: the wavefunction describes the situation 
before a specific outcome is actualised; the measurement outcome describes the situation after. 
The transition between them is not a process within the wavefunction formalism —the 
Schrödinger equation does not generate it—but the actualisation of one possibility from among 
those encoded in the probability structure. 
4.2 Why This Is Not the Epistemic Interpretation 
It is essential to distinguish the present account from the epistemic or QBist interpretation, with 
which it shares some surface features. Both accounts hold that the wavefunction does not 
describe a collection of simultaneously existing classical realitie s. But they differ 
fundamentally on the question of whether the wavefunction is objective. 
On the epistemic interpretation, the wavefunction represents an agent’s subjective degrees of 
belief. It is updated by measurement because the agent has acquired new information, not 
because anything physical has changed. The wavefunction is, in the strict sense, about the agent, 
not about the world. 
On the present account, the wavefunction is objectively real: it is a real feature of a physical 
system, not of any observer’s mental state. Its probabilistic character is not a sign of epistemic 
limitation but of the genuine physical character of the pre -actualisation domain. The 
wavefunction is updated by physical interaction not because an agent has acquired information 
but be cause a physical process has occurred that actualises one of the possible outcomes 
encoded in the probability structure. The update is ontological, not epistemic: something in the 
world has changed, not merely in the agent’s knowledge. 
This distinction matters for the Aspect problem. If the wavefunction is objective —if it 
describes a real feature of physical systems—then the non-local correlations between entangled 
particles can be grounded in the objective probability structure that bot h particles share. The 
correlations are objective because the wavefunction is a mathematically calculated objective 
probability structure; they are non -local because the wavefunction, as a description of the 
probability structure of a composite system, is defined over the composite system's 
configuration space, which is not decomposable into independent local components. Aspect is 
satisfied: the correlations are grounded in something real, objective, and physical.

===== PAGE 9 =====
At the same time, Zeilinger is satisfied: the wavefunction does not describe a classical world 
of definite simultaneous properties. It describes the probability structure of possible outcomes 
for a system that has not yet actualised one of them. "There is no quantum world" in the sense 
of a collection of simultaneously existing classical facts —correct. But there is a quantum 
domain: a domain of objective probability structures, mathematically real, physically 
significant, and causally efficacious in determining the statistics of measurement outcomes. 
4.3 What the Distinction Resolves 
With this distinction in place, the Zeilinger–Aspect divergence dissolves without remainder. 
Zeilinger is correct that there is no quantum world in the sense of a classical reality with definite 
properties existing prior to and independently of measurement. The wavefunction domain is 
not a classical domain. It is a domain of objective probability structures: real, but not yet 
actualised into definite classical facts. The informational character of the wavefunction that 
Zeilinger emphasises is genuine: the wavefunction does encode information about possible 
outcomes, and it is correctly described as  a probability amplitude structure rather than a 
catalogue of simultaneously existing classical states. 
Aspect is correct that the wavefunction describes something real and that the correlations 
between entangled particles are objective, non -local, and not derivable from any account that 
treats the wavefunction as merely subjective. The wavefunction domain is real, objective, and 
causally efficacious. The non -local correlations are grounded in the objective probability 
structure of the composite quantum system, which is not decomposable into independent local 
components. Realism about the wavefunction is appr opriate, provided it is understood as 
realism about a domain of objective probability structures rather than realism about a domain 
of simultaneously existing classical facts. 
The two positions are not contradictions. They are accurate descriptions of two different 
domains—the domain of objective probability structures and the domain of physically 
actualised facts —which the single -layer ontological assumption has forced into app arent 
conflict by treating both as descriptions of the same kind of entity. 
5. The Formal Interface: The Staged Mapping 
The conceptual distinction between mathematical reality and physical actuality has a formal 
expression. In the companion paper on quantum gravity (Zhong, 2026b), the interface between

===== PAGE 10 =====
the quantum description of matter and the classical description of spacetime is expressed as a 
staged mapping: 
|ψ⟩ → Cᵢ → Tμν(Cᵢ) → Gμν 
Here |ψ⟩ is the quantum state of a physical system, encoding its complete probability structure 
over possible configurations. C ᵢ is a classically realisable configuration —an actualised 
outcome, a definite physical fact. T μν(Cᵢ) is the energy -momentum tensor corresponding to 
that configuration, and Gμν is the spacetime geometry determined by that tensor through the 
Einstein field equations. 
The mapping is staged rather than direct. The standard framing of the quantum gravity problem 
assumes a direct mapping |ψ⟩ → gμν: the quantum state is supposed to immediately and directly 
determine a spacetime geometry. This assumption generates the apparent inconsistency, 
because a superposed quantum state cannot determine a unique classical metric. The staged 
mapping resolv es the inconsistency by recognising that the spacetime geometry does not 
correspond to the quantum probability structure | ψ⟩ but to the actualised configuration Cᵢ that 
the probability structure is a description of. The quantum description and the gravitational 
description apply at different levels of the physical situation. The mapping is not proposed as 
a dynamical equation but as an ontological decomposition of the transition between levels. 
This formal structure directly encodes the conceptual distinction of the preceding section. The 
transition from |ψ⟩ to Cᵢ is the transition from mathematical reality to physical actuality: from 
the objective probability structure of possible outcomes to the definite physical fact that is 
realised through physical interaction. The transition from Cᵢ to Tμν(Cᵢ) to Gμν is the application 
of the gravitational description to the actualised configuration—the domain in which classical 
and relativistic physics applies. 
For the purposes of the Zeilinger–Aspect dispute, the relevant portion of the staged mapping is 
the first transition: | ψ⟩ → Cᵢ. This transition is the domain transition from the wavefunction 
domain to the outcome domain. Zeilinger’s position correctly characterises the |ψ⟩ side of this 
transition: the quantum state is a probability structure, not a classical world. Aspect’s position 
correctly characterises the C ᵢ side: the actualised outcome is real, definite, and objectively 
correlated with the outcomes of measurements on entangled systems. The transition itself—the 
actualisation of a definite outcome from a quantum probability structure —is what the present

===== PAGE 11 =====
framework identifies as the physically significant event, and what all five major interpretations 
have been attempting, in their different ways, to account for. 
The staged mapping does not specify what physical process constitutes the transition from |ψ⟩ 
to Cᵢ. This remains the central open question of quantum foundations. What the mapping does 
specify is the conceptual structure that any account of that process must respect: the quantum 
state and the actualised outcome are not entities of the same type, and th e transition between 
them is not a process within the Schrödinger equation’s  domain but the boundary of that 
domain. Candidate physical accounts of the trans ition include objective collapse models 
(Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber, 1986), gravitational collapse proposals (Penrose, 1996), and 
decoherence-based accounts (Zurek, 2003; Schlosshauer, 2005). Each of these is a proposal 
about the physical mechanism of the |ψ⟩ → Cᵢ transition; none is in conflict with the conceptual 
framework proposed here. 
6. Objections and Clarifications 
6.1 Is This Just Copenhagen? 
The present account shares with the Copenhagen interpretation the claim that the wavefunction 
does not describe a classical world of definite simultaneously existing properties. But it differs 
from Copenhagen in two important respects. First, it offers a positive characterisation of what 
the wavefunction does describe: an objective probability structure over possible physical 
outcomes. Copenhagen declines to answer this question. Second, it does not require a 
classical/quantum cut imposed from outside the formalism, nor does it make the measurement 
outcome observer-dependent. The actualisation of an outcome is a physical process, not an 
epistemic event. The present framework is realist about both domains: the wavefunction 
domain and the outcome domain are both objectively real, each at its own level of description. 
6.2 Does This Solve the Measurement Problem? 
It does not solve the measurement problem in the sense of providing a dynamical mechanism 
for the |ψ⟩ → Cᵢ transition. It dissolves the measurement problem as classically formulated —
as the problem of how the Schrödinger equation generates a definite classical outcome —by 
showing that this formulation is malformed: the Schrödinger equation does not generate 
outcomes because outcomes are not within its domain. The correctly formulated question is 
what physical process constitutes the domain transition, and thi s question remains open. The 
present framework identifies what kind of answer would be adequate: a physical account of the

===== PAGE 12 =====
transition from the domain of objective probability structures to the domain of physically 
actualised facts. 
6.3 What About the Preferred Basis Problem? 
The preferred basis problem—the question of why the universe branches or decoheres in one 
basis rather than another —arises most acutely within the many -worlds interpretation. Within 
the present framework, the question takes a different form: in what basis does the domain 
transition from |ψ⟩ to Cᵢ occur? This is a genuine physical question, and the present framework 
does not answer it. Decoherence theory identifies the conditions under which certain bases 
become dynamically preferred through environmental in teraction (Zurek, 1981; Joos et al., 
2003), and these conditions are relevant to identifying the physical regime in which domain 
transitions occur. The present framework is compatible with decoherence as an account of the 
approach to the domain boundary; it does not specify the mechanism of the crossing. 
6.4 Is Mathematical Reality a New Ontological Category? 
The distinction between mathematical reality and physical actuality is not a new ontological 
category introduced ad hoc for this problem. It is a distinction that is implicit in the structure 
of quantum mechanics and that has been partially recognised by s everal existing accounts. 
Heisenberg’s notion of potentia —the wavefunction as describing objective tendencies or 
potentialities that are real features of the world but not actualities until realised through 
interaction—is the closest historical anticipatio n (Heisenberg, 1958). The present account 
makes this distinction explicit, characterises it precisely, and shows that it is sufficient to 
dissolve the Zeilinger–Aspect divergence without introducing any additional physical structure. 
7. Conclusion 
One hundred years after the birth of quantum mechanics, the Zeilinger –Aspect divergence at 
the Helgoland centenary conference is not a sign of conceptual stagnation. It is a precise 
diagnostic: it marks, with unusual clarity, the internal boundary of the c urrent theoretical 
framework. Both physicists are correct. Each is describing a genuine feature of quantum 
mechanics. The conflict between their descriptions arises from a shared conceptual 
conflation—the conflation of mathematical reality with physical ac tuality—that is embedded 
in all five major interpretations of quantum mechanics documented by the 2025 Nature survey.

The wavefunction is mathematically real and objectively defined. It describes the complete 
probability structure of possible outcomes for a physical system. This probability structure is a 
real feature of the world, not a representation of observer ignoran ce or a subjective degree of 
belief. Zeilinger is correct that this probability structure is not a classical world of 
simultaneously existing definite properties. Aspect is correct that it is objectively real and that 
the non -local correlations it generate s are objective, physical facts. The two positions are 
accurate descriptions of two different aspects of the same physical situation —the domain of 
objective probability structures and the domain of physically actualised facts —which the 
single-layer ontological assumption has forced into apparent conflict. 
The formal interface between these two domains is expressed by the staged mapping |ψ⟩ → Cᵢ 
→ Tμν(Cᵢ) → G μν, developed in the companion paper on quantum gravity (Zhong, 2026b). 
This mapping shows that the quantum description and the classical description of spacetime 
apply at different levels of the physical situation and that their apparent inconsistency dissolves 
once the staged character of the interface is recognised. The same conceptual move that 
resolves the quantum gravity problem at the level of gene ral relativity resolves the Zeilinger–
Aspect dispute at the level of quantum foundations: recognising that mathematical reality and 
physical actuality are descriptions that apply to different aspects of the physical situation, 
connected by a domain transition that neither the Schrödinger equation nor the Einstein field 
equations can generate from within their own domains. 
Robert Spekkens observed in the Nature survey that "once we find the correct interpretation, it 
will announce itself by virtue of offering more coherence than anything before" (Gibney, 2025). 
The present paper does not claim to have found the correct inter pretation in the sense of 
providing a dynamical mechanism for the domain transition. It claims to have identified the 
conceptual distinction —between mathematical reality and physical actuality —that is the 
minimum requirement for any coherent interpretation , and to have shown that this distinction 
dissolves the most persistent and public disagreement in the foundations of quantum mechanics. 
The Zeilinger–Aspect divergence, viewed in this light, is not embarrassing. It is informative. It 
tells us, with precision, what the next theoretical advance must accomplish: not a new equation, 
but a clear account of the physical process by which the domain of objective probability 
structures gives rise to the domain of physically actualised facts.
