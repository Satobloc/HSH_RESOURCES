Abstract 
Physics defines time as a real-valued variable, t ∈ ℝ, and exploits the full real line in its computational 
apparatus: two-sided Fourier transforms, time-ordered correlation functions, the time-reversal operator, 
Feynman propagators, Green's functions, analytic continuation. Yet in its interpretive framewo rk, 
physics treats the physically meaningful domain of t as the half-line [0, +∞) or, locally, as the forward 
direction alone. This paper isolates this discrepancy as a distinct foundational problem —the half-axis 
problem—and argues that it is not a variant of the arrow-of-time problem. The arrow problem asks why 
processes are asymmetric within an accepted temporal domain; the half-axis problem asks what licenses 
the restriction of the interpreted domain itself. I examine the three standard justifications fo r the 
restriction—thermodynamic, causal, and cosmological —and show that each establishes at most a 
physical asymmetry within the domain, not a restriction of the domain, and that each admits of a 
counter-objection internal to its own terms that has not been met. I then develop an inductive argument 
from the history of physical quantities: temperature, energy, spatial position, negative frequency, the 
negative-energy solutions of the Dirac equation, and negative curvature were each initially treated as 
positive-definite or meaningless on the basis of regime-limited intuition, and in each case extension to 
the full mathematical range revealed new physics. The paper concludes that the half -axis restriction is 
an unjustified convention, and that the point t = 0—the unique fixed point of the time-reversal operator, 
the additive identity of the temporal group, and the boundary of the interpreted domain —possesses a 
structural status that the interpretive framework of physics has not acknowledged. Whatever the correct 
interpretation of the complete axis turns out to be, the current asymmetric interpretation is not entitled 
to its own self-evidence. 
 
Keywords: time asymmetry, temporal domain, half -axis problem, arrow of time, time -reversal 
symmetry, negative time, formal domain, interpretive domain, t = 0 fixed point, regime-limited intuition

1. Introduction: A Discrepancy Hidden in Plain Sight 
In the dominant continuous-time formulations of physics developed since 1687, the temporal variable t 
is treated as a real number, defined on the complete real line (−∞, +∞), and the formal machinery of the 
discipline—differentiation, integration, Fourier analysis, the symmetry operations of field theory —
makes essential use of the completeness of that domain. In every interpretive statement physics makes 
about time, the negative half of that domain is treated as physically empty: not as a region where physics 
fails to apply, but as a region that is not so much as mentioned. 
This paper argues that the discrepancy deserves a name and an analysis. I will call it the half -axis 
problem: the problem of what, if anything, licenses the restriction of the physically interpreted temporal 
domain to half of the mathematically employed one. 
The half-axis problem must be distinguished at the outset from the arrow-of-time problem, with which 
it is easily conflated. The arrow problem—the subject of an enormous literature from Boltzmann (1877) 
through Reichenbach (1956), Price (1996), Albert (2000), and Callender (2021) —asks why physical 
processes exhibit temporal asymmetry given that the underlying dynamical laws are (CPT 
considerations aside) time-reversal invariant (Sklar, 1993; Earman, 1974). The arrow problem takes the 
temporal domain as given and asks about the behaviour of systems within it. The half -axis problem is 
prior: it asks about the constitution of the interpreted domain itself. One can hold any position whatever 
on the arrow problem —Boltzmannian statistical, interventionist, Past -Hypothesis-based, 
perspectival—and the half-axis problem remains untouched, because every one of those positions is a 
thesis about asymmetric behaviour on an axis whose interpretive truncation none of them addresses. A 
direction is a structure on a domain al ready given; the half-axis problem concerns the domain, not the 
direction, and is therefore prior to every version of the arrow debate. 
Two terms are used throughout this paper with a precision their ordinary usage does not always carry. 
The formal domain of a physical quantity is the set of values over which the quantity's mathematical 
definition ranges —the domain specified by the equations, the integration variables, the symmetry 
operators, and the analytic instruments of the theory. The interpretive domain is the set of values that 
the discipline's explanatory and representational practice treats as phy sically meaningful—the values 
that appear in physical narratives, that are assigned physical referents, and whose limits are treated as 
limits of the phenomenon rather than limits of the model. The half -axis problem is the problem that 
arises when these two domains diverge: when the formal domain is (−∞, +∞) and the interpretive 
domain is [0, +∞), and when no argument has been given for the divergence.

2. The Formal Commitment to the Complete Axis 
The claim of this section is descriptive: mainstream physics is formally committed, pervasively and 
ineliminably, to the domain t ∈ (−∞, +∞). The argument proceeds in four layers of increasing structural 
depth—from the equations of motion, to the analytic instruments that solve them, to the propagators 
that encode physical content, to the symmetry operations that define the laws themselves—because the 
completeness of the temporal axis is not a feature of one formalism but a presupposition of all of them. 
Two further formalisms, Minkowski spacetime and the path integral, are then examined separately 
because each makes the formal commitment in a geometrically and conceptually distinct way. The 
intention throughout is to establish that the complete axis is not an optional extension of the formalism 
but its native domain, and that the interpretive restriction to the half-axis enters, without argument, only 
at the level of interpretation. 
2.1 Classical and Quantum Dynamics 
Newtonian mechanics defines dynamics through derivatives d/dt whose domain is unrestricted (Newton, 
1687). The second law, F = ma = m d²x/dt², is an equation on ℝ; any restriction to t ≥ t₀ is an initial 
condition imposed on the solutions, a fact about the problem being solved, not about the equations

themselves. This distinction —between the domain of an equation and the domain of a particular 
solution—is elementary but consequential. An initial condition at t = t₀ selects a solution from the family 
of all solutions; it does not modify the equation, and the equation retains its full temporal domain 
regardless of which initial condition is imposed. The sa me holds for Hamilton's equations, dp/dt = 
−∂H/∂q and dq/dt = ∂H/∂p, whose Hamiltonian flow is defined for all t ∈ ℝ; for the Euler –Lagrange 
equations, d/dt(∂L/∂q̇ ) − ∂L/∂q = 0, which are equally unrestricted; and for every other formulation of 
classical dynamics, including the Hamilton–Jacobi equation and the Liouville equation for phase-space 
distributions. The formal commitment established here—that the temporal variable of the equations of 
motion ranges over ℝ —is not a peculiarity of classical mechanics but carries through without 
modification into the field equations that supersede it. 
Maxwell's equations, ∂ μ F^{μν} = J^ν and ∂ {[μ} F_{νρ]} = 0, are partial differential equations in a 
temporal variable running over ℝ  (Maxwell, 1865 ). The restriction to t > t₀ that appears in the 
formulation of initial-value problems for electromagnetic fields is an initial condition imposed by the 
physics of the specific problem —specifying the field configuration at some reference time —not a 
constraint on the temporal variable appearing in the equations. The equations themselves remain defined 
for t < t₀, and their retarded and advanced solutions, which propagate influence forward and backward 
in time respectively, both exist as mathematical solutions on the complete temporal axis.  The same 
structure persists in the passage to quantum mechanics, where the temporal variable of the field 
equations becomes the temporal variable of the state -evolution equation, with the same unrestricted 
domain. 
The Schrödinger equation, iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ, evolves the state ψ(x, t) for all real t  (Schrödinger, 1926). 
The unitary time-evolution operator U(t, t₀) = exp(−iĤ(t − t₀)/ℏ) is defined for all t ∈ ℝ, including t < t₀, 
where it implements time-reversal of the evolution. In scattering theory—the empirical core of particle 
physics—this two-sided character is not merely formal but physically essential. The in -state |ψ_in⟩ is 
defined by the asymptotic condition lim_{t→−∞} ||ψ(t) ⟩ − |φ_in(t) ⟩|| = 0, where |φ_in(t) ⟩ is a free-
particle state at t → −∞. The S-matrix, S = lim_{t→+∞, t₀→−∞} U_I(t, t₀), is explicitly a map from the 
asymptotic past to the asymptotic future, constructed by integrating the interaction Hamiltonian over 
the entire real line. The limit t₀ → −∞ is not a notational convenience; it is a physical requirement that 
the incoming particles be far enough separated in the past that their mutual interaction is negligible. The 
entire scattering formalism —which underlies the calculation of every cross -section, decay rate, and 
scattering amplitude in the Standard Model—is a two-ended construction on the complete temporal axis. 
The equations of motion, from classical mechanics through quantum field theory, share a single formal 
commitment: the temporal variable is defined on ℝ, and initial conditions are conditions on sol utions 
within that domain, not restrictions of the domain itself. The analytic instruments used to extract 
physical content from these equations make the same commitment, independently and for thei r own 
structural reasons.

2.2 The Two-Sided Fourier Transform 
The two -sided Fourier transform, F(ω) = ∫_{−∞}^{+∞} f(t) e^{−iωt} dt, is not a convenience of 
computational practice but a load-bearing structure whose physical content depends ineliminably on the 
integral running over the complete line. The claim that a t emporal signal decomposes into pure 
harmonic oscillations—the foundational claim of frequency-domain analysis in quantum theory, field 
theory, optics, acoustics, and signal processing —requires the two -sided integral. The mathematical 
content of this claim is the Plancherel theorem and its generalisations, which hold for functions defined 
on ℝ; they do not hold, without substantial modification, for functions defined on a half-line. 
The one-sided analogue of the Fourier transform is the Laplace transform, L{f}(s) = ∫_{0}^{+∞} f(t) 
e^{−st} dt, a different mathematical object with different analytic properties, different convergence 
conditions, and a different physical interpretation. W hile the Laplace transform has its own physical 
applications—primarily in the analysis of causal linear systems, precisely because its one -sided 
character encodes causality by construction—it is not the instrument that physics uses as its default tool 
in quantum mechanics, quantum field theory, or relativistic wave equations. Physics uses the two-sided 
Fourier transform, and in doing so asserts, at every use, that its functions are defined and integrable 
over (−∞, +∞). 
The interpretive restriction to t ≥ 0 is in direct tension with this choice of instrument. A function whose 
domain is intrinsically restricted to t ≥ 0 does not possess the same two -sided Fourier representation 
without an additional extension prescription; at best it has a Fourier transform of its extension by zero 
to t < 0, which is a different  function with different spectral properties.  Physics uses the two -sided 
transform ubiquitously and correctly —because its functions are genuinely defined on the comp lete 
axis—and then, in interpretive statements, treats those same functions as though they were defined only 
on the half-line. The inconsistency is not in the mathematics, which is self-consistent; it is between the 
mathematics and the interpretation. That the analytic instruments used to solve the equations of physics 
make the same formal commitment as the equations themselves is the second layer of the argument. 
2.3 Green's Functions and Propagators 
The Green's function formalism is the systematic method for solving linear differential equations with 
sources throughout classical and quantum physics, and it requires the complete temporal axis both in 
its formulation and in the physical identities it generates. The Green's function G(t, t') for a differential 
operator (∂/∂t − L) satisfies (∂/∂t − L) G(t, t') = δ(t − t'), where δ is the Dirac delta, and its integral 
representation is obtained by Fourier transform over the complete real frequency axis: G(ω) = 1/(−iω − 
L(ω)). The standard frequency -space representation —in which the poles of G(ω) in the complex 
frequency plane encode the natural frequencies and decay rates of the system —depends on the two -
sided Fourier construction running over all of ℝ.

The retarded and advanced Green's functions, G_R and G_A, are the two physically relevant special 
cases. The retarded Green's function, G_R(t, t') = −θ(t − t') [A(t), A(t')]/(iℏ), where θ is the Heaviside 
step function, vanishes for t < t' and is used to p ropagate solutions forward in time from a source at t'. 
The advanced Green's function, G_A(t, t') = θ(t' − t) [A(t), A(t')]/(iℏ), vanishes for t > t' and propagates 
solutions backward in time. Both are defined on the complete real line; the Heaviside step functions 
encode the directionality of propagation as a condition within the domain, not as a restriction of the 
domain. That G_R vanishes for t < t' does not mean that t < t' is outside the domain of G_R; it means 
that G_R takes the value zero there, which is a fact about the function, not about its domain. 
The relation between G_R and G_A is an identity on the complete frequency axis: G_R(ω) − G_A(ω) 
= −2πi A(ω), where A(ω) is the spectral function. This identity is the basis of the fluctuation-dissipation 
theorem, which connects the response of a system to external perturbations with its equilibrium 
fluctuations, and of the Kramers –Kronig relations, which connect the real and imaginary parts of the 
response function through the requirement of analyticity in the upper half of the complex frequency 
plane. Both  of these results —among the most general and widely applied results in many -body 
physics—are theorems on the complete frequency axis whose derivation requires that the Fourier 
transform be taken over all of ℝ. They would be unavailable, or would require substantial reformulation, 
if the temporal axis were genuinely restricted to t ≥ 0. That the analytic instruments used to construct 
and solve the propagators share the same formal commitment as the equations of m otion is not a 
coincidence of notation; it reflects the fact that the complete axis is the native domain of both. The same 
commitment appears, in its most operationally direct form, in the symmetry operations through which 
physics defines and tests its fundamental laws. 
The Feynman propagator, Δ_F(x − y) = ⟨0|T{φ(x)φ(y)}|0⟩, where T denotes the time-ordering operator, 
is the central object of perturbative quantum field theory. Its poles in the complex momentum plane—
at p² = m² − iε for the Klein –Gordon field —encode the particle spectrum of the theory, and its 
perturbative expansion in powers of the coupling constant generates the Feynman diagram expansion 
whose predictions are compared to experiment. The time-ordering prescription, T{A(t)B(t')} = θ(t − t') 
A(t)B(t') + θ(t'  − t) B(t')A(t), uses step functions to select either the retarded or advanced ordering 
depending on the temporal relation between t and t'; it is defined for all real t and t', and the combination 
of the two orderings is what gives the Feynman propagator its distinctive analytic structure—poles with 
the iε prescription—which is essential for the calculation of S-matrix elements. 
The Wick rotation, t ↦ iτ with τ ∈ ℝ, passes from the Minkowski propagator to the Euclidean propagator

Minkowski and Euclidean path integrals compute the same physical amplitudes—relies on the analytic 
structure of the Minkowski propagator as a function on the complete real temporal axis, and on the fact

presupposes the complex -time neighbourhood of the real axis, which is only defined if the real axis

2.4 The Time-Reversal Operator and Its Physical Consequences 
The time-reversal operator T: t ↦ −t is the most explicit and irreducible formal commitment to the 
negative half-axis in the entire apparatus of physics. It is not a device for computing negative -time 
quantities; it is an operator that maps the temporal variable onto its negative values, and its physical 
significance is the investigation of whether the laws of physics are symmetric under this mapping. That 
investigation is one of the precision enterprises of modern physics, and its results are experimentall y 
confirmed to high precision. 
In non-relativistic quantum mechanics, the time -reversal operator is an antiunitary operator Θ = UK, 
where U is a unitary operator and K denotes complex conjugation. Acting on a state |ψ⟩, Θ reverses the 
sign of momenta and spins —the quantities that are odd under time reversal —while leaving positions 
unchanged. The Schrödinger equation is invariant under the combined action of t ↦ −t and Θ: if ψ(x, t) 
is a solution, so is Θψ(x, −t). This invariance is the expression of T -symmetry in non -relativistic 
quantum mechanics, and it is the baseline from which T-violation in relativistic theories is measured as 
a departure. 
In quantum field theory, the CPT theorem —the Lüders –Pauli theorem —establishes that any local 
Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory is invariant under the combined operation CPT, where C is 
charge conjugation and P is parity inversion, from the axiomatic assumptions of the theory alone  
(Streater & Wightman, 1964). The CPT theorem does not require T-invariance individually; it requires 
only the combination. The observed CP violation in neutral kaon decay, established by Christenson, 
Cronin, Fitch and Turlay (1964), therefore entails compensating T-violation by the CPT theorem: if CP 
is violated and CPT is exact, then T must be violated by the same amount. Direct experimental 
observation of T -violation—not merely inferred from CP -violation via CPT —was achieved by the 
BaBar collaboration (2012) in the B -meson system, at a significance of fourteen standard deviations. 
The measurement establishes, from the observation of an asymmetry in decay rates under the exchange 
of initial and final states, that the laws governing B-meson decay are not invariant under time reversal. 
Physics does not merely tolerate the negative half-axis; it measures the symmetry properties of the laws 
under reflection into it, to a precision of parts per thousand, and it identifies physical effects —the 
matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe—with the failure of exact T-symmetry. The point for the 
half-axis problem is this: the time-reversal operator T is defined as the map t ↦ −t, and the experimental

2.5 Minkowski Spacetime and the Lorentzian Signature 
In special and general relativity, time enters the geometry as one coordinate among four, distinguished 
from the spatial coordinates not by the extent of its range but by the sign of its contribution to the 
spacetime interval. In the (−, +, +, +) signature convention, the metric takes the form ds² = −c²dt² + dx² 
+ dy² + dz² in Cartesian coordinates, and the temporal coordinate t runs over ℝ by the same mathematical 
licence as x, y, and z. The metric signature determines the causal character of displacements—timelike 
intervals with ds² < 0, null intervals with ds² = 0, spacelike intervals with ds² > 0—but it does not restrict 
the range of the temporal coordinate. A spacetime event is a point (t, x, y, z) ∈ ℝ⁴, and the geometry of 
Minkowski spacetime is the geometry of ℝ⁴ equipped with the metric η_{μν} = diag(−1, 1, 1, 1). 
The algebraic structure of Minkowski spacetime makes the formal commitment to the complete 
temporal axis precise. Minkowski spacetime (ℝ⁴, η_{μν}) is a four -dimensional real vector space, 
closed under addition and scalar multiplication of four-vectors. If t is restricted to [0, +∞) while x, y, z 
remain unrestricted, the resulting set {(t, x, y, z) : t ≥ 0, x, y, z ∈ ℝ} is not a vector space: the sum of 
two events with t > 0 remains in the restricted set, but the negation of an event, which is required for 
the vector space structure, has t < 0 and falls outside it. The resulting set is also not a group under 
addition, since the additive inverse of any event with t > 0 is not in the set. It is, in short, not the arena 
of special relativity. The geometry of Min kowski spacetime is constitutively the geometry of ℝ⁴ with 
the Lorentzian metric; restricting t to the half -line while maintaining the rest of the structure would 
produce an object that is not Minkowski spacetime.

2.6 The Path Integral 
The Feynman path integral, ⟨x_f, t_f | x_i, t_i ⟩ = ∫ Dx(t) exp(iS[x]/ℏ) (Feynman, 1948), is a sum over 
all paths x(t) connecting the initial configuration (x_i, t_i) to the final configuration (x_f, t_f), weighted 
by the phase factor exp(iS[x]/ℏ) where S[x] = ∫_{t_i}^{t_f} L(x, ẋ, t) dt is the classical action. The 
temporal interval [t_i, t_f] over which the action is integrated is a finite interval, but the Lagrangian L 
is defined for all t ∈ ℝ—it is a local function of the field and its time derivative at each moment, and its 
definition carries no restriction to positive times. More structurally, the amplitude ⟨x_f, t_f | x_i, t_i⟩ is 
itself a function of t_i and t_f ranging over ℝ; the case t_i < 0, t_f > 0 is as well -formed as any other, 
and the path integral over that interval is computed in exactly the same way. The path integral formalism 
imposes no half-axis restriction; the temporal endpoints are free parameters, and their range is all of ℝ. 
In quantum cosmology, where the path integral is taken over geometries rather than particle trajectories, 
this point becomes sharper. The Hartle–Hawking no-boundary proposal (1983) formulates the quantum 
state of the universe as a path integral over compact Euclidean four-geometries, with the Euclidean time 
τ running over a compact interval determined by the geometry itself—there is no external restriction of 
τ to positive values, because the geometry determines the temporal structure rather than being defi ned 
against a pre -given temporal axis. Bounce cosmologies in loop quantum cosmology (Ashtekar, 
Pawłowski and Singh 2006) replace the classical singularity with a quantum bounce, and their effective 
equations have solutions extending continuously through t = 0 to t < 0. These remain theoretical 
proposals; the structural point they illustrate is that the path integral, when applied at the cosmological 
level, is not confined to the half -line by any feature of the formalism —the half-axis restriction would 
have to be imposed by hand, from outside the mathematics.

3. Three Justifications and a Category Error 
If the formal domain is (−∞, +∞), what justifies the interpretive restriction? Three answers recur in the 
literature and in the working attitudes of physicists. I take them in turn, examining each justification on 
its own terms and then identifying the counter-objection available within those terms. 
3.1 The Thermodynamic Justification 
The thermodynamic justification holds that the direction of entropy increase constitutes the physical 
reality of temporal direction, so that the "negative" region is simply the region physicists call the past, 
exhaustively described by forward -time physics run from earlier initial conditions. On this view, the 
half-axis restriction is not a restriction at all: t < 0 is available for use as "before t = 0," and the apparent 
truncation is merely a labelling choice about where the clock starts. 
This response fails to engage the half -axis problem. The half-axis problem is not about labelling; it is 
about whether the temporal domain is the half -line [0, +∞) or the full line (−∞, +∞). If t < 0 is fully 
available and means "before the reference event," then there is no half-axis restriction—and the question 
becomes why physics, in its interpretive statements, consistently treats the structure of time as one -
ended rather than two -ended. If t < 0 is not available —if the physical universe has a first mom ent—
then the justification for that unavailability is the cosmological one discussed in Section 3.3, not the 
thermodynamic one. The thermodynamic justification conflates the arrow of time with the extent of 
time. 
The deeper failure, however, is structural. The second law is a statistical regularity of macroscopic 
systems whose microscopic laws are time-symmetric; the asymmetry is injected through a cosmological 
boundary condition —the Past Hypothesis (Albert 2000) —which is itself unexplained, and whose 
improbability under the standard measure Penrose (1989) estimated at one part in 10^{10^{123}}. An 
asymmetry that is (i) statistical, (ii) macroscopic, and (iii) inherited from an unexplained boundary 
condition is an asymmetry within the domain. It has no resources to legislate about the domain. 
The counter-objection available within thermodynamic terms makes this concrete. Suppose the Past 
Hypothesis is granted in full: the universe began in an extremely low-entropy state at t = 0, and entropy 
has increased monotonically since. This grants the thermodynamic justification everything it claims. It 
still does not follow that t < 0 is interpretively unavailable. The Past Hypothesis is a boundary condition

on a dynamical equation; boundary conditions do not determine the domain of the equation, they 
determine the solution within the domain. A cosmological model with a Past Hypothesis at t = 0 is fully 
consistent with a complete temporal axis on which t < 0 r epresents a pre-boundary region, whatever 
that region turns out to mean. To conclude from "entropy increases for t > 0 given the Past Hypothesis 
at t = 0" that "t < 0 is physically empty" is to treat a boundary condition as a domain restriction —a 
confusion of two formally distinct concepts. 
3.2 The Causal Justification 
The causal justification identifies temporal direction with causal direction: the future is where effects 
are, the past is where causes are, and negative time is therefore just past time under another name. On 
this account, the half -axis problem dissolves into the arrow problem, and the arrow problem is to be 
solved by a theory of causal asymmetry. 
The circularity of causal theories of temporal direction is well established (Price 1996, ch. 6; Mellor 
1998, ch. 10): the asymmetry of causation is either primitive—in which case it is stipulated rather than 
explained—or grounded in temporal or entropic asymmetry, in which case the account presupposes 
what it purports to explain. But there is a further point specific to the half-axis problem that the standard 
circularity objection does not reach. 
Even granting a non -circular causal arrow —granting, that is, that there is a fact of the matter about 
which direction in time is "toward causes" and which is "toward effects" —causal order is an ordering 
of events within the interpreted domain. An ordering relation on a set does not determine the extent of 
the set. The integers have a well-defined linear order, but the order does not entail that the set is bounded; 
the natural numbers have the same order type as the integers above any fixed element, but the natural 
numbers are bounded below and the integers are not, and no fact about the order relation within either 
set settles the question of the other's lower bound. 
The causal justification therefore answers a question about structure within the temporal axis —which 
direction does causation run?—and is silent on the question actually posed—does the axis have a lower 
bound? These are not the same question. Establishing that causation runs from past to future does not 
establish that the past has a first moment, any more than establishing that the integers increase to the 
right establishes that there is a leftmost integer. The causal justification, like the thermodynamic o ne, 
addresses asymmetry and mistakes this for an answer about extent. 
The counter-objection sharpens the point. Causal asymmetry and temporal asymmetry are related by a 
mutual dependence that the literature on both has not resolved (Price 1996; Woodward 2003; Frisch 
2014). If causal direction is grounded in temporal direction, then causal asymmetry is a consequence of 
whatever asymmetry time possesses—and an asymmetry within the interpreted domain cannot generate, 
from within itself, a restriction on what the domain contains. If temporal direction is grounded in causal

===== PAGE 12 =====
12 
 
direction, then the causal order is prior, and the temporal axis is whatever structure supports that order; 
but a causal order on an unbounded set supports the same local asymmetry as a causal order on a 
bounded set—the order is indifferent to the bound. In neither direction of the dependence does causal 
asymmetry constrain the extent of the temporal domain. 
3.3 The Cosmological Justification 
The cosmological justification is the most direct: time began at the Big Bang, so the physical domain 
of t is [0, +∞), with t = 0 the initial singularity; the negative half -axis is an artefact of the formalism 
with no physical referent. This justification, unlike the first two, does address the domain question rather 
than deflecting to the direction question. It therefore deserves correspondingly more detailed 
examination. 
The Penrose –Hawking singularity theorems establish that, under the standard energy conditions, 
general-relativistic spacetimes of the cosmological class are geodesically incomplete toward the past 
(Hawking & Ellis, 1973)—that is, that past-directed timelike geodesics cannot be extended indefinitely 
but terminate at a boundary where the spacetime description becomes geodesically incomplete and the 
classical metric formulation ceases to provide an extension.  This is a precise mathematical result. But 
it establishes that general relativity breaks down at that boundary, not that the boundary is a boundary 
of reality. The distinction is between a theory's domain of validity and the domain of the phenomena 
the theory describes. Geodesic incompleteness is a singularity of the theoretical description; the physical 
inference that follows is that a more complete theory is needed at the relevant scale, not that the 
phenomena stop. 
This reading is not exotic. It is the explicit and consensus position of the quantum -gravity research 
programme. Bounce cosmologies in loop quantum cosmology (Ashtekar, Pawłowski and Singh 2006) 
resolve the classical singularity by quantum -geometric effect s—the quantum bounce replaces the 
classical big bang, and the geometry extends to a contracting pre -bounce phase at t < 0 in the 
parametrisation natural to the model. Pre -Big-Bang scenarios in string cosmology (Gasperini and 
Veneziano 1993) extend the spacetime manifold through the string-scale regime to a pre-big-bang phase 
governed by the duality symmetries of the low -energy string effective action. Conformal cyclic 
cosmology (Penrose 2010) treats the classical singularity as a conformal boundary between aeons, with 
a well -defined geometric continuation. These proposals differ in their physical content and in their 
evidential status; they share the structural commitment that the classical singularity is a transition, not 
a terminus. 
But the critical counter-objection does not require any of these models. It requires only the distinction 
between two propositions that the cosmological justification conflates: 
(A) The Big Bang is a boundary condition of the classical relativistic cosmological model.

===== PAGE 13 =====
13 
 
(B) The Big Bang is the ontological beginning of time. 
These are not the same proposition, and no argument available within the resources of classical general 
relativity establishes (B) from (A). A boundary condition of a model is a constraint on the solutions of 
a differential equation within a specified domain; it says nothing about what exists outside that domain, 
because the model is not defined there. The inference from (A) to (B) is a category error of the same 
form as inferring, from the fact that Newtonian mechanics is undefined for velocities exceeding c, that 
velocities exceeding c do not exist. The failure of the theory at the boundary is evidence that the theory 
needs replacing; it is not evidence about what lies at or beyond the boundary, because the theory that 
would describe that region does not yet exist. 
This point can be sharpened by attending to the Hawking–Penrose disagreement about the singularity, 
which the cosmological justification tends to ignore. Hawking (1988) proposed that the no -boundary 
condition in quantum cosmology—the Hartle–Hawking state—effectively removes the singularity by 
replacing the Lorentzian geometry near t = 0 with an Euclidean geometry in which "time" becomes a 
spatial dimension; on this account, the question "what came before the Big Bang?" is as malformed as 
"what is south of the South Pole?" Penrose (2010), by contrast, treats the classical singularity as a 
genuine boundary with a definite geometr ic continuation. The disagreement between two of the 
foremost contributors to singularity physics is not a settled matter; a foundational interpretive 
framework that rests its domain restriction on one side of this unresolved dispute does not stand on firm 
ground. 
3.4 The Category Error and Its Common Structure 
The three failures share a common structure, and identifying it is the central analytical contribution of 
this section. Each justification establishes, at best, a physical asymmetry: an asymmetry of entropic 
behaviour, of causal order, or of the domain of validity of a particular classical theory. Each then treats 
this physical asymmetry as though it effected a mathematical restriction: a contraction of the domain 
on which the temporal variable is defined and interpreted. 
These are different kinds of claim. A physical asymmetry is defeasible —it can be superseded by a 
broader theory, a resolved singularity, a different boundary condition. A domain restriction, were it 
legitimate, would be a fact about the definition of the variable and thus indefeasible by physical 
developments. The justifications offer defeasible grounds and draw a conclusion that, if it is to be any 
conclusion at all, must be indefeasible. This mismatch—defeasible premises, indefeasible conclusion—
is the category error. 
Nothing in this analysis determines what the negative half -axis means or whether it has a physical 
interpretation. The analysis establishes that the three standard justifications for interpreting it away do 
not succeed, because none of them engages the right kind of question. A physical asymmetry within the

===== PAGE 14 =====
14 
 
domain does not answer the question of what the domain is. Until an argument of the right kind is 
produced—one that addresses the domain directly, rather than deflecting to behaviour within it —the 
interpretive restriction is an unsupported convention. 
 
4. The Inductive Argument: Negative Quantities and Regime-Limited Intuition 
The half-axis restriction of time is not the first time physics has treated a quantity's mathematical range 
as truncated on the strength of regime-limited experience. The historical pattern is consistent enough to 
carry inductive weight, and it is worth developing beyond the three cases that are most frequently cited. 
4.1 Temperature 
Temperature was for two centuries a paradigmatically positive -definite quantity; the Kelvin scale 
seemed to make the positivity analytic, with absolute zero as an unreachable floor. Yet the statistical -
mechanical definition of temperature, 1/T = ∂S/∂E, con tains no positivity constraint: a system whose 
entropy decreases with energy—a population-inverted spin system—has negative absolute temperature 
(Ramsey, 1956). Purcell and Pound (1951) produced such a system in nuclear magnetic  resonance; 
negative-temperature ensembles are now standard objects in quantum optics, and motional negative -
temperature states have been realised in cold-atom lattices (Braun et al. 2013). The intuition of positivity 
was an artefact of the regime—unbounded energy spectra—in which thermometry happened to develop. 
Within that regime, no state with negative temperature was accessible; the impossibility was regime -
bound, not mathematical. 
4.2 Energy 
Classical kinetic energy is positive-definite, and pre-relativistic physics treated the positivity of energy 
as constitutive. General relativity introduced negative gravitational binding energy as a standard 
concept; the total energy of a closed universe i s plausibly zero by the argument of Tryon (1973), in 
which the positive energy of matter is exactly cancelled by negative gravitational energy —a proposal 
that has remained live in inflationary cosmology. Quantum field theory delivered locally negative 
energy densities: the Casimir configuration, in which vacuum fluctuations between two parallel 
conducting plates produce a negative energy density between them, was experimentally confirmed by 
Lamoreaux (1997). The positivity intuition encoded the regime of it s formation —slow particles, 
negligible gravitational binding, no vacuum fluctuations—not a property of the quantity. 
4.3 Spatial Position 
Spatial position is the case that reveals the structure of the restriction most clearly. No physicist regards 
negative x, y, or z as problematic, because the arbitrariness of the spatial origin is transparent: the

===== PAGE 15 =====
15 
 
coordinate's full range is used and interpreted without residue. A negative x -coordinate means "to the 
left of the origin I chose," and the physics is indifferent to the choice of origin. Space shows what a 
fully symmetric interpretive treatment of a coordinate looks like. 
Time, formally identical in the Lorentzian signature to within a sign—the temporal coordinate entering 
the metric with opposite sign to the spatial coordinates —does not receive the same treatment. The 
formal parallelism is routinely invoked in relativity theory when it is convenient (Wick rotation, 
Euclidean field theory, conformal methods); it is abandoned, without argument, in the interpretive 
stance toward neg ative values. The asymmetry in treatment is not grounded in a formal difference 
between the coordinates; it reflects an asymmetry in the physical intuitions brought to the interpretation, 
intuitions that may well be regime-limited in the same way as those that initially restricted temperature 
and energy. 
4.4 Negative Frequency 
In classical wave theory, negative frequencies are formal artefacts: the condition that a real -valued 
signal f(t) equals its own complex conjugate, f(t) = f*(t), entails that its Fourier transform satisfies F(−ω) 
= F*(ω), so that the negative -frequency com ponents carry no independent information. Negative 
frequencies appeared to be a bookkeeping device, not a physical reality. 
In quantum field theory, the status of negative frequencies is entirely different. The quantisation of the 
Klein–Gordon field decomposes the field into positive- and negative-frequency parts: φ(x) = ∫ d³k/(2π)³ 
[a(k) e^{−ikx} + a†(k) e^{ikx}], where the po sitive-frequency part is associated with particle 
annihilation operators a(k) and the negative-frequency part with particle creation operators a†(k). The 
physical distinction between particles and antiparticles, in the charged version of the field, is 
implemented through the assignment of positive and negative frequencies to different charge states. 
Negative frequencies are not eliminated by the quantisation procedure; they are promoted to physical 
significance as the Fourier components of the field that create particles from the vacuum. A formalism 
that regarded negative frequencies as physically meaningless would be unable to describe particle 
creation—that is, unable to describe the principal phenomenon of quantum field theory. 
4.5 Dirac's Negative-Energy Solutions 
The Dirac equation, (iγ^μ ∂_μ − m)ψ = 0, was derived in 1928 as the relativistic equation for the electron. 
Its solutions include, for every positive -energy solution with energy E > 0, a corresponding negative -
energy solution with energy E < 0. In the cont ext of 1928 physics, negative -energy electron states 
appeared physically impossible —an electron in a positive -energy state could radiate photons and 
cascade indefinitely downward through negative energies, violating conservation of energy. The 
solutions were mathematical objects without physical interpretation, and Dirac's initial response was to 
seek a way to eliminate them.

===== PAGE 16 =====
16 
 
The resolution—the Dirac sea, and subsequently the concept of antiparticles—preserved the negative-
energy solutions by giving them physical content. The filled Dirac sea interpretes the negative -energy 
states as occupied, and a "hole" in the sea corresponds to an antiparticle: the positron, predicted by 
Dirac (1931) and observed by Anderson (1932). In the modern quantum field theory reformulation, the 
negative-energy solutions are reinterpreted, in the Feynman –Stueckelberg picture, as positive -energy 
antiparticle solutions propagating backward in time—a reinterpretation that is itself a use of the negative 
temporal direction as a physically meaningful concept. The history of the Dirac equation is the history 
of a mathematical structure whose "unphysical" half —the negative -energy, or equivalently the 
negative-frequency, solutions—proved to encode the most unexpected physical content: the existence 
of antimatter. 
4.6 Negative Curvature 
Riemann's classification of constant -curvature geometries includes three cases: positive curvature 
(spherical), zero curvature (flat), and negative curvature (hyperbolic). In the context of nineteenth -
century physics, positive curvature was familiar from t he geometry of the sphere and plausible as a 
model of physical space; negative curvature was a mathematical curiosity whose physical relevance 
was unclear. In general relativity and modern cosmology, negative spatial curvature is a standard 
cosmological pa rameter: the curvature density parameter Ω_k can be positive, zero, or negative, 
corresponding to closed, flat, or open universes, and observational evidence—the CMB angular power 
spectrum—constrains Ω_k to be close to zero but is consistent with small negative values. Negative 
curvature is not a pathological extension of the geometric formalism; it is an empirically constrained 
physical possibility. 
4.7 The Inductive Schema and Its Epistemic Weight 
The six cases share a common pattern. A quantity is mathematically defined on a range that includes 
negative values. Experience within a limited regime—the regime in which the physics of the quantity 
was initially developed—gives no access to negative values. The mathematical structure is therefore 
either not noticed or actively interpreted as physically meaningless. Subsequent theoretical development, 
driven by the internal requirements of more comprehensive theories, reveal s that the negative values 
are p hysically significant: they represent new states of matter (negative temperature), new physical 
effects (Casimir energy), necessary degrees of freedom (creation operators), or entirely new classes of 
entities (antiparticles). In each case the extension is made by the mathematics before it is made by the 
physics, and in each case resistance to the extension reflects the limits of the regime in which the 
quantity was initially understood. 
The inductive schema is: (i) a quantity is mathematically defined on a range; (ii) experience within a 
limited regime suggests a truncated range; (iii) the truncation is reified into a quasi -definitional

===== PAGE 17 =====
17 
 
restriction; (iv) the restriction is eventually recognised as regime-bound; (v) extension to the full range 
reveals new physics. Temperature, energy, spatial position, negative frequency, Dirac's negative-energy 
solutions, and negative curvature have completed this schema. Time stands at stage (iii). 
The six cases do not, taken together, constitute a proof that negative time is physically meaningful. 
Inductive arguments of this form establish no such thing. What they establish is something more modest 
and more defensible: an epistemic heuristic. When a quantity is mathematically defined on a complete 
range, when the restricted region is inaccessible within the regime of the quantity's original development, 
and when the restriction has been reified without explicit argument, the default rational attitude is 
suspicion of the restriction rather than confidence in it. The heuristic is not a theory of induction; it is 
the distillation of a pattern whose recurrence across six independent cases of fundamental physics 
warrants treating as methodologically significant. To reject the heuristic in the case of time while 
accepting its deliverances for temperature, energy, and negative-energy solutions is to require a positive 
argument for the exemption—an argument that singles out the temporal axis as uniquely immune to the 
pattern. That argument has not been produced. The heuristic therefore applies, and the burden falls on 
the restriction. 
 
5. The Structural Status of t = 0 
Suppose the complete axis is taken interpretively seriously, as the formalism has always taken it 
computationally. One consequence follows immediately, and it concerns the point t = 0. 
5.1 Three Characterisations of t = 0 
On the half-axis reading, t = 0 is a boundary point of [0, +∞): topologically distinguished, but interpreted 
merely as the first moment—the initial condition, the singularity, the place where the account starts. On 
the complete-axis reading, t = 0 acquires a different and stronger characterisation, expressible in three 
equivalent ways. 
Topologically, t = 0 is the unique point belonging to the closure of both open half -axes. Every 
neighbourhood of 0 intersects both (−∞, 0) and (0, +∞), and no other point of ℝ has this property. It is 
the unique point of ℝ that is a limit point of both the  negative and positive rays simultaneously —the 
only point at which the two halves of the axis are in contact. 
Algebraically, t = 0 is the identity of the additive group (ℝ, +) under which the temporal axis has its 
natural structure. Every moment t has an additive inverse −t satisfying t + (−t) = 0. The complete 
temporal axis is, in this precise algebraic sense, ce ntred on 0: the point from which +t and −t are 
symmetric images of one another, and the only point invariant under the map t ↦ −t considered as a 
group automorphism. The half-axis [0, +∞) is not closed under additive inverses and does not form a

===== PAGE 18 =====
18 
 
group; it lacks the algebraic structure that the complete axis possesses, and in particular it lacks an 
identity element in the additive group -theoretic sense—no point that stands in the same relation to all 
others that 0 stands to on the complete line. 
Dynamically, t = 0 is the unique fixed point of the time-reversal operator T: t ↦ −t. For any t ≠ 0, T(t) 
= −t ≠ t; only t = 0 satisfies T(t) = t. This is the one point of the temporal continuum that is invariant 
under the reflection whose symmetry properties physics measures with the experimental precision 
described in Section 2.4. Th e fixed point of a symmetry operation is not an arbitrary element of the 
domain; it is the element that the operation leaves unchanged, and in the context of a continuous 
symmetry group, fixed points characterise the structure of the symmetry in a way that no other points 
do. 
5.2 Fixed Points and Physical Significance 
The significance of fixed points of symmetry operations in physics is not confined to the temporal case. 
It is a general feature of the discipline's practice that the fixed points of fundamental symmetry 
operations are assigned determinate physical status,  often serving as the natural reference states from 
which asymmetries are measured and against which departures require explanation. 
The fixed point of spatial inversion P: x ↦ −x is the spatial origin, which in a translation -invariant 
theory is arbitrary but in a theory with a preferred centre —as in the hydrogen atom, where the proton 
defines a preferred origin—becomes physically distinguished. The charge-symmetric configuration—
zero net charge, electrical neutrality—serves as the reference baseline from which the observed charge 
asymmetry of macroscopic matter is measured, playing the same structural role as a fixed point of the 
relevant symmetry operation.  The fixed point of the combined CP operation in the space of matter -
antimatter configurations is the symmetric state of equal matter and antimatter density, from which the 
observed baryon asymmetry of the universe—one of the principal unsolved problems of cosmology—
represents a departure that demands explanation. In each case the symmetric fixed point is not merely 
a mathematical convenience; it is the structurally natural state, the state that the symmetry operation 
leaves unchanged, and therefore the state relative to which all asymmetric configurations are defined. 
The fixed point of T on the temporal axis is t = 0. On the complete-axis reading, t = 0 is to the temporal 
structure what electrical neutrality is to the charge structure and what equal matter-antimatter density is 
to the baryon number structure: the point  of maximal symmetry, the unique temporally direction -
independent moment, the reference state from which all temporal asymmetries are departures. Physics 
has an established and systematic practice of asking, for each such fixed point, why the observed state 
departs from it—why there is net charge, why there is a matter surplus, why there is a preferred temporal 
direction. The half -axis reading of time makes the analogous question invisible, because a boundary 
point of a half -line is a terminus, not a centre of symmetry, and termini do not invite the question of 
why departures occur in both directions. Only on the complete-axis reading does the question become:

===== PAGE 19 =====
19 
 
why is there a temporal asymmetry given that the temporal structure is centred on a point of maximal 
symmetry? 
This is a more demanding version of the arrow -of-time problem than the standard one. The standard 
problem asks why there is a preferred temporal direction given that the microscopic laws are 
(approximately) time-symmetric. The complete -axis version asks wh y there is a preferred direction 
given that the temporal axis itself is centred on a direction -independent point. The two questions are 
related but not identical: the standard problem takes the half -axis domain as given and asks about 
asymmetric behaviour within it; the complete-axis version treats the symmetric point t = 0 as the natural 
reference and asks why the physical history is asymmetric about it. The complete -axis reading thus 
raises the explanatory standard, not by adding a new assumption, but by removing an unjustified one. 
5.3 The Asymmetric Treatment of the Temporal and Spatial Origins 
The contrast with spatial coordinates is instructive and largely unremarked. In spatial geometry, the 
origin x = 0 is treated as entirely conventional: any spatial point may serve as origin, the physics is 
invariant under spatial translation, and the negat ive half of each spatial coordinate axis is interpreted 
freely. The question "what is at negative x?" is answered straightforwardly: whatever is to the left of 
the chosen origin. 
The temporal origin t = 0 is not treated conventionally. It is identified with a specific physical event—
the Big Bang, or the initial conditions of the system under study —and the negative region is either 
treated as inaccessible or ignored. This asymmetry in interpretive practice is not licensed by the 
formalism. The Lorentzian metric distinguishes the causal character of intervals —timelike, null, 
spacelike—but it does not distinguish the temporal origin from any spatial origin in a way that would 
justify i nterpretive asymmetry. In the Minkowski line element ds² = −c²dt² + dx² + dy² + dz², the 
temporal coordinate and the spatial coordinates appear on fully parallel footing as coordinates of an 
event; the distinction is the sign in the metric, which affects c ausal structure, not the interpretation of 
the coordinate's range. 
If the spatial origin's status as conventional is taken seriously —as it universally is—and if the formal 
parallelism between temporal and spatial coordinates in the Minkowski metric is taken seriously—as it 
is when computationally convenient —then the conclusion that the temporal origin is similarly 
conventional follows from combining two positions the discipline already holds. The asymmetry in 
interpretive practice is not a considered philosophical stance grounded in a formal disanalogy; it is  an 
unconsidered habit whose continuation requires either a positive argument for the disanalogy or an 
acknowledgement that the habit is unjustified.

5.4 Structural Status Without Ontological Commitment 
These are elementary mathematical facts—the topological, algebraic, and dynamical characterisations 
of t = 0—and no ontological weight is claimed for them here beyond what they state. The argument of 
this section is not that t = 0 is a cosmologically significant moment, that it marks a phase transition in 
the laws of physics, or that the physical history is symme tric about it. The argument is prior and 
narrower: that the complete -axis reading assigns t = 0 a determinate structural status for which the 
interpretive framework of physics currently has no account and recognises no obligation to provide one. 
On the half -axis reading, t = 0 is a terminus, and no structural account of a terminus is required: a 
terminus is simply where the domain starts, and the question of why it starts there is deflected to the 
cosmological or thermodynamic justifications examined in Section 3. On the complete -axis reading, t 
= 0 is a centre of symmetry, and centres of symmetry in physics require accounts: they are the points 
from which departures demand explanation, and the discipline has a systematic practice of providing 
such explanations. The half -axis convention has not merely truncated a range; it has suppressed a 
question. Any complete interpretation of the temporal formalism owes an account of the fixed point of 
the time-reversal operator. The current interpretive framework does not have one, and has not noticed 
that one is owed. 
 
6. Relation to Existing Programmes, Scope, and Directions 
6.1 The Archimedean Programme and the Present Extension 
The argument closest in spirit to the present one is Price's (1996) Archimedean programme: the demand 
that the analysis of temporal asymmetry be conducted from an "atemporal standpoint" not itself infected 
by the asymmetries under analysis. The present paper is aligned with Price's methodological demand 
and can be read as extending it one level down. Price applies the atemporal standpoint to the 
asymmetries of physical process —radiation, thermodynamics, causation —and exposes double 
standards in their standard treatments: physicists use different inferential standards when reasoning 
toward the past and toward the future, without justification, and the Archimedean standpoint requires 
that the same standards be applied in both directions. 
The half-axis problem applies the same standpoint to the constitution of the temporal domain itself, and 
exposes a double standard more basic than any Price treats: the discipline computes on a symmetric 
domain and interprets an asymmetric one. Price's tar get is the double standard in the treatment of 
directions within the domain; the half -axis problem's target is the double standard in the treatment of 
the domain's extent —the use of (−∞, +∞) in computation and [0, +∞) in interpretation. The arrow 
literature, Price included, has been a debate about the direction of time. The half-axis problem concerns

===== PAGE 21 =====
21 
 
the extent of time. Extent is prior to direction, because a direction is a structure on a domain already 
given. 
The relationship is not one of conflict but of complementarity. A satisfactory response to the half -axis 
problem—an argument that entitles the interpretive restriction to the half -axis, or an argument for the 
full axis—would change the domain against which Price's analysis is conducted. If the complete axis is 
warranted, then the Archimedean demand applies not only to the asymmetries of processes at positive 
times but to the relationship between the positive and negative temporal regions. The two problems are 
nested, with the half-axis problem at the outer level. 
6.2 What Has Been Established 
It is worth being precise about what the foregoing has and has not established. It has not established 
that negative time is physically real, that the universe extends through t < 0, or that any particular 
cosmological model is correct. The argument is dia gnostic and destructive, not constructive; it 
establishes that the current interpretive framework has not paid for its restriction, not what the correct 
alternative is. 
Specifically, the paper has established three things. 
First, the interpretive restriction of time to the half -axis is supported by none of the justifications 
standardly assumed to support it. The thermodynamic justification conflates asymmetry within the 
domain with restriction of the domain, and the counter-objection shows that the Past Hypothesis, even 
if granted, is a boundary condition that does not settle the extent of the domain. The causal justification 
confuses an ordering relation on a set with a constraint on the set's extent, and the counter -objection 
shows that the mutual dependence of causal and temporal asymmetry is indifferent to the question of a 
temporal bound. The cosmological justification conflates the boundary of a theory's domain of validity 
with the boundary of reality, and the counter-objection—internal to the physics of singularities—shows 
that the singularity theorems establish geodesic incompleteness of the relativistic description, not the 
ontological terminus of time. 
Second, the history of physical quantities gives inductive grounds for suspecting regime -limited 
truncations of formally complete ranges, and time exhibits every diagnostic feature of the pattern: 
mathematical completeness, regime -limited inaccessibility o f the restricted region, reification of a 
convention into a quasi -definitional restriction. Temperature, energy, spatial coordinates, negative 
frequency, Dirac's negative -energy solutions, and negative curvature all completed the pattern by 
extension. The onus of argument falls on the position that time is uniquely exempt. 
Third, the complete-axis reading assigns the point t = 0 a determinate structural status —fixed point of 
time reversal, additive identity, topological centre of the complete line —for which the interpretive 
framework of physics currently has no account and recognises no obligation to provide one. The half -

===== PAGE 22 =====
22 
 
axis convention has not merely truncated a range; it has rendered invisible a structure that the 
mathematics defines precisely and that physics's own practice—in symmetry analysis, in the assignment 
of physical significance to symmetric zeros—is committed to taking seriously. 
6.3 Directions for Further Investigation 
Three questions arise naturally from the analysis and are left for further work. 
The first concerns quantum cosmology. If the complete temporal axis is taken interpretively seriously, 
the boundary conditions of quantum cosmological models —including the Hartle –Hawking no -
boundary proposal and the tunnelling proposal of Vilenkin (1982) —require re -examination. These 
proposals are formulated as conditions on the path integral over geometries; they do not, in their 
standard formulations, take a position on the extent of the temporal axis prior to the quantum -
cosmological calculation, because that extent is determined by the calculation. The question of whether 
consistent quantum-cosmological boundary conditions can be formulated on a complete axis, and what 
their physical implications would be, is open. 
The second concerns the interpretation of t = 0. The structural analysis of Section 5 assigns t = 0 a 
determinate formal status—fixed point of time reversal, additive identity, topological centre—but does 
not assign it a physical interpretation. The range of physically distinct interpretations available within 
the complete-axis framework is not obvious. The point might be an unobservable structural symmetry, 
an observational horizon, a phase transi tion in the properties of physical law, or the locus of a 
cosmological symmetry whose breaking generates the observed temporal asymmetry. Distinguishing 
these possibilities requires a theoretical framework that takes the complete -axis reading as a starting 
point. 
The third concerns the observational status of negative time. The diagnostic features of the inductive 
pattern—new physics revealed by the extension —suggest asking whether negative time has 
observational consequences distinguishable from those of a pure half-axis cosmology. This is a question 
for physical modelling and does not admit of a general answer at the foundational level. It is nonetheless 
the question that a positive extension of the present negative argument would eventually face, and 
framing it precisely is part of the productive agenda opened by clarifying the half-axis problem. 
6.4 The Debt 
The conclusion is deliberately austere. Physics is entitled to its computational use of the complete axis; 
it has earned that entitlement in three centuries of empirical success. It is not entitled to the interpretive 
deletion of half of it, because it has never paid for that deletion in argument. The debt has been invisible 
because the convention is old. It is nonetheless a debt. The half -axis problem is the demand that it be 
either paid or acknowledged as unpayable —and either outcome would constitute prog ress in the

foundations of the subject, because a discipline that knows what it has assumed is in a different 
epistemic position from one that has forgotten that an assumption was made.
