How Smuggling Groceries To The
Starving Landed Me In Jail
Winter.of.Discontext 6 min read · Sep 3, 2025
A Mental Health Crisis, A Court System On A
Vendetta, One Unforgivable Act of Compassion.
How I ended up smuggling groceries to a starving
woman
From 2019 to 2021, I was in a love story so intense it felt like it bent the laws
of physics. Days and nights melted into each other — antique hunts, epoxy
disasters, spontaneous phonograph sessions, thrift store raids, and weird
half-jokes turned half-truths. She moved me in during the early Covid
lockdown — into a strange little kingdom made up of two teens, a rotating

===== PAGE 2 =====
tween, her other partner, and me. We made art, cooked elaborate meals for
five, walked the empty streets in top hats like ghosts out of time, and tried to
hold each other together.
Even as the center couldn’t hold, she found me a job. That’s the kind of
wreckage it was — tangled and generous, even in collapse. We never stopped
messaging. Letters, playlists, QR codes hiding voice memos. Even when we
weren’t together, we were.
Then CPS started showing up. Anonymous tips. Allegations inflated to
absurdity — an argument at a grocery store twisted into a claim of beating her
daughter ten times in public. The investigation found no cause for
complaints.
But it was the wedge.
Not knowing where the complaints came from blamed everyone — her other
partner, me, even her kids. Eventually, she traced it all back to her father,
who disapproved of her home, her parenting, her life — and was about to put
her on trial for it. Literally.
Too many people. Too much intensity. Too much love in too strange a shape.
And, with a legal maneuver that leveraged decade old divorce papers, and an
absentee ex husband in another state, her father began dismantling our
shared life.
She started unraveling. And so did I.

===== PAGE 3 =====
The Accusation
Her parents came for custody — using legal leftovers from her first marriage,
propositioning her ex, and lavishing gifts on her daughter. Under pressure
and already slipping into early-stage psychosis, she lashed out in every
direction.
I went downhill, fast. Then I moved out. Devastated. Bankrupt in every way
except emotionally — because emotionally, I was still in it. Still receiving
texts. Still hiding our continued connection from her other boyfriend. Still
bringing antiques to drop-offs we pretended weren’t romantic.
Then, one day, she told me to delete our entire message history.
I didn’t.
But then she vanished.
I spiraled. Messages. Letters. Some desperate, some angry, some full of that
old impossible love. I begged her to stop the lies I feared she was preparing.
I’d seen her do it before — to others. I knew the pattern. But I didn’t know
how far it would go.
One day I was pulled over for expired tags. Then handcuffed.
And told it was for “stalking.”
Had I messaged her? Yes — mostly in response to hers. Hundreds of them.
Thousands. Had I dropped off things? Yes — at her request. Had I contacted
her after she told me not to? In the past, yes — we both had blocked each

===== PAGE 4 =====
other; and we had both broken the silence. After she told me to erase
everything and then ghosted me. That’s when I panicked. I threatened to sue
her for partial ownership of the antiques business we had started together.
It wasn’t stalking, it was preemptive self-defense. From what, I wasn’t yet
sure. But you don’t just tell someone to delete your shared history unless
you’ve got plans that make that history a problem.
I was answering her messages. I was reacting to a situation that was falling
apart in real time. My messages weren’t threats. They were peace offerings
frayed by fear: My fear — of her.
The Deal
Still struggling with covid-related employment chances, my lawyer charged
me thousands. I scraped it together.
He replied to a single email from the prosecutor. Prosecutor: “Thinking what
I’m thinking? CPO.” My own defense attorney: “Otherwise it’ll be a day or two
trial full of love declarations.”
They never reviewed the evidence. Never let me speak. Never gave me a
chance to explain that I wasn’t the one doing the contacting. Never even
allowed me into the closed door proceedings.
Then I agreed to sign it. Because every reason I gave that it was bogus landed
on deaf ears, every alternative I suggested was waved away dismissively
without consideration.

===== PAGE 5 =====
It was the only choice I was given. Once accused, you cannot fight. Because
to this court, context doesn’t matter. At all.
I gave in because it is what I thought she wanted. That there’d be no hearing.
That the order was “just a no-contact thing.” They didn’t tell me it was in a
separate court. Under a different number. Impossible to locate or modify.
I told my lawyer, “She’ll contact me again.” He shrugged.
I said, “This will cost me my job.” He said it wouldn’t. Verbally. Then he
reversed himself when I asked via email.
I lost the job.
What I didn’t know is that my name had already been used in her custody
case. That she, or her parents — or both — -had primed the court to see me as
a threat. And that I’d never be able to defend myself again.
“You agreed.” Said my attorney.
“Under duress” I said.
“Did you have a gun to your head?”
Because it seems that’s what it takes to undo a court order conceived under
duress by the requester, lightly agreed to by the defendant’s own counsel.
And completely unchallengeable within a system that will admit no wrong.

===== PAGE 6 =====
The Years of Silence
From late 2022 through early 2024, I followed the order. Zero contact.
Then came the first message.
Then another.
Then dozens.
She had totally lost the custody battle for her daughter. She was starving.
Alone. Broke. Unemployable.
She was scared.
And I… ignored her. Like I’d been told to.
Until I couldn’t anymore.
The Docket Disappears
She said the order was lifted.

===== PAGE 7 =====
I didn’t believe her. I checked the online docket — the same one I’d used
before. The case was gone. Not sealed. Not dismissed. Just… disappeared.
And so I helped. I fed her dogs. Cleaned her dishes. Fixed the washing
machine. Mowed the lawn. Repaired the plumbing. Brought food. Hauled
groceries.
We weren’t “back together.” She was barely the person I’d loved. She’d
stopped eating. Barely slept. Talked to herself in the mirror for hours.
Her teenaged son had been her caretaker. Then her ex husband had the son
removed by the court. The same court. Now he was gone, too. Again, citing
me as a reason. Once smeared, I had become the go-to excuse.
But the order had been lifted.
The Trip
On her birthday in 2025, I brought scallops and filet mignon. Her favorites.
For a few hours, she smiled. Laughed. Asked me to trip with her again — like
the old days.
Last time she’d asked, I’d refused. Miffed, she’d taken too much on her own.
Her anger turned to rage, borderline violence.
So this time, I said yes.

===== PAGE 8 =====
Because what’s worse than babysitting a schizophrenic while you’re tripping?
Babysitting an angry schizophrenic while she’s taken twice as much.
I don’t remember much. The police found me naked in the street at 3am. The
body cam shows I was polite, if disoriented. I called one officer’s bald head
“shiny.” He laughed.
Then they arrested me.
Homeless (According to Them)
I was released barefoot. No phone. No keys. No wallet. Wearing a GPS ankle
monitor.
I was told I couldn’t go back to get my stuff. Because the protection order was
still active.
So I walked.
When I finally got my keys and left the exclusion zone, I got pulled over
again. Field sobriety test. I passed. They searched my car. No drugs.
Later, I learned I’d violated the order by walking too close to her house. Even
though I’d given them my address, they now called me homeless.
I missed work. Again. Lost another job.

===== PAGE 9 =====
The System Isn’t Broken. It’s Working Exactly As Designed.
I was prosecuted for a crime that didn’t exist, based on messages she sent.
I was excluded from negotiations about my own rights.
I was told not to show up to court.
I was given an order I couldn’t locate — let alone understand.
I was forbidden from helping someone everyone else expected me to help — 
her family, her doctors, even her.
Now I face charges for violating an order I was never told how to follow, and
for doing drugs during a mental health emergency that wasn’t even mine.
The Groceries That Got Me Charged
She messaged me again. “I have no food. I’m starving. Please help.”
I picked her up. Took her to the store. Carried bags to her door.
Then my phone buzzed.
“You’ve entered the exclusion zone. One more time and you’re going to jail.”

Would I do it again?
Honestly?
Yes.
She’s sick. The system is cruel.
Someone had to carry the groceries.
