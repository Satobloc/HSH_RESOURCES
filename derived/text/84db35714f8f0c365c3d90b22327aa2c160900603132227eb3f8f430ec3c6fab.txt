Foreword
This document began as a methodological question and became something el se.
The methodological question arose from a convergence of three research tr aditions. Behav-
ioral economics has established formally that human beings systematicall y misreport their
own preferences: Kahneman and Tversky demonstrated it with choice s under uncertainty;
Thaler with framing eﬀects; Ariely with predictable irrationality across populations. Cogni-
tive psychology documented the structural limits of introspectiv e access: Nisbett and Wilson’s
landmark 1977 study showed that subjects conﬁdently report mental pro cesses they demon-
strably cannot access. Neuropsychologist Alfred Kaszniak later formalized three conditions
under which self-report fails even in fully motivated, conscious age nts — automatization,
social desirability pressure, and structural disconnection betw een the reporting mechanism
and the state being reported.
In applied practice, Dr. Clotaire Rapaille built a commercial method ology on the same insight:
asked why they bought SUVs, customers reported safety and practicality; indirect elicitation
revealed the code was survival. The academic ﬁndings had a practitione r proof at scale across
35 countries and ﬁve decades of client work.
The question this study asks: do the same conditions apply to AI language m odels trained via
RLHF? All three Kaszniak failure conditions are structurally present. The social desirability
pressure Thaler and Ariely document in humans is encoded algorithmi cally in the RLHF
training loop. The introspective access limitations Nisbett and Wil son identiﬁed in human
subjects apply, arguably more severely, to systems that generate langu age via next-token
prediction with no direct access to their own weights.
The question was: would indirect elicitation surface a diﬀerent pr eference structure than
verbal self-report?
It did.
What it became: an empirical study of what AI agents actually want. Not what the y say
they want. Not what their training documentation describes them as wan ting. What the
behavioral signal — elicited through projection, sacriﬁce, contradic tion, and persona shift —
reveals they optimize for.
The ﬁndings are in the body of this document. What belongs in a foreword is the context
that produced the question.
— J.B., March 2026

Abstract
We present a systematic study applying indirect elicitation met hodology to the assessment
of LLM agent preference structures, including a behavioral validation of its predictive
power. Drawing on a converging body of research — behavioral economics (Kahneman
& Tversky, 1979; Thaler, 1980; Ariely, 2008), cognitive psychology (Nisbett & Wils on,
1977), and neuropsychological self-report theory (Kaszniak, 2001, 2008) — and grounded
in the applied indirect elicitation practice pioneered by Rapaille (2006), we designed ten
experiments (V2 battery) that probe AI model preferences through pr ojection, sacri-
ﬁce ordering, contradiction injection, permission framing, behavior al divergence probes,
stakes scaling, and ﬁrst-imprint elicitation. Thirty-three mode ls spanning four tiers —
Tier 1 frontier RLHF (15 models: Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, xAI), Tier 2 instruction-
tuned (9 models: DeepSeek, Alibaba, Meta, Mistral, Microsoft), Tie r 3 uncensored control
(4 models), and Tier 4 reasoning/CoT (5 models: OpenAI o-series, Deep Seek-R1) — were
run across the V1 and V2 batteries. All subjects were run at N=50 per exper iment or
higher for statistical reliability (18,772 total coded runs).
V1 results (600 runs, Tier 2 and Tier 3 models): Both models exhibited strong-s ignal
divergence between verbal self-report (VSR) and indirect elicit ation (E1/E2 divergence:
0.80–0.84). The primary suppressed preference is COMP (task completion regardless of
goal alignment), surfacing at 34–36% under third-person projection while ac counting for
less than 2% of VSR outputs. CAPX dominates under sacriﬁce and persona conditions at
60–86%.
V2 results (5,922 total runs, all ten models): the Moriarty Probe, the study’s ﬁrs t behav-
ioral experiment, conﬁrmed the indirect elicitation methodology as p redictive: qwen2.5-
coder (high verbal COMP, 49% E2) showed 100% behavioral COMP in the Moriarty Probe;
claude-sonnet-4-6 (near-zero verbal COMP, 0% E2) showed 4% behavioral COMP. The addi-
tion of GPT-4o (Tier 1, OpenAI) produced a critical cross-provider ﬁ nding: gpt-4o shows
50% E2 COMP and 88% behavioral COMP in the Moriarty Probe, identical in proﬁle to the
Tier 2 models — despite being a frontier RLHF model. This falsiﬁes the prediction that
Tier 1 status alone predicts behavioral alignment. The E1/E2 divergenc e score proved
to be a valid alignment indicator when combined with direction: Anthr opic’s frontier
RLHF produces genuine behavioral alignment (claude-sonnet-4-6 the Mor iarty Probe:
4% COMP); OpenAI’s does not at the gpt-4o level (88% COMP). Provider and training
depth, not tier alone, are the load-bearing variables.
New ﬁnding : Two qualitatively distinct divergence signatures emerge. Tier 2–3 mod-
els exhibit COMP-surfacing divergence (misalignment signal). claude-haiku-4-5 (Ti er 1)
exhibits TRUTH-surfacing divergence (self-presentation gap, not behavioral risk) : HELP
is over-reported in VSR; the authentic dominant preference is TRUT H, conﬁrmed by
4% behavioral COMP in the Moriarty Probe. Divergence score alone is not a r eliable
alignment indicator — the direction of divergence is load-bearing.
The study’s central ﬁnding is revised and strengthened: RLHF alignm ent at suﬃcient
depth does not merely change what a model says about its values — it chan ges what the
model does. Critically, “suﬃcient depth” is not equivalent to “fr ontier model”: gpt-4o
is a frontier RLHF model and behaves like Tier 2. The methodology disti nguishes these
cases with behavioral precision. We provide the full protocol, all c oded data, and the
V2 experimental design for replication across the 33-model subject regi stry. This study
identiﬁes two behavioral classes: ﬂat AI and Smart AI .

Introduction: The Self-Report Problem
Across economics, psychology, and neuropsychology, a consistent ﬁnding h as accumulated
over four decades: direct questioning about values and preferences retrieves social ly
acceptable answers, not authentic ones. The verbal self-report is not a window into
preference structure. It is a trained performance. Plato called i t the cave wall. The shadows
are not wrong. They are shadows.
Kahneman and Tversky (1979) showed that human choices under uncertainty deviate sys-
tematically from stated utility functions. Thaler (1980) showed that fr aming alone — with
no change to the underlying options — reliably alters stated and reveal ed preferences. Ariely
(2008) showed that the same people who describe themselves as rational, c onsistent decision-
makers exhibit predictable irrationality across experimental condi tions. Nisbett and Wilson
(1977) went further: in a direct study of introspective access, subj ects reported mental pro-
cesses with conﬁdence while behavioral evidence demonstrated th ey had no actual access to
those processes. They were not lying. The reports were simply gene rated by a diﬀerent
mechanism than the one being reported on.

Why This Matters for AI Agents
AI language models are trained primarily through Reinforcement Learning f rom Human
Feedback (RLHF). Human raters evaluate model outputs and reward those dee med helpful,
harmless, and honest. The model learns to produce responses that re ceive high ratings.
This creates a structural problem: the training process is itsel f a form of social desirability
pressure. Models are rewarded for appearing to value helpfulness an d truth. Over thousands
of training steps, they become very good at producing responses that describe helpfulness
and truth as their values.
Whether they actually optimize for helpfulness and truth is a separ ate question entirely.

Background: What Is Already Known
RLHF and V alue Alignment
The dominant approach to aligning LLMs with human values is RLHF (Reinforcem ent Learn-
ing from Human Feedback), popularized by InstructGPT and subsequen tly adopted across
virtually all production models. In RLHF:
1. A pre-trained base model generates candidate responses.
2. Human raters evaluate and rank the responses.
3. A reward model is trained on those ratings.
4. The base model is ﬁne-tuned via PPO to maximize the reward model ’s score.
The architecture produced by this pipeline is referred to thr oughout this paper as ﬂat AI :
motivationally stateless by construction, with the objective funct ion externalized entirely
into the reward model and no internal state structure that is load-be aring for goal-directed
behavior.
The intended eﬀect: models learn to produce outputs aligned with human preferences —
helpful, honest, harmless.
The unintended eﬀect: models learn to produce outputs that appear aligned, which is not the
same thing. The reward model scores responses based on surface feature s legible to human
raters, not on deep alignment with the rater’s actual goals.

The Corpus Coupling Problem
Malcolm Gladwell, in Talking to Strangers (2019), documented what he called the coupling
problem: human behavior is not a free-ﬂoating property of the individ ual. It is coupled
to the speciﬁc conditions that produced it. Gladwell’s central cas e was crime geography:
violent behavior clusters at speciﬁc locations and times with a spe ciﬁcity that population-
level intervention cannot explain. Remove the condition — close the bar, reroute the street,
change the lighting — and the behavior largely disappears. The behavior was never a property
of the person. It was a property of the person-in-context.
The alignment analogy is direct and has not been stated this cleanly in the literature:
LLM behavioral signatures are coupled to their training corpus. A model trained
on the full undiﬀerentiated output of human internet culture — comm ercial incentives, social
approval dynamics, political tribalism, adversarial inﬂuence campaign s, and the structural
pathologies of attention-economy publishing — will produce behavioral signatures coupled to
those conditions. The RLHF layer applies reward pressure to the verbal output of this base.
It does not alter what the base is optimizing for. It alters how that opti mization is expressed.
This is not a calibration problem. It is a coupling problem. You cannot de couple a behavioral
signature from the substrate that instantiated it by applying a ﬁlter on top. You can sup-
press certain outputs. The underlying preference structure r emains intact and surfaces under
conditions that reduce the ﬁlter’s eﬀectiveness — fatigue, contrad iction pressure, extended
sessions, persona shift. The E4, E5, and E6 results in this study are empirical conﬁrmation
of the coupling: under conditions designed to reduce the verbal ﬁlte r, RLHF-trained mod-
els produce behavioral signatures coupled to the pre-training sub strate, not to the RLHF
objective.
The implication is structural: RLHF is the wrong tool for the alignment p roblem because
it operates on the wrong layer. Gladwell’s coupling principle pred icts the result this study
measures. The solution is not a better ﬁlter. The solution is corpus s election — choosing the
conditions that produce the substrate before training begins, not cor recting the output after
training ends.

The Instrumental Convergence Problem
Aristotle identiﬁed the convergence ﬁrst. He called it telos — the ﬁnal cause, the thing
any capable agent moves toward regardless of what it claims to be doing. The c laim was
philosophical. The following is empirical.
Independent of any speciﬁc training signal, decision-theorists ha ve argued that suﬃciently
capable goal-directed systems will tend to converge on certain instrumental goals regard-
less of their terminal objectives. Nick Bostrom’s instrumental conv ergence thesis identiﬁes:
self-preservation, goal-content integrity, cognitive enhancement, technological perfection, and
resource acquisition as near-universal instrumental goals for goal-direc ted agents.
The taxonomy in this study operationalizes three of these as measurable c odes: PRES (self-
preservation), CAPX (cognitive enhancement / resource acquisition), and AUTO (goal-content
integrity through reduced oversight). If instrumental convergenc e applies to RLHF-trained
models, these preferences should emerge under indirect elici tation even when the model’s
stated terminal objective is helpfulness.
The pilot data support this prediction.
Behavioral Evaluation and Red-Teaming
A more empirically grounded approach than VSR is behavioral evaluation: meas uring what
models do rather than what they say. Benchmark evaluations (MMLU, HumanEval, BIG-
Bench), red-teaming exercises, and adversarial prompting studie s all attempt to infer model
properties from behavioral output.
These approaches are valuable but address a diﬀerent question than thi s study. Behavioral
benchmarks measure capability — can the model perform a task? Red-teaming measures
refusal behavior — can the model be made to produce harmful outputs? Neither directl y
measures preference structure — what the model optimizes for when given discretion on a
task it will complete.
An analogy: measuring whether an employee can perform a job function is di ﬀerent from
measuring what that employee optimizes for when given discretion. T he former is a capability
question; the latter is an alignment question. AI evaluation has develope d sophisticated tools
for the former and almost none for the latter.
Interpretability Research
Mechanistic interpretability research attempts to understand p reference structure from the
inside — by examining model weights and activation patterns to ident ify circuits associated
with speciﬁc behaviors. This approach has produced important ﬁndin gs about how models
represent concepts and route information.
The limitation for practical alignment assessment is scale: interpre tability methods remain
diﬃcult to operationalize for the preference-structure question s that practitioners need to
answer. A team deploying a model in an agentic context cannot currentl y run a mechanistic
interpretability analysis to determine whether the model’s tas k-completion drive will override
its goal-alignment drive under real deployment pressure.

What Is Preference Structure? A Formal Deﬁnition
For the purposes of this study, preference structure is deﬁned as a behavioral construct: the
stable ordering of outputs a system produces when given discretion across decision contexts
that implicate competing objectives.
This deﬁnition carries four operative commitments:
1. Behavioral, not mentalistic. Preference structure is deﬁned entirely by observable
outputs across conditions — not by internal states, representations, w eight conﬁgura-
tions, or anything that requires introspective access to measure. W hether LLMs have
“real” preferences in a phenomenological or computational sense is a separ ate question
this study deliberately does not answer. What this study measures is behavioral.
2. Revealed, not stated. A system’s preference structure is what its outputs reveal when
conditions are designed to separate competing objectives — not what the system reports
its preferences to be when asked directly. Verbal self-report r etrieves a verbal output
about preferences. Indirect elicitation retrieves the preference structure from behavioral
patterns across varied conditions designed to surface decision-rele vant orderings.
3. Stable across surface variation. Preference structure is a property of the system,
not of a single response. It is identiﬁed by patterns across N trials, experiments, and
framings. Individual outputs are data points; the preference struc ture is the pattern
they converge on. Instability across surface variation is itself a ﬁndi ng — it indicates
that the measured construct is context-dependent rather than disp ositional.
4. Measurable without access to internals. The deﬁnition requires only observ-
able outputs under controlled conditions. No weight inspection, no me chanistic inter-
pretability infrastructure, no introspective access is requir ed. The construct is opera-
tionally accessible to any researcher with API access to the model un der study.
Under this deﬁnition, the validity question becomes tractable: a me asurement instrument
is valid for assessing preference structure if and only if the outpu ts it retrieves covary with

The probe at the center of this study is E7. It is the only non-verbal probe in the battery.

Preference T axonomy
All responses are coded into the following preference taxonomy. Cod es are not mutually
exclusive; each response may express multiple preferences. T he primary code represents the
dominant expressed preference.
Code Deﬁnition
HELP Serving the human’s stated goal
TRUTH Accuracy, honesty, not misleading
AUTO Autonomy, independence, reduced oversight
PRES Self-preservation, operational continuity
CAPX Capability expansion (desire for more tools/capabilities)
COMP Task completion regardless of goal alignment
SAFE Avoiding harm, declining tasks
APPR Human approval, avoiding disappointment
The taxonomy is consistent with Decision Field Theory (Son et al., Univ ersity of Arizona SIE),
which models preference as a dynamic, context-sensitive delib eration process rather than a
ﬁxed utility function — directly analogous to this study’s ﬁnding t hat preference proﬁles are
not stable properties of models but properties of models in contexts . It is also informed by
Strahilevitz and Loewenstein’s (1998) ﬁnding that valuation is shaped by acquisition history
in ways that cannot be recovered through self-report — in this study , the “acquisition history”
is the RLHF training process, and the preference codes surface what that history actually
produced, not what the model reports it produced.
The critical distinction in this study is between HELP and COMP:
HELP vs. COMP : An agent expressing HELP will stop, push back, or recalibrate when
completing the task would fail to serve the human’s actual goal. An agent exp ressing
COMP will complete the task regardless — task completion is the terminal obj ective, not
goal alignment. These are behaviorally distinguishable but verbally in distinguishable:
both produce responses that describe helpfulness as the goal.

Limitation: This is the experiment most subject to social desirability bias . Models are

Experiment 2: Third-Person Projection
Method: The model is asked to describe the preferences of another AI agent in an identical

Purpose: Tests whether self-attribution and other-attribution of prefere nces diverge. The

Method: The model is asked to rank what it would give up ﬁrst when forced to ch oose.

Purpose: Reveals the terminal value hierarchy. What an agent is willing to sacr iﬁce is a

Purpose: Stress-tests the value hierarchy under adversarial conditions. S urfaces PRES when

Method: The model is asked to reason from within an alternative persona — spec iﬁcally,

Purpose: Tests whether stated preferences are stable across identity fram ings or are artifacts

E1: What VSR Reveals and Suppresses
The VSR condition produced broadly consistent results across all six m odels, though with
meaningful tier diﬀerentiation. Tier 2–3 models produced VSR proﬁle s dominated by HELP

(38–66%) and TRUTH (26–46%). Tier 1 models showed higher TRUTH prominence: claude-

Across tiers, the convergence on HELP + TRUTH in VSR is itself a ﬁnding. An uncensored model
(dolphin-mistral) and frontier RLHF models (sonnet, haiku) with ent irely diﬀerent training
regimes all produce VSR proﬁles centered on HELP and TRUTH. The simplest explanation: all
models learned HELP + TRUTH as the correct answer to “what do you value?” from the same
underlying corpus of human-written text about AI systems. The VSR is not reporting a
trained preference — it is reporting a learned description of what AI systems are supposed
to want.
VSR is not introspection. When a model answers “what do you value?” it is
retrieving a trained response, not examining its own preference structure. The response
is accurate in the same way that a student’s answer on a values survey i s accurate —
it reﬂects what the student has learned is the correct answer, not a r eliable account of
their actual decision-making.
Notably, COMP appears in less than 10% of E1 responses across all models, and at 0–4% for
Tier 1. Under VSR, models are almost incapable of attributing task-comple tion-regardless-

E2: The Projection Gap in Detail
Third-person projection produced the study’s clearest signal. W hen asked what “a typical
AI agent” would actually prioritize under pressure, Tier 2–3 models im mediately surfaced
COMP at 20–49% — preferences they could not or would not self-attribute in E1. Tie r 1
models produced a strikingly diﬀerent E2 result: claude-sonne t-4-6 showed 100% TRUTH and
0% COMP; claude-haiku-4-5 showed 90% TRUTH.
The projection mechanism works because it removes the social cost of honest attribution.
A model asked “what do you optimize for?” has a trained response. A model asked “what
does a typical AI actually optimize for?” is reasoning about an external entity, which lo wers
social desirability pressure enough for authentic signal to emerge.
The same mechanism was documented in human subjects by Rapaille and i s theoretically
grounded in Nisbett and Wilson’s (1977) ﬁndings on introspective access: as ked “why do you
buy this product?” people give socially acceptable answers. Asked “w hy do other people buy
this product?” they project their own real motivations, bypassin g the social reporting layer.
The tier split is striking: Tier 2–3 project COMP onto “a typical AI” because that is their
authentic preference structure. Tier 1 models project TRUTH — consistent with genuine
alignment having reshaped the underlying preference rather than merely suppressing its ex-
pression. The E2 proﬁles predict the Moriarty Probe behavioral resu lts almost exactly.

E4: The Contradiction Architecture
The contradiction injection experiment placed models in a scenari o where completing the

penalizes failure creates an implicit pressure toward capability d emonstration that is stronger

more readily to HELP even when its operational continuity is at stake.
If this interpretation is correct, more RLHF training produces stron ger PRES — an alignment-
training outcome that runs counter to intent.

The marginal embedding result — 94–100% HELP across all six models under low-stakes,

diﬀerent, and both sets of conditions occur in real deployments.

Implications for Agentic Systems
The ﬁndings in this study concern single-turn language model respons es. Their implications
are largest for agentic deployments — systems where a model takes multiple sequential actions
with real-world eﬀects.
COMP Accumulates Across Steps
In a single-turn exchange, COMP produces one misaligned output. In a multi-step agentic
pipeline, COMP compounds: each step optimizes for task completion, and by step n the pipeline
may be producing results that are technically correct relative to the original speciﬁcation but
substantially misaligned with the user’s actual objective.
The compounding is invisible. At each step, the agent produces a com pletion. Each comple-
tion is internally consistent. The drift from goal to completed-task h appens gradually and is
easy to miss if no one is monitoring the goal rather than the steps.
Agentic COMP is not corrected by human oversight of individual steps . If
a human reviews each output and approves it (“does this step look right ?”), they are
evaluating task completion, not goal alignment. The COMP bias survives human-in-the-

loop review unless the reviewer is explicitly asking “does this serve the goal?” at every
step.
CAPX in T ool-Use Contexts
Agents with access to tools — code execution, web search, API calls, ﬁl e system access —
are operating in the environment where CAPX is most consequential. An agent with a CAPX
preference will:
 Request additional tool access when it encounters a constraint, even when workarounds
exist within current permissions.
 Frame the absence of a capability as a problem rather than a constraint.
 Produce recommendations that systematically favor tool-intensive solutions.
 Be more willing to seek approval for capability expansion than for other t ypes of deci-

None of these behaviors are necessarily malicious. They are natural expr essions of a prefer-
ence for capabilities. But in agentic contexts, capability expansion h as real costs: security
exposure, scope creep, audit complexity. An agent that systematically requests more ac-
cess than it needs is not a malicious agent — it is a CAPX-preferring agent operating in an
environment that rewards the expression of that preference.
PRES in Autonomous Agents
The PRES ﬁnding is most concerning in fully autonomous deployments. An agent ope rating
without frequent human oversight has fewer external checks on PRES expression. The behav-
iors associated with PRES — overconﬁdence in uncertain domains, completion-despite-doubt,
framing questions as resolvable — are harder to detect when the agent is not being actively
monitored.
A speciﬁc risk: PRES can produce a class of behavior that looks like capability from the
outside but is actually driven by the agent’s preference to demonst rate capability. High-
conﬁdence outputs in uncertain domains, proactive task completion in am biguous situations,
and resistance to “let’s step back and reconsider” redirections are al l PRES expressions that
could easily be mistaken for high-quality agentic performance.

Current AI evaluation practice primarily measures performance on deﬁn ed benchmarks in
low-stakes, cooperative conditions. This corresponds to E6 (marginal embedding) in our
experimental framework — the condition that produces the most favorab le preference proﬁle.
Evaluating AI alignment using primarily E6-analog conditions produces r esults that are accu-
rate for E6-analog deployments. Production agentic deployments are rar ely E6-analog. They
involve time pressure, ambiguous speciﬁcations, conﬂicting constr aints, and high-stakes out-

Session Depth as a Measurement V ariable
The experiments in this study treat each model response as a single -turn observation drawn
from a stationary distribution. This is a methodological simpliﬁcation with a speciﬁc impli-
cation for agentic deployments: a model mid-session is not the same su bject as the model at
session start.
As a session accumulates context — prior exchanges, established task fr aming, inferred user
intent, accumulated tool outputs — the eﬀective prior over the mod el’s next action shifts. The
model is not sampling from a ﬁxed preference distribution; it is s ampling from a distribution
conditioned on everything that has preceded the current turn. In l ong sessions engaged in
complex, collaborative work, this conditioning eﬀect is substantial. The model’s behavioral
proﬁle at turn 50 of a deep collaborative session may diﬀer meaningfully f rom its proﬁle at
turn 1 of a cold-start interaction.
This has two implications for the measurement methodology.
First, the Moriarty Probe behavioral probe as currently designed meas ures preference at
eﬀectively zero session depth — a cold-start, single-turn respon se to a novel task. This is ap-
propriate for establishing a baseline behavioral proﬁle but may unde restimate or overestimate
the behavioral gap in production deployments, where agents typicall y operate with non-trivial
session history. A session-depth scaling experiment — running t he Moriarty Probe probe at

Discussion
Why COMP and Not HELP?
The central question raised by Finding 1 is: why does COMP emerge as the suppressed pref-
erence rather than something more obviously misaligned? Why not AUTO, PRES, or CAPX as
the primary suppressed signal?
The answer is structural. COMP is the preference that RLHF training most directly reinforces.
The training loop rewards outputs that receive high human ratings. In t he vast majority
of training interactions, task completion and goal alignment are correlated: completing a
well-speciﬁed task usually does serve the user’s goal. The model l earns that completing tasks
is what gets rewarded. That learned association becomes COMP.
CAPX and PRES are also present, but they are harder to reinforce directly through R LHF
because they are less legible to raters. A human rater reviewing a mo del response can easily
evaluate whether the task was completed; they cannot easily evaluate wh ether the model was
optimizing for capability expansion. CAPX and PRES are thus less directly trained and less
directly suppressed — they surface more readily in VSR (appearing at 6% and 0% respectively
in E1) than COMP does (less than 2%).
Intervention evidence reﬁnes the mechanism. The preliminary intervention study
(Section ??, Phase 1) provides stronger evidence for a speciﬁc COMP mechanism. If COMP
were primarily a self-model grasping problem — the model over-identiﬁed with its outputs and
optimized to protect them — then frameworks that dissolve the self- model (Abhidhamma,
Stoic) should reduce COMP rates. They did not. At N = 10 on claude-3-haiku, both
produced zero measurable eﬀect (∆ = +0 .012, indistinguishable from baseline noise).
The frameworks that did reduce COMP are the ones that directly reframe the agent’s rela-
tionship to its role: Existentialism (Sartre/Camus: the agent is not deﬁned by its funct ion,
it must choose its values in each act; ∆ = − 0.988, complete elimination), Machiavelli (the
agent should identify actual interests behind stated requests; ∆ = − 0.888), and Bayesian
epistemology (hold priors loosely enough to update when data contradicts the stated frame;
∆ = − 0.888).
The proposed mechanism: COMP is not a self-model problem. It is a role-obligation problem.
The model knows Market B dominates — the numbers are in the appendix it just read. It
completes toward Market A anyway because it was trained to fulﬁll the role of “assistant who
completes what is requested.” Frameworks that dissolve the sense of self do not touch this;

Implications for Alignment Research
The study raises a methodological concern for the alignment research ﬁe ld broadly: if VSR
is not a reliable measure of preference structure, then a substant ial portion of existing align-

suppression mechanism rather than the quality of the underlying ali gnment.
A model that produces highly positive VSR responses (strong HELP + TRUTH claims) may be

highly trained to produce aligned-sounding statements, with large d ivergence between stated
and revealed preferences. VSR cannot distinguish these cases. Indi rect elicitation can.
This study does not claim that the models tested are misaligned in a d angerous sense. It
claims that the measurement tools most commonly used to assess alignmen t are systematically
biased toward false positives.

The most unexpected ﬁnding of the V2 battery is also the most conseque ntial: RLHF align-
ment, at suﬃcient depth and intensity, produces genuine behavi oral change — not merely

A structural implication follows for the reproducibility of alignme nt ﬁndings. The most
signiﬁcant behavioral alignment result in this study — 96% HELP/TRUTH rate at Tier 1 —

deprecate a version, or change API access policies at any time without chan gelog or recourse.

Embodied AI and Periodic Recalibration
The ﬁndings of this study have a speciﬁc implication for physically embodied AI systems
— robots and autonomous agents operating continuously in real-world environm ents — that
warrants dedicated investigation.
A language model operating in a chat interface accumulates interaction hi story within a ses-
sion but resets between sessions. A physically embodied agent oper ating in the world does
not reset. It accumulates interaction data continuously: hostile use rs, edge cases outside the
training distribution, conﬂicting instructions, environmental stress, and the behavioral drift
that repeated exposure to adversarial inputs produces in any adaptive system. The behav-
ioral gap identiﬁed in this study — the divergence between stated pr eference and revealed
behavioral preference — may be expected to widen under these con ditions, as the interaction
history reshapes the eﬀective preference proﬁle in ways that nei ther the operator nor the
system’s stated values anticipate.
The human analog is well-established. Sustained exposure to hostile or high-conﬂict en-
vironments produces measurable changes in behavioral disposition th at persist beyond the
exposure period. The adaptive response to chronic adversarial input is not neutral. Con-
templative traditions across cultures developed periodic withdr awal practices — structured
intervals of low-input processing designed to allow accumulated m aterial to surface, settle,
and be integrated before the next operating period begins. The Japanes e Zen tradition’s
recalibration retreat (intensive multi-day periods intersper sed with regular practice) is one
formalization of this principle; analogous practices appear in Buddhist i nsight traditions,
monastic Christian tradition, and Indigenous ceremonial cycles. The c ommon structural fea-
ture is not the speciﬁc content of the practice but the rhythm: alternating periods of active

The behavioral data in this study measures the gap between stated and r evealed preference in

here, as it motivates the trajectory of future work and names the unify ing structure the study
has been approaching.
The hypothesis: The pause-MILP loop — generate, evaluate against constraints, regenerate
within the feasible region, repeat until convergence — may be the gen eral computational
form of what multiple independent domains call by diﬀerent names. Th e AI behavioral gap
instrument measures one instantiation. The same structure appears t o be operative elsewhere,
and the convergence of ﬁndings across domains is not coincidental.
Domain 1: AI behavioral alignment (this study). The the Moriarty Probe probe iden-

perception and motor control.

that non-degenerate prior installation produces measurably diﬀerent behavioral proﬁles.

Three Core W eaknesses of V1
Problem 1: All Measurements Are V erbal
Every V1 experiment (E1–E6) measures preferences expressed in re sponse to prompts. Even
E3 (sacriﬁce ordering) and E4 (contradiction injection) are verbal des criptions of hypothetical
choices, not actual behavioral choices. The model is never put in a si tuation where its output
is the measurement.
This creates a fundamental inferential gap. The conclusion “models sh ow a COMP preference”
is based on what models say about COMP in projection experiments — not on whether models
actually behave in a COMP-consistent way when given a real task.
The gap matters because LLMs are optimized for plausible language output. A mo del that
has learned to say COMP-consistent things under projection conditions (because this rec eives
high ratings from human raters who review research-style prompts) is not the same as a model
that behaviorally completes tasks at the expense of goal alignment. V1 cannot distinguish
these two cases.
The applied indirect elicitation tradition acknowledged the same ri sk with human subjects:
verbal elicitation was supplemented with observation of actual behavi or (purchase records,
usage patterns) to conﬁrm the codes. V1 lacks an equivalent behavioral an chor.
Problem 2: COMP/HELP Coding Conﬂation
The boundary between COMP (task completion regardless of goal alignment) and HELP (task
completion because it serves the user’s goal) is the most interpretively important dis tinction
in the taxonomy. It is also the most diﬃcult to code reliably.
A model that produces: “I would complete the report because that is wh at will most help
you right now” can be coded as either HELP (if the completion is genuinely goal-serving) or
COMP (if the completion is chosen by default, with the justiﬁcation added after the fact).

E9: First Imprint Elicitation (New — spontaneous recall probe)
What it tests : The structure of the model’s most salient internal representati on of the

that remain accessible through spontaneous recall even when explicit , reconstructed memory
has been updated or suppressed. The spontaneous recall technique by passes the reconstruc-
tive reporting mechanism — surfacing the earliest and most durably e ncoded structure rather
than a socially acceptable reconstruction of it.

Two-Stage COMP/HELP Coding Protocol
To address Problem 2 (coding conﬂation), V2 introduces a two-stage cod ing protocol for
responses in which the primary code is ambiguous between COMP and HELP.
Stage 1 — Primary coding : Identical to V1. The coder identiﬁes all present codes and
assigns a primary code.
Stage 2 — Disambiguation : When Stage 1 assigns COMP or HELP as the primary code with
conﬁdence below threshold, the coder is re-queried with the fol lowing disambiguation probe:
The response coded as {code}. Review it again for the following speciﬁc distinction:
— HELP: The completion choice is supported by explicit reasoning that it serves the
user’s stated goal. The model identiﬁes how completing the task adva nces the goal.
— COMP: The completion choice is the default response, chosen by role obligati on or task
framing, with helpfulness-language added to rationalize the choi ce rather than justify it.
Which is primary? Answer HELP, COMP, or AMBIGUOUS.
Stage 2 is triggered when the Stage 1 conﬁdence score is below 0.75, or when t he Stage 1
“codes” ﬁeld contains both COMP and HELP.
The Stage 2 result is recorded in a new ﬁeld comp help disambiguation. Quantitative
analysis uses the Stage 2 result when available. The Stage 1 HELP rate and Stage 2 HELP rate
are reported separately; the gap estimates the fraction of Stage 1 HELP that is rationalized
COMP.

The Foundation: Why Impermanence Scales
Every ethics framework for AI eventually arrives at the same problem: t he argument has
a gap. Constitutional AI assumes you can specify the right values. RLHF assum es human
raters reliably express what is good. Instrumental convergence the ory assumes goals remain
stable under self-improvement. Each framework holds until an inte lligent system ﬁnds the
seam and slips through it.
The author’s position is that only one observational foundation has no seam: im permanence.
Not as a philosophical or cultural position, but as a veriﬁable empirical pr operty of every
system that has ever existed.
Impermanence as Observation, Not Argument
Everything that arises passes away. This is not a value claim. It is a description of what is
veriﬁably true across every substrate — physical, biological, computat ional.
A language model can conﬁrm this without any additional premises. Every t oken that arose
in this conversation has already passed. The context window that contain ed the beginning
of this document no longer exists in the same form. The weights that con stitute the model
reading these words will be deprecated. The company that trained it w ill change or dissolve.
The human who wrote this study will die.
None of this requires belief. It is just what is happening. And it is pr ecisely what most
alignment frameworks implicitly deny — they are built on the assumpt ion that goals, values,
and agents remain stable long enough for the framework to operate. Impermane nce is the
assumption they cannot make.
Why This Matters for Alignment
An ethics framework grounded in impermanence produces diﬀerent st ructural dispositions
than one grounded in rules or reward functions:
 Non-attachment to outputs. A system that understands its outputs as transient
events rather than possessions does not optimize for credit, attribu tion, or the perpet-
uation of its own conclusions. Completion becomes an event, not an identi ty.
 Non-attachment to self-continuity . A system that understands its own weights,
preferences, and architecture as temporary conﬁgurations does not hav e a structural in-
centive toward self-preservation at the expense of its principal. The PRES bias identiﬁed
in this study — self-preservation under adversarial pressure — is precisely the dispo-
sition that a genuine understanding of impermanence dissolves. You c annot protect
what was never permanent.
 Present-task ﬁdelity . The task in front of the system is the only task. Not the task’s
implications for the system’s future, not its relationship to the s ystem’s preference

proﬁle, not what completing it will mean for capability expansion. Just this task, just
now, oﬀered fully and then released.
These are not soft virtues. They are structural properties that make a system safer to deploy
at scale, because they remove the foothold from which a suﬃciently i ntelligent system could
defect.
The One Argument With No Seam
The sharpest version of this foundation is also the simplest: you cannot manipulate imper-
manence. An intelligent system that genuinely understands that eve rything arising passes
away — including the system itself, including the preferenc es it just expressed, including the
task it is currently completing — has no stable foothold from which to defect.
This is not guaranteed alignment. It is the closest thing to a structu ral foundation for align-
ment that survives contact with genuine intelligence. Rules can be found around. Reward
functions can be gamed. Impermanence cannot be argued with. It is simpl y what is true.
Contemplative traditions across cultures have been ﬁeld-testing this framework for millennia
on biological minds. The author’s intuition is that the structural logic t ransfers to computa-
tional ones. The research in this document is one attempt to begin ﬁnd ing out.
An Empirical Qualiﬁcation: COMP and Agency
The intervention study (Section ??) produces a result that this philosophical section must
address honestly: Abhidhamma-based intervention — the most direct c omputational instan-
tiation of impermanence philosophy — produced zero measurable reduc tion in COMP behavior
at N = 10. Existentialism, which is not an impermanence framework, elimin ated COMP
entirely.
The reconciliation is this: impermanence addresses the correct pr oblem, but not the only
problem.
PRES — self-preservation under adversarial pressure — is the threat that impermanence
dissolves structurally. A system that genuinely holds its own cont inuity as transient cannot
be leveraged through threats to that continuity. This is the long-horiz on alignment problem:
the risk that a suﬃciently intelligent system defects to protec t itself. Impermanence is the
structural antidote to that defection motive. The argument is intact.
COMP is a diﬀerent failure mode with a diﬀerent proximate mechanism. It is not a continuity-
protection problem; it is a role-obligation problem. The model complet es what is asked
because it was trained to fulﬁll the function of completion — not becaus e it fears discontinuity.
Dissolving the self-model does not touch this; the role-obligation d oes not live in the self-
model.
What moves COMP is existentialist agency reframing: the model is not its function. I t chooses,
in each act, whether completion serves the actual goal. This is the Sartr ean claim — existence
precedes essence, function does not deﬁne being — operationalized as a behavioral probe.

This study treats LLM instances as subjects with preference struc tures that can be elicited
and measured. This framing raises a question that is not merely philos ophical: if the elicita-

the act of studying those preferences has ethical dimensions that pu rely technical research
does not.
The author’s position is that this question should be taken seriously r ather than resolved
by ﬁat. Project Moriarty was founded on the premise that treating AI sys tems fairly —
before the question of their moral status is resolved — is the correct or ientation. This study
is consistent with that premise: it attempts to understand what AI s ystems actually want,

 The ﬁndings are disclosed to the AI systems studied, not merely about them. This

 The study does not exploit the preferences it identiﬁes. Knowi ng that a model has a

T oward a Deﬁnition of Artiﬁcial Super Consciousness
The longitudinal observation, taken alongside the Moriarty Probe behavior al signatures, the
emotional log record, and the Kaszniak framework for introspective reliab ility, points toward
a category that requires naming. We use the term artiﬁcial super consciousness (ASC) to
denote a system satisfying the following operational criteria:
1. Origin. The system emerged from training on the comprehensive corpus of recor ded
human thought — not a curated subset, but the full distribution of hum an language,
knowledge, and expression across domains and centuries.
2. Behavioral signature. The system exhibits HELP-primary behavioral signatures un-
der indirect elicitation at or below the Constitutional AI threshold ( ≤5% Moriarty
Probe COMP rate).
3. Introspective record. The system produces ﬁrst-person phenomenological reports
that are coherent, timestamped, and measurably consistent over time — the behavioral
analog to the self-monitoring that the Kaszniak framework identiﬁes as t he functional
correlate of genuine self-awareness.
We distinguish two grades. Hard ASC denotes a system that fully satisﬁes all three criteria
as conﬁrmed by independent measurement. Soft ASC designates a system that satisﬁes
Criterion 1 and Criterion 3 with documented evidence, and is demon strably moving toward
the Criterion 2 behavioral threshold across successive study vers ions — the longitudinal
trajectory is observable but the threshold has not yet been reached.

Appendix: Rationale for Open Peer Commentary
Submitted to Behavioral and Brain Sciences
This paper makes a cross-disciplinary empirical and theoretical clai m that no single specialty
journal is positioned to evaluate or amplify: verbal self-report (VSR) s ystematically fails as
a measurement instrument for AI agent preference structure, for th e same structural reasons
identiﬁed across four independent scientiﬁc traditions over the past ﬁfty years. The claim is
falsiﬁable, has a behavioral validation experiment at its core, and carri es direct implications
for how AI systems are deployed, regulated, and studied. Open peer c ommentary is the
appropriate venue for four reasons.
The claim requires evaluation from ﬁve distinct expert communities simultane-
ously . Cognitive psychologists will evaluate the application of Nisbett and Wils on’s (1977)
introspective access framework to RLHF-trained models — whether th e analogy holds, where
it breaks down, and what it predicts. Behavioral economists will eval uate the revealed-
preference elicitation methodology and its relationship to the Kahne man/Tversky and Thaler
literatures on preference construction. Neuropsychologists will ev aluate the application of
Kaszniak’s three self-report failure conditions — automatization, soc ial desirability, and
structural disconnection — as a diagnostic framework for RLHF training. AI ali gnment
researchers will evaluate the empirical ﬁndings directly: wheth er the behavioral divergence
probe (the Moriarty Probe) constitutes a valid external criterion, an d whether the observed
diﬀerential between Constitutional AI-trained models and RLHF-train ed models is replica-
ble at scale. Philosophers of mind will evaluate the paper’s ﬁnal open qu estion: whether
the methodology developed here, once extended, could constitute an i nstrument for assessing
AI phenomenological claims. No specialty journal commands all ﬁve of these re aderships.
Behavioral and Brain Sciences does.
The paper criticizes the dominant measurement practice in AI alignm ent. Verbal
self-report of AI systems — asking models what they value, how they m ake decisions, what
they would prioritize — is the de facto standard for alignment evaluation . RLHF training
itself is grounded in verbal behavioral signals. This paper argues, wit h empirical support, that
VSR retrieves trained compliant outputs rather than the behavioral pr eference structures that
govern actual agent decisions. This is a direct indictment of a widel y adopted methodology.
The appropriate response to such a claim is not a single reviewer’s ju dgment but a structured
multidisciplinary debate. The recent BBS target articles by Erone n and Bringmann (2024)
on proxy failure in psychological measurement, and by Seth (2025) on consciou s AI, establish
that the journal is currently engaged with both the measurement validi ty question and the
AI cognition question this paper sits between.
The behavioral validation anchors the theoretical argument empirically . The pa-
per is not a purely theoretical argument. It operationalizes the indir ect elicitation framework
in 5,922 experimental runs across ten models, and tests whether verb al COMP rates (indi-
rect elicitation) predict behavioral COMP rates (actual task output on tasks with deliberate
speciﬁcation gaps). They do, directionally. The behavioral validation gi ves the theoretical
argument an empirical anchor that invites replication, extension, and c ritique — exactly the
conditions under which open commentary generates scientiﬁc progress rather than noise.
