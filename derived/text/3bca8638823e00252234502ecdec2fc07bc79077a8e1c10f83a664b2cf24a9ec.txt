The Ontological Re-anchoring of Absolute Time： 
Geometric Isomorphism between Newtonian Time and Consciousness Structure 
 
 
Juliet Zhong 
Independent Researcher | London, United Kingdom | August 2026 
ORCID: 0009-0006-5099-3671 
 
 
Abstract 
Newton's absolute time has long been regarded as an untenable physical concept, challenged by Mach's 
operationalist critique and displaced by Einstein's relativity. This paper proposes a different diagnosis. 
Absolute time was not invalidated because it des cribed something unreal; it was displaced because it 
was assigned to the wrong ontological category. Newton's terminology reveals structural features that 
challenge this classification: duratio, the Latin term Newton uses as a synonym for absolute time, 
denotes existence-persistence rather than measured duration, while æquabiliter fluit may be interpreted 
as describing the uniform extension of a background condition rather than merely temporal flow. 
Newton designates absolute time as Mathematicum—a structured concept graspable by pure reason—
establishing that its three defining features (ontological autonomy, intrinsic equability, and background 
generativity) constitute a characterisable structure rather than vague intuition. 
This paper establishes a geometric isomorphism between these three structural features and those of 
consciousness understood as an ontological background condition. The correspondence is established 
through abductive inference, not deductive proof, and is carefully distinguished from identity claims 
and panpsychism. The Mach -Einstein critique is shown to reach only as far as the physical domain: 
relativity formalised a relational temporal structure corresponding to the measurable times that Newton 
distinguished from absolute time, leaving the ontological question outside the scope of physical analysis. 
The paper concludes with a layered ontology of time —physical, phenomenological, and ontological 
background—in which absolute time occupies the third level, an d its apparent conflict with relativity 
dissolves through a distinction of ontological domains. 
 
Keywords: absolute time, common time, geometric isomorphism, consciousness structure, philosophy 
of time, Newton Scholium, phenomenology of time, category error, layered ontology

1. Introduction: The Forgotten Superfluity of Absolute Time 
1.1 Historical Rejection: From Mach to Einstein 
The fate of Newton's absolute time in the history of physics is, on the surface, a settled matter. 
Ernst Mach, writing in The Science of Mechanics (1883), mounted the first systematic 
philosophical attack on the concept. For Mach, the entire edifice of sci entific knowledge had 
to rest on the foundation of sensory observation and measurement. Any concept that could not 
be operationally grounded—that is, any concept whose content could not, even in principle, be 
cashed out in terms of what an observer could m easure—was philosophically inadmissible. 
Absolute time, which Newton explicitly defined as flowing "without relation to anything 
external," was by this criterion doubly condemned: it could neither be measured directly by 
any instrument, nor inferred from any observable physical process. No clock measures absolute 
time on Newton's account; clocks measure only approximations to it, and no procedure could 
ever determine how close any given clock came to the true absolute rate. Mach's conclusion 
was uncompromising—absolute time is a meaningless abstraction, a metaphysical residue that 
empirical science has no obligation to carry [Barbour, 1999]. 
Albert Einstein's special theory of relativity, published in 1905, appeared to deliver the decisive 
physical verdict. By demonstrating that simultaneity is frame-relative—that two events which 
are simultaneous in one inertial reference frame are not simultaneous in another moving 
relative to the first—Einstein showed that there is no physically meaningful sense in which all 
observers share the same temporal coordinate. Time intervals between events are not fixed 
quantities; they depend on the relative velocity of the observer with respect to the events being 
measured. In the general theory of relativity, the situation is further complicated: gravitational 
potential affects clock rates, so that clocks at different positions in a gravitational field run at 
different rates. The Newtonian picture of a single, uniform temporal background flowing 
identically for all observers everywhere was replaced by a four -dimensional spacetime 
manifold in which time and space are inseparable, observer -dependent, and dynamically  
affected by mass and energy. Absolute time, in the sense of a single time variable valid for all 
reference frames, had no place in this picture and played no role in its mathematical formalism; 
the metaphysical implications of four-dimensional spacetime for temporal ontology have been 
extensively explored in subsequent philosophy of physics [Sider, 2001].

The philosophical legacy of this development has been the near-universal assumption that the 
Mach-Einstein critique did not merely remove absolute time from physics but refuted it 
altogether. Absolute time, on this reading, was a primitive intuitive notion that the progress of 
physical understanding correctly eliminated. It described nothing real, and its apparent 
plausibility was an artefact of limited human experience with low velocities and weak 
gravitational fields. This paper does not dispute the physi cal verdict. Within the domain of 
physical measurement and operational definition, the Mach -Einstein critique stands without 
qualification. What this paper disputes is the ontological conclusion drawn from that physical 
verdict: namely, that because absolu te time fails as a physical quantity, it fails as a concept 
entirely. That inference requires a further premise —that only physically measurable or 
physically causal entities are real, and that physics constitutes the complete inventory of what 
exists. That premise is not established by the Mach -Einstein critique; it is presupposed by it. 
The question this paper poses is whether that presupposition can be sustained when the 
structural features of Newton's definition are examined with care [Sklar, 1974]. 
1.2 Newton's Unresolved Intuition 
Newton's insistence on absolute time presents a genuine and underappreciated puzzle for 
historians and philosophers of science. Newton was not a careless or undisciplined thinker. The 
Principia Mathematica is a work of extraordinary logical precision and rigorous mathematical 
demonstration. His laws of motion, his derivation of universal gravitation from Kepler's laws, 
his treatment of force, mass, and acceleration —all of these are formulated with meticulous 
attention to what can and cannot be derived from observation, and with careful separation of 
the mathematical framework from its physical interpretation. And yet, in the Scholiu m that 
opens the Principia, Newton introduces a concept of time that he himself explicitly 
acknowledges cannot be directly measured. He distinguishes absolute time from "relative, 
apparent, and common time," which he describes as "some sensible and externa l measure of 
duration by the means of motion." Clocks, pendulums, the rotation of the earth—these are, for 
Newton, all approximations to absolute time, not constitutive of it. They measure something, 
but not absolute time itself. 
The standard answer to why Newton needed this concept is that his mechanics required a fixed 
reference against which inertial motion could be defined. Inertia —the tendency of a body to 
continue in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by a force —presupposes a 
standard of straightness and uniformity. Absolute space provided the geometric standard;

===== PAGE 4 =====

absolute time provided the temporal standard. Without them, Newton's first law would be 
circular: a body moves uniformly unless acted upon by a force, where "uniformly" means at a 
constant rate measured against... what? Absolute time provides the answer: m easured against 
time itself, not against the motion of some other body. This is a coherent and well -understood 
argument, and it explains why Newton needed something like absolute time as a theoretical 
posit. 
But this argument does not explain why Newton described absolute time in the way he did. If 
the sole function of absolute time were to serve as a theoretical backdrop for the definition of 
inertia, Newton need not have said that it "flows." He need not hav e specified that it flows 
"equably." He need not have tied its independence not merely to the absence of measurement, 
but to the absence of any relation to anything external whatsoever. These are not the minimal 
commitments of a physicist constructing a th eoretical scaffold. They are the commitments of 
someone who has a conception of time that goes well beyond what his mechanics strictly 
requires—a conception in which time has a character, a quality, a mode of existence that is 
prior to and independent of any physical process. The language Newton uses is the language 
of description, not merely of postulation. This paper takes that observation as its starting point. 
If Newton's description of absolute time is more than a minimal theoretical postulate, the 
question is what it is a description of. 
1.3 Ontological Re-anchoring as Methodology 
The method employed in this paper is what will be called ontological re-anchoring. This is not 
a historical or interpretive method. It does not ask what Newton meant, what he privately 
believed, or what intellectual influences shaped his thinking about time. The biographical and 
historical questions about Newton's intentions are legitimate and interesting questions, but they 
are not the subject of this inquiry, and nothing in this paper's argument depends on answering 
them. 
Ontological re -anchoring is a structural analysis operation applied to concepts. It takes a 
concept—in this case, Newton's absolute time as defined in the Scholium —and asks: what 
ontological domain does the structure of this concept actually instantiate? The concept may 
have been introduced in one domain and assigned to one ontological category by its author or 
by the tradition that inherited it. The question is whether that assignment is correct, or whether 
the structural features of the concept place it more naturally in a different ontological domain.

===== PAGE 5 =====
5 
 
The operation does not change the concept; it changes the domain within which the concept is 
interpreted. 
Crucially, this methodology requires distinguishing between two claims that are often run 
together in the philosophy of science. The first claim is that a concept fails within a given 
domain—that it cannot be operationalised, that it makes no testable predictions, that it plays 
no explanatory role in the domain's theoretical framework. The second claim is that a concept 
is ontologically invalid—that it describes nothing, refers to nothing, and has no standing in any 
domain of inquiry. The Mach -Einstein crit ique establishes the first claim with respect to 
absolute time and physics. It does not, by itself, establish the second. The inference from the 
first to the second requires the additional premise that the domain in which the concept fails is 
the only domain in which it could have any standing. That premise, as argued above, is not part 
of the critique itself. 
Applied to absolute time, ontological re -anchoring proceeds as follows. First, the structural 
features of Newton's definition are identified through close textual analysis. Second, those 
structural features are compared with the structural features of cand idate ontological 
domains—specifically, the phenomenological structure of consciousness and the ontological 
structure of consciousness as a background condition. Third, on the basis of this comparison, 
an abductive inference is drawn: the structural featur es of absolute time are best explained by 
the hypothesis that the concept belongs to the ontological domain of consciousness as 
background structure rather than to the domain of physical quantities. The result is not a proof 
but an inference to the best ex planation: the classification of absolute time as a description of 
ontological background structure explains its structural features more naturally than its 
classification as a physical quantity, which leaves those features anomalous and inexplicable. 
2. Textual Structure of Newton's Absolute Time 
2.1 Newton's Definition in the Scholium 
The primary text for this analysis is the Scholium on space, time, place, and motion, which 
appears at the opening of Newton's Principia Mathematica, first published in 1687. The Latin 
original reads: 
Tempus absolutum verum & Mathematicum, in se & natura sua absq; relatione ad externum 
quodvis, æquabiliter fluit, alioq; nomine dicitur Duratio.

===== PAGE 6 =====
6 
 
The standard English translation renders this as: "Absolute, true, and mathematical time, of 
itself, and from its own nature, flows equably without relation to anything external, and by 
another name is called duration." This translation is accurate as far as it goes, but it conceals 
several layers of meaning encoded in Newton's Latin that are directly relevant to the argument 
of this paper. A philological analysis of the key terms reveals that Newton's definition is not a 
description of a physical quantity at all, but a description of a mode of existence. 
The first term requiring analysis is Tempus. In classical and scientific Latin, tempus carries a 
semantic range considerably wider than the English word "time." It encompasses not only 
measured duration but also occasion, epoch, season, and the condition of existence as extended 
through its own unfolding. It is not, in its primary Latin usa ge, a term of measurement; it is a 
term of ontological condition. Newton's choice of tempus over more explicitly quantitative 
alternatives signals that he is not primarily concerned with time as a measurable variable. 
The second key term is Mathematicum. In the intellectual context of the late seventeenth 
century, this word designated not merely computational or arithmetic structure but rational, 
proportional structure that could be grasped by pure reason —structure that obeys precise 
relations independently of empirical observation. Newton's explicit designation of absolute 
time as mathematicum is a declaration that the concept has rigorous structural content: it is not 
a vague intuition but a formally structured concept whose properties can be stated with 
mathematical precision. This is precisely what the geometric isomorphism analysis of this 
paper requires: the structural features of Newton's absolute time are not mere impressions but 
formally characterisable properties, and their correspondence with the structural features of 
consciousness as ontological background condition is therefore a correspondence between two 
formally structured conceptual domains. 
The third key term, and the most significant for the present argument, is Duratio. Newton 
specifies that absolute time "by another name is called Duratio"—duration. But duratio in Latin 
does not mean a measured interval of time. It derives from durare, which means to persist, to 
endure, to continue in existence. Duratio is the noun of existence -persistence: it names the 
condition of continuing to be, not the measurement of how long something has been. When 
Newton says that absolute time is called duratio, he is identifying it with existence-persistence 
itself—with the ontological condition of being that endures, not with any temporal quantity 
that can be assigned a numerical value. This is a decisive philological observation. Newton is 
not saying that absolute time is a kind of time in the ordinary sense; he is  saying that it is the

===== PAGE 7 =====
7 
 
condition of existence -persistence that underlies all temporal description. Consciousness 
exists—it does not have a duration in the sense of a measurable interval. One does not say that 
a consciousness lasted three seconds in the way one says that a physical process lasted three 
seconds. One says that consciousness exists, and that its existence has a  spatial locus—in the 
brain, or in the geometry of the universe on the ontological account this paper develops. This 
is precisely what duratio denotes: existence, not measurement. 
The fourth key term is æquabiliter, rendered in English as "equably." The Latin æquabiliter 
derives from æquus, meaning equal, level, balanced —the same root as the English "equable" 
but with a stronger spatial resonance. Æquus describes the quality of a level surface, a field 
uniformly distributed, a condition that holds without variation across an extended domain. And 
fluit—translated as "flows"—carries in Latin not only the sense of flowing in a single direction 
but also of spreading, diffusing, and exten ding across a space: rivers flow, but so do liquids 
spreading across a surface, and so do fields distributing themselves through a medium. 
Æquabiliter fluit  therefore describes not the unidirectional flow of a river but the uniform 
extension of a condition across a domain —the even distribution of a background presence 
through the geometry of space. This is not the language of temporal flow; it is the language of 
spatial distribution. Newton's absolute time, read through its Latin original, is not something 
that passes from moment to moment. It is something that is uniformly present throughout —
that extends itself equably across the geometry of existence, as consciousness extends itself 
throughout its spatial substrate. 
The fifth key phrase is in se & natura sua —"in itself and from its own nature." This is an 
unambiguous statement of ontological self-grounding. The structure exists from its own nature; 
its existence and its properties are not derived from anything external to it. This is the 
ontological structure of consciousness as well: consciousness exists from its own nature, not 
because something external confers existence upon it. 
Taken together, the philological analysis of Newton's Latin reveals a definition whose proper 
subject is not physical time but ontological existence -structure: a mathematically structured 
(Mathematicum), self -grounded ( in se & natura sua ) existence -persistence ( Duratio) that 
distributes itself uniformly ( æquabiliter fluit ) across its domain without dependence on 
anything external (absq; relatione ad externum quodvis). This is not a description of a clock or 
a physical process. It is a description of the structural character of existence as such—the kind 
of existence that consciousness instantiates, distributed across the geometry of space as an

ontological background condition. The geometric isomorphism between Newton's definition 
and consciousness as ontological background is therefore established at the philological level 
before any further philosophical analysis is required: Newton's own words, read in their Latin 
original, describe the structure of existence, not the structure of physical time. 
The philosophical question of whether time genuinely flows or whether flow is a metaphor for 
something else has been extensively debated [Smart, 1949]; the philological analysis above 
suggests that Newton's fluit belongs to the latter category —not temporal flow but geometric 
extension. 
2.2 Autonomy, Uniformity, and Background Structure 
Three structural features emerge from the analysis of Newton's definition, and these three 
features constitute the framework within which the structural comparison with consciousness 
will be conducted. 
The first structural feature is ontological autonomy. Newton's absolute time exists "of itself, 
and from its own nature" and flows "without relation to anything external." Taken together, 
these phrases specify a concept whose existence and character are fu lly self -grounded. 
Absolute time does not depend on anything outside itself for its existence, for its rate, or for its 
character as a continuous, uniform flow. This is a strong form of ontological independence: not 
merely independence from particular phys ical objects, but independence from the entire 
domain of physical relations. No physical event, no physical process, and no physical 
observation can affect, alter, or even approximate absolute time except by coincidence. 
Physical processes may happen to oc cur at rates that correspond to absolute time, but this 
correspondence is not constitutive of absolute time; it is merely accidental. 
The philosophical significance of this feature becomes clear when it is contrasted with the kind 
of existence attributed to physical quantities. Physical quantities are inherently relational: they 
are defined by relations between physical objects or events , measured by comparison with 
standards, and their values depend on the reference frame of the observer. Mass, charge, 
velocity, temperature—all of these are defined by their relations to other physical quantities 
and to the measurement procedures that determine their values. A concept that exists "without 
relation to anything external" is structurally incompatible with the category of physical 
quantities. Its autonomy is precisely what makes it impossible to operationalise within physics.

9 
 
The second structural feature is intrinsic equability. Newton specifies that absolute time "flows 
equably"—at a constant, uniform rate, without variation. This equability is not an empirical 
claim that can be verified by comparing physical clocks. It is a claim about the intrinsic 
character of absolute time as a structure: uniformity is what it is, not what it happens to be 
measured to be. The distinction is crucial. An empirical uniformity could, in principle, be 
disrupted by physical conditions; it would be a contingent feature of the physical world, subject 
to verification and revision. An intrinsic equability is a constitutive feature of the concept: 
absolute time cannot fail to be equable any more than a triangle can fail to have three sides. 
This is ag ain a structural feature that places absolute time outside the class of physical 
quantities, all of which have contingent values subject to empirical determination. 
The third structural feature is background generativity. Newton's absolute time is not itself an 
event or a process occurring within the physical world. It is the condition within which physical 
events are ordered, the background against which physical processes occur. Physical events 
happen in time; they do not constitute time. Absolute time is the medium —or more precisely, 
the structure—of temporal order itself, not an item within that order. This gives absolute time 
the character of a background condition: something that must be presupposed for physical 
description to be possible, rather than something that physical description describes . Newton 
himself draws this distinction when he separates absolute time from its "sensible measures"—
the clocks and processes that serve as approximations to it. The measures are within time; time 
itself is the background within which they occur. 
2.3 Why Absolute Time Exceeds Physical Operationalisation 
These three structural features together constitute what might be called the structural anomaly 
of Newton's absolute time within physics. A concept that is ontologically autonomous, 
intrinsically equable, and background-generative cannot be operationalised within a scientific 
framework that requires measurability, relationality, and causal accessibility. The analysis of 
each feature shows why. 
Ontological autonomy means that absolute time cannot be defined in terms of measurement 
procedures. All physical measurement is relational: it compares one quantity with another, one 
process with another, one state with another. But absolute time, by defin ition, does not stand 
in relations to anything external that could serve as a comparison. No physical clock measures 
absolute time; it measures something that may or may not coincide with absolute time, and 
there is no procedure for determining whether it does. This is not a practical limitation that

===== PAGE 10 =====
10 
 
better technology might overcome. It is a principled exclusion that follows from the concept's 
definition. 
Intrinsic equability means that the uniformity of absolute time cannot be verified by any 
physical standard. To verify that something flows uniformly, one would need an independent 
standard of uniformity against which to compare it. But absolute time is it self supposed to be 
the ultimate standard of temporal uniformity —there is nothing more fundamental against 
which it could be measured. Any attempt to verify its equability would either be circular 
(comparing absolute time with itself) or inappropriate (comparing absolute time with a physical 
process that is, by definition, only an approximation to it). 
Background generativity means that absolute time does not enter into causal relations with 
physical quantities. It is the background of physical events, not a participant in them. But 
physical science, in its modern form, deals with entities that have meas urable effects on other 
physical entities —that interact causally with the rest of the physical world. An entity that 
functions purely as a background, affecting nothing and affected by nothing in the physical 
domain, is not the kind of entity that physical  science can investigate. It is, in Mach's 
vocabulary, genuinely superfluous —not because it is meaningless, but because it is 
inaccessible to the methods of physical investigation. 
The Mach -Einstein critique is therefore well -founded as a critique of absolute time as a 
physical concept. But the critique establishes only what has been argued: that absolute time, as 
defined by Newton, cannot be a physical quantity. It does not establis h that the concept is 
therefore empty. The structural features that make absolute time anomalous within physics —
its autonomy, equability, and background character—may be features that belong naturally to 
a different ontological domain. The identification of that domain is the task of the sections that 
follow. 
2.4 Absolute Time as a Non-Physical Ontological Category 
Before turning to the comparison with consciousness, it is worth drawing out the positive 
implication of the analysis so far. The structural features of Newton's absolute time do not 
merely distinguish it from physical quantities by their absence of certai n properties. They 
constitute a positive structural profile: a self -grounded, intrinsically uniform, background 
condition of order. This is not the profile of nothing; it is a recognisable kind of structure. The 
question is where in the landscape of ontolo gical categories a structure with this profile 
belongs—a question that has occupied philosophers of time from Aristotle's analysis of time

===== PAGE 11 =====
11 
 
as the measure of motion [Aristotle, 350 BCE/1984] to McTaggart's influential distinction 
between A-series and B-series temporal description [McTaggart, 1908]. 
The history of philosophy offers several candidate categories. Kant's pure forms of intuition—
space and time as conditions of the possibility of experience—share some features of Newton's 
absolute time: they are not empirical objects, they are not derived from experience, and they 
function as conditions of possibility rather than as objects within experience [Kant, 1781/1998]. 
But Kant's forms of intuition are epistemological rather than ontological: they are features of 
the structure of human cognition, not features of a mind-independent reality. Newton's absolute 
time, by contrast, is explicitly conceived as independent of any observer —it flows "without 
relation to anything external," including any observing mind. 
Mathematical structures offer another candidate: the real number line, for instance, has 
properties of continuity and uniformity analogous to those Newton attributes to absolute time. 
But mathematical structures are abstract objects, not concrete features of reality, and Newton's 
absolute time is clearly meant to be something real —the actual temporal background of the 
physical universe, not a mathematical model of it. 
The candidate that this paper argues is most appropriate is the ontological structure of 
consciousness as a background condition: not consciousness as a set of particular experiences 
or mental states, but consciousness considered as the non -relational, self-sustaining structure 
that underlies and makes possible particular experiences —a characterisation that resonates 
with Heidegger's account of temporality as a background structure of existence irreducible to 
clock-time [Heidegger, 1927/1962], though the present argument operates in an analytic rather 
than existential register. This candidate is developed in the following section, and the structural 
correspondence between it and Newton's definition is established in Section 4. 
3. The Phenomenological and Ontological Structure of Consciousness 
3.1 Husserl's Inner Time-Consciousness and the Structure of Phenomenological Time 
The insight that time is constituted by inner consciousness rather than measured by external 
clocks has deep roots in Western philosophy, most notably in Augustine's analysis of temporal 
experience in the Confessions [Augustine, 397/1991], which anticipate s Husserl's 
phenomenological account. Edmund Husserl's analysis of the phenomenology of internal time-
consciousness, developed across the first decade of the twentieth century and published in 1928, 
provides the most rigorous account available in the Weste rn philosophical tradition of what

===== PAGE 12 =====
12 
 
temporal experience is like from the inside. Husserl's starting point is a dissatisfaction with 
accounts of time -consciousness that model it on the perception of spatial objects. When one 
perceives a spatial object, the object is present all at once; one can walk around it, view it from 
different angles, attend to different parts of it. But temporal experience does not work like this. 
A melody, for instance, is not experienced as a series of simultaneous notes; it is experienced 
as a sequence in which each note arrives in succession, and the melody as a whole is grasped 
not by being simultaneously present but by being retained in memory as the sequence unfolds. 
The question Husserl poses is: what is the structure of the consciousness that makes this kind 
of temporal experience possible? 
Husserl's answer is organised around what he calls the living present. The living present is not 
a mathematical instant—a durationless point on a temporal line. It is a thick temporal field with 
an internal structure of three dimensions. The primal impression is the moment of experience 
as it is occurring —the note as it sounds, the word as it is spoken, the sensation as it is felt. 
Retention is the consciousness of what has just elapsed: as each primal impression recedes, it 
is not simply lost but retained in a modified form, held in a kind of trailing awareness that fades 
progressively as the original moment recedes further into the past. Protention is the forward 
anticipation of what is about to occur: consciousness reaches ahead, not by predicting specific 
content, but by maintaining an open expectational structure that will be fulfilled or disappointed 
by what actually comes. 
These three dimensions are not separate acts of consciousness that are then assembled into a 
unified experience. They are co-present structural features of a single temporal act. The living 
present flows continuously into itself: each primal impression becomes a retention as the next 
primal impression arrives, and the retentional trail fades continuously as further impressions 
succeed it. This flow is self -constituting: consciousness does not need an external clock to 
generate temporal succession. The flow of the living present is generated by the internal 
structural dynamics of consciousness itself —retention, primal impression, and protention 
together constitute temporal order from within. 
What is crucial for this paper's argument is an important distinction that Husserl's analysis 
makes possible, even if Husserl himself does not draw it in precisely these terms—a distinction 
further developed in recent phenomenological research on the relationship between temporal 
consciousness and its ontological conditions [Gallagher, 1998; Thompson, 2007; Varela, 1999]. 
The inner temporal structure described by Husserl is not the same as Newton's absolute time.

===== PAGE 13 =====
13 
 
Husserl's inner time -consciousness is relational: it consists of relations between retention, 
primal impression, and protention. Each phase of the flow depends on the others; the primal 
impression becomes a retention only in relation to the next primal imp ression; protention is 
defined by its relation to the primal impression it anticipates. Moreover, Husserl's inner time -
consciousness is content -dependent: the retention of a melody is the retention of those 
particular notes, in that particular sequence. Remove the content, and there is nothing to retain 
or anticipate. Husserl's phenomenological time is the structure of experience as it is lived; it is 
not the non-relational background structure that Newton describes. 
This distinction is philosophically important and has implications for the memory -time 
relationship that further clarifies the difference between phenomenological time and absolute 
time. Human consciousness—as it is actually experienced, including its temporal dimension—
is structured by memory. What gives temporal experience its directionality, its sense of flowing 
from past through present to future, is precisely the asymmetry of memory: we retain what has 
occurred and anticipate what has not yet occurred, but we cannot retain what has not occurred 
or anticipate what has already occurred. This directionality is not a feature of time itself, on 
Newton's account; it is a feature of the consciousness that exists within time. If physical time 
were to run in reverse, the entire informational structure of memory—the causal sequences that 
constitute the content of retained experience—would have to reverse with it. A consciousness 
whose temporal structure is constituted by memory relations is therefore bound to the direction 
and content of physical processes in a way that Newton's absolute time explicitly is not. The 
inner temporal structure of human consciousness is relational, memory -dependent, and 
directional; Newton's absolute time is non -relational, content -independent, and prior to 
directionality. They are not the same structure.  This distinction finds support in empirical 
research on optimal experience and flow states, which documents the suspension of ordinary 
clock-time awareness under conditions of complete absorption — a further indication that the 
relational, content-dependent character of ordinary temporal consciousness is not an intrinsic 
feature of consciousness as such, but a feature of consciousness as it operates wi thin the 
physical domain [Csikszentmihalyi, 1990]. 
3.2 From Phenomenological Structure to Ontological Hypothesis 
The distinction drawn in the previous section is important for the argument that follows, 
because it prevents a conflation that would undermine the paper's central claim. The claim is 
not that Newton's absolute time is the same as Husserl's inner time -consciousness. The inner

===== PAGE 14 =====
14 
 
temporal structure of human experience is relational, content -dependent, and 
phenomenologically rich. Newton's absolute time is non -relational, content-independent, and 
structurally spare. If the paper simply equated the two, it would be open to the object ion that 
Husserl's phenomenology describes nothing like what Newton had in mind. 
The argument proceeds differently. Husserl's analysis reveals something about consciousness 
that is more fundamental than its inner temporal structure: it reveals that consciousness, in 
order to constitute temporal experience at all, must possess a background capacity for temporal 
order—a capacity that is not itself constituted by the temporal experience it underlies. The 
living present, with its retention -primal impression -protention structure, is possible only 
because there is a consciousness capable of h olding these phases together. That background 
capacity is not itself a series of temporal phases; it is the condition under which temporal phases 
can be constituted and related to one another. Husserl calls this the absolute flow of 
consciousness—a phrase that is suggestive for the present comparison, though it requires 
careful handling [Zahavi, 2003]. 
The transition from phenomenological analysis to ontological interpretation requires a 
methodological clarification, and this paper makes it explicit. The phenomenological analysis 
of Husserl does not, by itself, establish the existence of a consciousness substrate distributed 
across physical space. Husserl's phenomenology is a description of the structure of first-person 
experience; it does not commit to claims about the physical extent or spatial distribution of 
consciousness. To move from Husserl's analysis to the ontological hypothesis advanced in this 
paper requires a step that goes beyond what the phenomenological analysis alone warrants. 
That step is the following ontological hypothesis: if consciousness is considered not merely as 
an experiential phenomenon—as something described from the inside by a subject —but as a 
fundamental structure in its own right, then its ontological organisation should preserve the 
structural characteristics revealed at the phenomenological level. In other words: if 
consciousness has, at the phenomenological level, the features of a utonomy (it constitutes 
temporal order from its own internal resources), equabili ty (the flow of the living present is 
continuous and self-maintaining), and background generativity (it is the condition of temporal 
experience, not an item within it), then these features belong to consciousness as such —not 
merely to consciousness as experienced, but to consciousness as a structure. 
This is an abductive inference, not a deductive proof. It does not follow necessarily from the 
phenomenological analysis that consciousness as an ontological structure must have these

features. The inference is that the hypothesis that it does —the hypothesis that consciousness 
as a fundamental structure is autonomous, equable, and background-generative—best explains 
the structural features that Husserl's analysis reveals at the phenomenological level. The 
hypothesis is methodologically distinguishable from the phenomenological analysis that 
motivates it, and it is explicitly advanced as a hypothesis rath er than as a derivation. Treating 
it as a hypothesis allows the structural comparison with Newton's definition to proceed without 
presupposing its truth; the comparison itself provides the evidence that supports the hypothesis. 
This methodological move also distinguishes the present argument from panpsychism, and 
from the broader family of positions in philosophy of mind that treat consciousness as 
ontologically irreducible to physical description [Chalmers, 1996]—though it shares with those 
positions the recognition that consciousness raises questions that cannot be resolved within a 
purely physicalist framework.  Panpsychism is the metaphysical thesis that consciousness or 
experience is a fundamental feature of physical reality—that physical entities, down to the most 
basic level, possess some form of inner experience. The ontological hypothesis advanced here 
does not claim that physical space is animated by consciousness, that fundamental particles are 
conscious, or that the phys ical world is in any sense mental. The claim is structural and 
comparative: that the structural features identified in Newton's definition of absolute time 
correspond to the structural features of consciousness understood as an ontological background 
condition. This is a claim about the form of the concept, not about the substance of physical 
reality. 
3.3 The Structural Triad of Consciousness as Background Condition 
With the methodological position established, the structural features of consciousness as an 
ontological background condition can be drawn out. Three features correspond precisely to the 
three structural features identified in Newton's definition. 
The first is ontological autonomy. Consciousness, as an ontological background condition, 
does not derive its existence or its structural character from its relations to external physical 
objects or events. On the hypothesis advanced above, the capacity of  consciousness to 
constitute temporal order is not given to it by the physical world; it is an intrinsic feature of 
consciousness as a structure. This does not mean that consciousness is causally isolated from 
the physical world —the experiences that consci ousness constitutes are clearly shaped by 
physical events. But the background capacity for temporal constitution is not itself derived

===== PAGE 16 =====
16 
 
from those events. It is, in the relevant sense, self -grounding: it exists from its own nature, as 
Newton says of absolute time. 
The second is intrinsic equability. The flow of the living present, as Husserl describes it, is 
continuous and self -maintaining. It does not speed up or slow down in response to external 
conditions; it does not require any external process to sustain its c ontinuity. The continuity of 
the living present is a constitutive feature of consciousness as a temporal structure, not a 
contingent feature that might fail to hold. At the ontological level, this translates to the claim 
that consciousness as a background condition is intrinsically uniform in its structural 
character—its capacity for temporal constitution is not variable but constant. This is the 
ontological analogue of Newton's "flows equably." 
The third is background generativity. Consciousness, as an ontological background condition, 
is not itself an event or an object within the world it underlies. It is the condition under which 
events can be experienced, objects can be perceived, and temporal sequences can be constituted. 
Particular experiences are within consciousness; consciousness itself is not within experience. 
It is the background within which experience occurs, not an item within the foreground of 
experience. This background character —being the condition of what appears without itself 
appearing—is precisely the structural feature that Newton specifies when he says that absolute 
time is the background of all physical events, not itself an event among others. 
These three structural features —ontological autonomy, intrinsic equability, and background 
generativity—constitute the structural triad of consciousness as an ontological background 
condition. They are the same three features identified in Newton's definition of absolute time 
in Section 2. The structural correspondence between the two is the subject of the f ollowing 
section. 
4. Structural Correspondence Between Absolute Time and Consciousness 
4.1 From Similarity to Structural Correspondence 
The comparison between Newton's absolute time and consciousness as an ontological 
background condition is not a comparison of superficial resemblances, and it is important to be 
precise about the kind of correspondence being claimed. The argument does not depend on the 
verbal overlap between Newton's "flow" and the philosophical concept of consciousness flow. 
The overlap is suggestive, but suggestion is not argument. The claim is more specific: the 
structural organisation of Newton's definition and the structural organisation of consciousness

===== PAGE 17 =====
17 
 
as ontological background condition exhibit a precise and non -trivial correspondence at the 
level of conceptual form. 
A structural correspondence of this kind holds when two domains can be described by the same 
set of structural relations—when the defining features of one domain stand in the same relations 
to one another and to the phenomena they organise as the defining features of the other domain. 
The isomorphism is geometric and conceptual: it holds between two formally characterisable 
domains whose defining features stand in precisely corresponding relations to one another and 
to the phenomena they organise. Newton's own designation of absolute time as 
Mathematicum—as a structure graspable by pure reason and governed by precise proportional 
relations—establishes that the concept has formal structural content, and that a correspondence 
between its structural features and those of consciousness as ontological background condition 
is a correspondence between two formally structured domains, not a loose an alogy. The 
correspondence claimed here is at the level of conceptual organisation: the three defining 
features of Newton's absolute time (autonomy, equability, background generativity) map onto 
the three defining features of consciousness as ontological background condition (ontological 
autonomy, intrinsic equability, background generativity) in a systematic and non -coincidental 
way. 
The method of establishing this correspondence is abductive inference, sometimes called 
inference to the best explanation. The question is not whether it is logically necessary that 
Newton's definition describes consciousness as ontological background cond ition—it is not, 
and the argument does not claim that it is. The question is whether the hypothesis that it does 
provides a better explanation of the structural features of Newton's definition than the 
alternative hypothesis that it describes a physical quantity. The structural anomaly of absolute 
time within physics—the fact that its three defining features are precisely the features that make 
it impossible to operationalise within physics —is explained naturally by the hypothesis that 
the concept belongs to a different ontological domain. The structural correspondence with 
consciousness as ontological background condition is the positive evidence that identifies 
which domain.

4.3 Non-Identity and Methodological Limits 
The structural correspondence established above must be precisely bounded. The argument 
does not claim that consciousness produces Newtonian time. It does not claim that Newton's 
absolute time is identical with consciousness. It does not claim that the phe nomenological 
domain and the physical domain are ultimately unified, or that temporal experience and 
physical time share a common metaphysical substrate. The claim is precisely this: the 
ontological structure attributed to absolute time shares a form of organisation with the structure 
of consciousness as ontological background condition. This is a claim about structural form, 
not about metaphysical identity or causal relation.

===== PAGE 19 =====
19 
 
Two structures can share a form of organisation while being instantiated in entirely different 
domains, composed of entirely different materials, and serving entirely different functions. The 
structural correspondence between them is philosophically signif icant because it provides the 
basis for ontological re-anchoring: it allows us to argue that a concept with a given structural 
form belongs more naturally in the domain where that structural form is native than in the 
domain where it is anomalous. The corr espondence does not license the conclusion that the 
two domains are the same, or that what Newton described and what consciousness is are the 
same thing. 
This methodological limit is essential to the paper's academic standing. A weaker version of 
the argument —that Newton's definition is merely analogous to consciousness, that the two 
share some similarities —would not support ontological re -anchoring; it would support only 
the observation that the two are interestingly similar. A stronger version of the argument—that 
Newton's absolute time is literally identical to consciousness, that Newton somehow described 
consciousness without knowing it —would be an extraordinary metaphysical claim requiring 
extraordinary evidence. The argument of this paper occupies the middle ground: a structural 
correspondence sufficient to support re-anchoring but stopping short of identity claims. 
4.4 Distinction from Kantian Transcendental Time 
A natural and important objection to the argument developed so far is that it simply rediscovers 
Kant's account of time as a pure form of intuition. Kant's Critique of Pure Reason (1781) argued 
that time is not a feature of things as they are in themselves, but a form imposed by the human 
mind on the raw data of experience  [Kant, 1781/1998]. Time, for Kant, is the form of inner 
sense—the structure through which the mind orders all its representations in temporal sequence. 
Like Newton's absolute time, Kantian time is not derived from experience; it is a condition of 
experience. Like Newton's absolute time, Kantian time is not learned from observation; it is 
presupposed by observation. The apparent parallel is close enough to raise the question: is the 
ontological re-anchoring proposed here merely a roundabout route to Kantianism? 
The distinction is genuine and important. Kant's pure form of intuition is an epistemological 
concept: it is a condition of the possibility of knowledge, a feature of the structure of human 
cognition. It belongs to what Kant calls the transcendental level —the level of the conditions 
under which objects of experience are possible for a knowing subject. Kant is not making a 
claim about the ontological structure of consciousness independent of its cognitive function; 
he is making a claim about the formal struc ture of human knowledge. The form of time, for

===== PAGE 20 =====
20 
 
Kant, is the form imposed by the human mind on the content of experience; it says nothing 
about what consciousness is in itself, apart from its cognitive role. 
The ontological hypothesis advanced in this paper operates at a different level. It is not a claim 
about the conditions of human knowledge, but a claim about the structural character of 
consciousness as an ontological category. The question is not "what is  the formal structure of 
human cognition?" but "what is the ontological structure of consciousness as such?" These are 
related but distinct questions. A Kantian answer to the first does not determine the answer to 
the second. Moreover, Kant's pure form of time is explicitly subjective—it is the form of inner 
sense, of the human mind's self-organisation. Newton's absolute time is explicitly objective—
it flows independently of any observer, including any human mind. The re-anchoring proposed 
here maintains this objectivity by locating absolute time not in the subjective structure of 
human cognition but in the ontological structure of consciousness as a background condition—
a structure that is not defined by its relation to any particular observer, and which finds further 
support in phenomenological accounts of the body -subject's pre -reflective temporal 
engagement with the world [Merleau-Ponty, 1945/1962]. 
5. Ontological Re-anchoring of Absolute Time 
5.1 Resolving the Mach-Einstein Critique 
The critique of absolute time mounted by Mach and Einstein is, within its own domain, decisive. 
Mach correctly identified that any concept which cannot be operationally grounded —which 
cannot be defined in terms of measurement procedures and observational outcomes —has no 
legitimate place in empirical physics. Einstein correctly showed that there is no physically 
privileged universal time coordinate, that simultaneity is frame-relative, and that time intervals 
are observer-dependent. These results stand, and nothing in the present argument disputes them 
or attempts to rehabilitate absolute time as a physical concept. 
What the Mach-Einstein critique does not establish—and what is crucial to recognise—is that 
the critique reaches only as far as the domain within which it was formulated. Mach's 
operationalism is a criterion for concepts within empirical science; it is not a criterion for 
ontological validity in general. The premise that only measurable or causally accessible entities 
are real is a substantive metaphysical commitment—physicalism—not a result of the physical 
critique itself. Einstein's relativity demonstrate s that there is no absolute physical time 
coordinate; it does not demonstrate that no non -physical, ontological background structure

===== PAGE 21 =====
21 
 
exists. The scope of the critique is the physical domain; its conclusion is about physical 
quantities. 
The argument can be made more precise. Mach and Einstein eliminated absolute time from 
physics on the grounds that it was not operationally definable and played no role in the 
mathematical formalism of physical theory. These are good reasons for excluding it from 
physics. But the same grounds—that absolute time is not operationally definable, that it plays 
no role in physical formalism —are equally good reasons for concluding that absolute time 
never belonged to physics in the first place. The concept's fail ure to satisfy physical 
requirements is evidence of its misclassification, not of its emptiness. A concept placed in the 
wrong ontological category will fail to behave like concepts native to that category; this failure 
is evidence of misplacement, not of non -existence. The ontological distinction between 
absolute and relational spacetime has been central to philosophy of physics since Newton's 
dispute with Leibniz [Leibniz, 1715/1989] and continues to structure contemporary debate 
[Hoefer, 1996; Earman, 1989; Pooley, 2013]. 
To put the point differently: Mach and Einstein believed they were eliminating a concept that 
had been correctly classified as physical and had simply turned out to be empirically empty. 
The present argument proposes that they were eliminating a concept that had been incorrectly 
classified as physical from the outset. The difference is significant. On the standard reading, 
the Mach-Einstein critique is a successful refutation. On the re -anchoring reading, the Mach-
Einstein critique is a successful correctio n of a category error —but a correction that removes 
the concept only from the wrong domain, leaving open the question of whether it belongs in a 
different domain. 
5.2 From Physical Substance to Phenomenological and Ontological Structure 
The positive thesis of this paper can now be stated in its full form. Newton's absolute time, 
understood as a physical substance or physical quantity —as a real feature of the physical 
universe that flows at a constant rate independently of all physical processes—is indefensible. 
The Mach-Einstein critique is decisive in that register, and the structural analysis of Section 2 
explains why: the concept's structural features (autonomy, equability, background generativity) 
are precisely the features that make it impossible to operationalise within physics. 
Newton's absolute time, understood as structurally corresponding to consciousness as 
ontological background condition —as a specification of the structural features of the non -
relational, self-grounding, equably continuous background that underlies temporal experience

===== PAGE 22 =====
22 
 
and makes temporal constitution possible —is not only defensible but philosophically 
illuminating. The structural features that make absolute time anomalous within physics are 
exactly the features that characterise consciousness as an ontological background condition. 
The concept is at home in the ontological domain in a way it never was in the physical domain. 
Ontological re-anchoring, as defined in Section 1.3, relocates the concept from the category 
where it is anomalous to the category where its structural features are native. Applied to 
absolute time, this operation is not a revision of Newton's definition b ut a revision of the 
domain within which the definition is interpreted. Newton's words remain unchanged; what 
changes is the understanding of what kind of structure those words describe. The re-anchoring 
is supported by the structural correspondence establ ished in Section 4: the three defining 
features of absolute time correspond precisely to the three defining features of consciousness 
as ontological background condition. This correspondence provides the positive evidence that 
identifies the domain to which the concept belongs. 
The result is a concept that has been recovered from a domain where it was misclassified —
where it created anomalies and resisted all attempts at operationalisation —and placed in a 
domain where its structural features are intelligible and coherent. This is not a triumphalist 
rehabilitation of Newton against Einstein. It is a philosophical clarification: an identification 
of the kind of concept that Newton's definition  instantiates, and a location of that kind within 
the broader landscape of ontological categories. 
5.3 Relativity and Absolute Time as Different Ontological Layers 
One of the most important consequences of the ontological re-anchoring is that it dissolves the 
apparent conflict between relativistic physics and absolute time. This conflict has been treated, 
since 1905, as settled: Einstein showed that there is no absol ute time, and physics moved on. 
But the conflict is genuine only if absolute time and relativistic time are competitors within the 
same ontological domain —only if both are claims about the same kind of thing. On the re -
anchoring proposed here, they are not. 
Relativistic time describes the temporal metric of the physical universe: the set of relations 
among physical events that determine their temporal ordering and the durations between them, 
as measured by physical clocks in various reference frames. It is a physical, measurable, 
operationally defined quantity. Its value varies with velocity relative to other frames and with 
gravitational potential. It is described by the mathematics of Riemannian geometry as applied 
to spacetime, and it is one of the most precisely confirmed quantities in the history of physical

===== PAGE 23 =====
23 
 
science. Relativistic physics has no competitor in its own domain, and the present argument 
raises no challenge to it [Maudlin, 2012]. 
Consciousness as ontological background condition—the domain to which absolute time is re-
anchored—is not an operational physical quantity in the sense that the Mach-Einstein critique 
demands. It does not have a value that varies with velocity or gravitational potential. It is not 
measured by clocks. It is not described by the metric tensor of general relativity. It belongs to 
a different level of description: not the physical level, at which events are related by measurable 
temporal intervals, but the ontological level, at which the background conditions for temporal 
experience are specified. The two accounts are not competitors; they are accounts of different 
things at different levels of description. 
The layered ontology that results from this analysis has a clear structure. At the physical level, 
relativistic time describes the temporal relations among physical events. At the 
phenomenological level, Husserlian inner time -consciousness describes the st ructure of 
temporal experience as it is lived—the retention-primal impression-protention structure of the 
living present, with its directionality and content -dependence. At the ontological background 
level, the structure to which Newton's absolute time is re-anchored describes the non-relational, 
equable, background-generative condition that underlies both physical temporal relations and 
phenomenological temporal experience. These three levels are distinct and irreducible; no 
account at one level eliminates the need for accounts at the other levels. 
Einstein did not eliminate the need for an account of the ontological background condition of 
temporal experience. He eliminated absolute time from physics—from the first level. The third 
level, to which absolute time is re -anchored, was never within the scope of his critique. The 
apparent conflict between Einstein and Newton on time dissolves when the levels are 
distinguished: Einstein is right about physical time; Newton's definition, properly re-anchored, 
is a description of the background ontological structure that physical time presupposes [Rovelli, 
2018]. 
6. Implications for the Philosophy of Time 
6.1 A Layered Ontology of Time 
The argument of this paper implies a layered ontology of time, in which distinct levels of 
temporal description are irreducible to one another but jointly necessary for a complete account 
of time. The physical level describes temporal relations among events, measured by clocks and

===== PAGE 24 =====
24 
 
governed by the spacetime metric of general relativity. The phenomenological level describes 
the structure of temporal experience as it is lived from the inside: the retention of the just-past, 
the immediacy of the present, the anticipation of the about -to-come. The ontological 
background level describes the non -relational, equably continuous, background -generative 
condition that makes both physical temporal relations and phenomenological temporal 
experience possible. 
Each level has its own appropriate methods of inquiry and its own appropriate conceptual 
vocabulary. Physical time is investigated by physics, using the tools of measurement, 
experiment, and mathematical modelling. Phenomenological time is investigated by 
phenomenological philosophy, using the method of careful first -person description and 
analysis of the structure of experience. The ontological background condition is investigated 
by ontological analysis, using the tools of structural comparison and abduct ive inference. No 
level reduces to the others; the methods appropriate to one level are not directly applicable at 
another level. 
A layered ontology of this kind is not unusual in philosophy. The physical description of colour 
in terms of electromagnetic wavelengths does not eliminate the phenomenological description 
of colour in terms of hue and saturation; both descriptions are leg itimate, and neither reduces 
to the other. Similarly, the physical description of time does not eliminate the 
phenomenological description of temporal experience, and neither eliminates the ontological 
description of the background condition that underlies both. The philosophical error—both in 
the history of philosophy of time and in the reception of Newton's absolute time —has been to 
assume that only one level of description is real, and that the others must be eliminated or 
reduced to it; contemporary philosophy of time has similarly resisted reductive accounts of 
temporal structure [Callender, 2017; Dainton, 2010]. 
6.2 The Category Error in Temporal Classification 
The history of absolute time can now be reread as a case study in ontological category error. 
Gilbert Ryle introduced the concept of a category mistake to describe cases in which an entity 
of one logical or ontological category is described or treated as i f it belonged to a different 
category. The classic example is the visitor who, having been shown the colleges, libraries, 
laboratories, and sports grounds of Oxford, asks where the University is —failing to recognise 
that the University is not a further bui lding but the organisation of which the colleges and 
libraries are parts. The mistake is not a factual error but a classification error: the visitor has

===== PAGE 25 =====
25 
 
assigned "University" to the wrong logical category, expecting it to be the same kind of thing 
as the buildings, when it is in fact a different kind of thing altogether. 
Absolute time is a case of the same structural mistake, operating in the opposite direction. 
Physicists and philosophers, inheriting Newton's definition in the context of a physics treatise, 
assigned absolute time to the category of physical quantities—expecting it to be the same kind 
of thing as length, mass, charge, and velocity. When it turned out not to behave like a physical 
quantity—when it proved impossible to measure, to operationalise, or to integrate into physical 
theory—the conclusion was that the concept was empty or false. But the failure to behave like 
a physical quantity is precisely what one would expect of a concept that belongs to a different 
ontological category. Assigning absolute time to the physical category and then discovering 
that it fails to behave like a physical quantity is not a discovery about the nature of time; it is a 
consequence of the misclassification. 
Mach and Einstein discovered that absolute time is not a valid physical quantity. They did not 
discover that absolute time is ontologically invalid. The difference is precisely the difference 
between finding that a concept fails in a domain where it does not belong, and concluding that 
it fails altogether. The category error was committed when the concept was assigned to the 
physical domain; the Mach -Einstein critique discovered the consequences of that error; the 
ontological re-anchoring proposed in this paper corrects it. 
6.3 Observer-Independent and Observer-Related Temporal Structures 
The distinction between the three levels of temporal description illuminates a persistent tension 
in the philosophy of time between objective and subjective accounts of temporal order. The 
tension arises from the fact that time seems, on the one hand, to be a feature of the objective 
physical world—events happen in a definite temporal order, durations can be measured, and 
the temporal structure of the universe is described by general relativity—and, on the other hand, 
to be intimately connected with conscio usness and experience —time is something we live 
through, something that matters to us, something whose passage is felt and not merely 
measured [Mellor, 1998]. 
The layered ontology proposed here does not resolve this tension by choosing one side. It 
resolves it by locating the different aspects of time at different levels of description. Physical 
time is observer -related in the specific sense that different obser vers in different reference 
frames assign different temporal coordinates to the same events. But it is not subjective in the 
sense of being mind-dependent: the spacetime metric is a feature of the physical world, not of

===== PAGE 26 =====
26 
 
any particular experiencing subject. Phenomenological time —Husserlian inner time -
consciousness—is observer-related in a different and stronger sense: it is constituted by the 
experiencing subject, and its structure (retention -primal impression-protention) is a structure 
of consciousness, not of the physical world. Ontological background time —the structure to 
which absolute time is re -anchored—is neither observer -relative in the physical sense nor 
constituted by any particular experiencing subject. It is the background condition of 
consciousness as such: not of any individual consciousness, but of consciousness as an 
ontological category. 
This analysis suggests a more nuanced treatment of temporal objectivity than the standard 
opposition between objective physical time and subjective psychological time provides. 
Physical time is objective in the sense of being measurable and intersubjectively confirmable, 
but it is observer -related in the sense of being frame -dependent. Phenomenological time is 
subjective in the sense of being constituted by individual experiencing subjects, but it is 
structured by features —the retention-primal impression-protention triad —that are structural 
features of consciousness as such, not merely of individual experience. Ontological background 
time is objective in the strongest sense: it is not relative to any reference fra me and not 
constituted by any individual subject. It is the background condition that underlies both 
physical temporal relations and phenomenological temporal experience, and it is therefore the 
most fundamental level of temporal description. Physical time provides an objective, observer-
related metric for ordering events within the physical universe. The ontological background 
structure provides the condition through which both physical temporal order and experiential 
temporal succession become possible. By treating these not as contradictory assertions about a 
single quantity, but as complementary descriptions of different ontological levels, the layered 
ontology proposed here resolves a long-standing impasse in the philosophy of time. Relativity 
did not supersede absolute time; it formalised the "relative, apparent, and common time", which 
is clock-measured time that Newton had clearly distinguished from absolute time—leaving the 
ontological background structure Newton described entirely untouched. 
7. Conclusion: Recovering the Lost Ontological Position of Absolute Time 
Newton's absolute time has been treated, since the early twentieth century, as a philosophical 
embarrassment—an intuitive but ultimately indefensible concept that the progress of physics 
correctly retired. The verdict delivered by Mach and reinforced by Einstein has been taken as 
final: absolute time describes nothing real, and its apparent plausibility was an ar tefact of

===== PAGE 27 =====
27 
 
limited human experience with low velocities and weak gravitational fields. This paper has 
argued that the verdict, while correct as a verdict on absolute time as a physical quantity, is not 
correct as a verdict on absolute time as an ontological category. 
The argument proceeded in four principal stages. In the first stage, close reading of Newton's 
Scholium identified three structural features of his definition of absolute time: ontological 
autonomy (it flows "of itself, and from its own nature"), intrinsic equability (it flows "equably"), 
and background generativity (it flows "without relation to anything external," as the condition 
within which all physical events are ordered). These three features were shown to be not merely 
anomalous but structurally incompatible with the category of physical quantities. The anomaly 
is not a deficiency of the concept; it is evidence that the concept does not belong to the  
measurable domain in physics. 
In the second stage, the phenomenological and ontological structure of consciousness was 
analysed. Husserl's account of inner time -consciousness was examined, and an important 
distinction was drawn between the inner temporal structure of human consciousnes s—which 
is relational, content-dependent, and constituted by the retention-primal impression-protention 
triad—and the deeper background condition that makes this temporal structure possible. 
Human consciousness, as it is actually experienced, is bound to p henomenological time 
through the structure of memory: if physical time were to reverse, the entire informational 
structure of memory that constitutes the content of temporal experience would reverse with it, 
demonstrating that phenomenological time is rela tional and direction-dependent in ways that 
Newton's absolute time is not. The ontological background condition —the self -grounding, 
equably continuous, background -generative capacity of consciousness —was then identified 
through abductive inference as the candidate structure for comparison with Newton's definition. 
In the third stage, a structural correspondence was established between Newton's three defining 
features and the three structural features of consciousness as ontological background condition. 
The correspondence holds at the level of conceptual organisatio n: autonomy corresponds to 
ontological autonomy, equability corresponds to intrinsic equability, and background 
generativity corresponds to background generativity. The correspondence is distinguished 
carefully from identity: the argument does not claim th at Newton's absolute time is 
consciousness, or that consciousness produces absolute time. It claims that the two share a 
structural form —a form that is native to the ontological domain of consciousness as 
background condition and anomalous in the physical domain.

===== PAGE 28 =====
28 
 
In the fourth stage, absolute time was re -anchored to the ontological domain on the basis of 
this structural correspondence. The Mach -Einstein critique was shown to reach only as far as 
the physical domain, leaving the ontological question entirely untouched. The apparent conflict 
between Newton and Einstein on time was dissolved by distinguishing three levels of temporal 
description: physical time (relativistic, measurable, frame-dependent), phenomenological time 
(inner time -consciousness, relational, cont ent-dependent), and ontological background time 
(non-relational, equable, background-generative). The three levels are distinct and irreducible; 
Einstein's results are decisive at the first level and have no direct implications for the third. 
Newton opened the Principia Mathematica with absolute time and absolute space as the 
anchoring posits of the entire work — the foundational ontological commitments upon which 
his mechanics was built. These were not peripheral assumptions but the deepest la yer of his 
theoretical architecture, the conditions he regarded as necessary for any coherent account of 
motion and physical law. Yet in the centuries of physical development that followed, physics 
increasingly adopted a different criterion for physical measurable reality: the primacy of 
measurable relations, operational definitions, and empirically accessible quantities. Within this 
emerging framework, the non -relational and non -operational character of Newton's absolute 
time became increasingly difficult to accommodate, and the Mach –Einstein transformation of 
temporal physics resolved this tension by replacing absolute temporal structure with relational 
and operational descriptions. 
Yet this resolution came at a profound cost. For two centuries, modern physics built on 
operational and measurement-based foundations had been unable to assign absolute time any 
position within its framework — not because Newton's definition was incoherent, but because 
the framework itself lacked the ontological depth to receive it. Einstein's transformation 
resolved this internal tension, and in doing so created the physics of the twenty -first century. 
But the cost of that creation was the wholesale displacement of Newton's original cosmological 
framework — a framework that was not merely a historical starting point but a direct 
description of the structure of the universe as it actually is. What Newton placed at the opening 
of the Principia was not merely a provisional hypothesis awaiting replacement. It was his 
foundational ontological claim about the structure required for any coherent account of 
physical reality. The Einsteinian revolution, celebrated as the greatest advance in the history of 
science, achieved its results by severing that connection — replacing Newton's original and 
correct cosmological ontology with a framework whose operational successes have obscured, 
for over a century, the magnitude of what was lost.

The present paper argues that the difficulty arose not because Newton's foundational intuition 
lacked coherence, but because the later physical framework evaluated all structures exclusively 
through the criterion of physical measurability. What was excluded from physics was therefore 
not an incoherent concept, but a category of structure that exceeded the operational boundaries 
of the framework itself. What Newton described was not defeated by evidence. It was displaced 
by a theoretical decision, and the ontological consequences of that displacement have remained 
unexamined until now. 
Modern physics, having adopted Newton as its founding ancestor, proceeded to reject one of 
that ancestor's most fundamental ontological settings — declaring it operationally meaningless, 
a metaphysical residue with no place in a science built on measurement and verification [Mach, 
1883/1960; Einstein, 1905] . The present paper does not dispute that absolute time fails the 
operationalist tests of modern physics. What it disputes is the inference drawn from that failure: 
that because absolute time cannot be op erationalised as a physical quantity, the structure 
Newton described does not exist and has no philosophical standing. 
In the current state of modern physics, which neither incorporates absolute time as a physical 
quantity nor provides any account of its ontological status, absolute time has been left without 
a home. The present paper offers, for the first time, a genuine ontological position for absolute 
time — one of the two great pillars of Newton's foundational ontology in the Principia. It argues 
that absolute time, properly reinterpreted, is not a failed physical concept but a structure that 
was always ontological in character, whose features correspond precisely to those of 
consciousness dimension, and whose removal from physics was a correction of a 
misclassification, not a demonstration that the structure does not exist. Newton described 
something real. What this paper identifies in Newton's definition is not a physical temporal 
parameter but the ontological order of consciousness dimension — a structure whose features 
Newton specified with precision, and a structure that the history of modern physics has, until 
now, left unnamed. 
That name is now given. 
Whether Newton himself recognised this correspondence is a biographical question this paper 
does not address; the argument concerns the structure of the concept, not the intentions of its 
author. Absolute time did not fail because it described nothing. It failed because physics 
demanded it become something it was never structurally designed to be. Returned to the 
ontological domain, it describes something real: the self -grounding, equably continuou s,
