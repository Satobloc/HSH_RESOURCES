2.2 Volatility-of-volatility in finance 
The financial economics literature has long recognized second -order uncertainty as a first -20 
order risk factor. Hull and White (1987) 13, Heston (1993) 11, and the stochastic -volatility 
tradition established that volatility itself must be modeled as a stochastic process; 
band/oscillator extensions (e.g., the Heston-model second moment) formalize vol-of-vol as the 
variance of the variance process. Empiricall y, Huang, Sialm, and Zhang (2024) 12 show that 
volatility-managed portfolios’ performance is driven by volatility -of-volatility rather than 25 
volatility itself—a result that anticipates our finding that VVIX (not VIX) governs the field’s 
mean-reversion strength (P1). Park (2024) 18 documents that volatility -of-volatility carries a 
distinct risk premium, implying markets price it as an independent state variable; our 
contribution is to identify which state variable it is in a field-theoretic system: the temperature.

2.4 Kramers Rate Theory 
The thermal Kramers law used in Section 3.3 originates with Kramers' 1940 model of a 5 
Brownian particle escaping a potential well under thermal agitation15, which related the escape 
rate to the barrier height and an effective diffusion (temperature-like) constant in what is now 
the standard reference for barrier-crossing rate theory. Hä nggi, Talkner and Borkovec's fifty-
years-later review consolidated the ex tensions of this theory across friction regimes and 
remains the standard modern reference 6. Both works concern literal thermal systems; our use 10 
of the same functional form for VIX microstructure is, as throughout this series, a mathematical 
borrowing whose justification rests on the plausibility of the analogy, not on a physical 
derivation from first principles. 
 
2.5 Phase transitions in financial markets 15 
Critical phenomena language has been applied to market crashes since Sornette 21 (2003) and 
the log -periodic-power-law literature; contango/backwardation regime switching has been 
modeled with Markov -switching dynamics (see also the HMM tradition in term -structure 
modeling). What this literature lacks is a  microstructurally grounded order parameter and an 
observable control variable. Our P3 supplies both: the order parameter is the well-count of the 20 
Onsager–Machlup effective potential 𝑈𝑒𝑓𝑓(𝑞₀|𝑇), and the control variable is the directly 
observable VVIX level. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration in the finance 
literature of a potential-landscape phase transition driven by an identifiable, tradeable index. 
 
2.6 Critical Phenomena and Crash Prediction in Finance 25 
Sornette's program on critical phenomena in financial markets is the most direct precedent for 
this paper's critical -temperature hypothesis: it proposes that market crashes are preceded by 
identifiable, physics-inspired precursor signatures (in Sornette's case, log -periodic power-law 
signatures tied to a renormalization-group argument)21 analogous to the approach to a critical 
point. We flag, in the same spirit as Section 2.4's discussion of the limits of the physics analogy, 30 
that this precedent is a cautionary one as much as a supportive one: Sornette's log -periodic 
power-law model has an extensively documented mixed empirical record as a predictive tool 
despite its theoretical elegance, and independent reviewers have repeated ly noted the gap 
between the model's conceptual sophistication and its out-of-sample forecasting performance. 
We see our own P1 –P4 results in Section 5 as continuous with that history rather than an 35 
exception to it, and try to report them with the same caution the crash-prediction literature has, 
in retrospect, needed.

Wilson’s criterion: an operator is irrelevant if its coupling flows to zero under coarse-graining.

6. Conclusion 
 5 
This paper added an explicit temperature variable, constructed from VVIX, to the field theory 
developed in Paper III, and derived four falsifiable predictions from it: mass softening (P1), a 
thermal Kramers law (P2), a critical temperature (P3), and a reno rmalization-group reversal 
near that critical temperature (P4). Testing these across four windows and a candidate 
tradeable signal, we find: P2 is directionally right but does not reach the R²  we had hoped for; 10 
P3's critical temperature is clusters tightly around T*≈−0.85; P1's mass-softening slope is too 
small and the underlying relationship too non -monotonic to call confirmed; P4 runs in the 
opposite direction from the prediction; the claimed thermodynamic–field unification does not 
hold on average; and the tradeable sig nal's hit-rate pattern is inverted often enough that we 
believe it couldn’t serve as a market signal, tradeable or otherwise. 15

We think the theoretical architecture, which recasting temperature as an explicit variable is a 
natural and, in our view, correct next step after Papers I –III. Several of the constructs (the 
thermal Kramers law's consistent sign, in particular) point in directions worth pursuing further. 
But this paper's contribution, on the evidence in hand, is the falsifiable framework and the 
honest audit of it, not a validated  result. We think this is a genuinely useful outcome for a 5 
falsifiable research program: one prediction gained real support, doing exactly what our own 
diagnosis said it should if given the chance, which is some of the strongest evidence in this 
series so far that the underlying framework is tracking something real rather than fitting noise. 
The other four did not move the same way, and we report that plainly.
