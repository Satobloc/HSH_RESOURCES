Chromium trichloride (CrCl ₃ ) and alpha-ruthenium trichloride ( α -RuCl ₃ ) are, on a
crystal-structure diagram, virtually indistinguishable: both belong to the layered
transition-metal trihalide family, both host metal ions at the vertices of a
honeycomb lattice, and both undergo the same type of temperature-driven
structural rearrangement as they are cooled from room temperature. Yet in
practice, CrCl ₃  is structurally robust — it cycles through that rearrangement
gracefully and recovers — while α -RuCl ₃  progressively degrades with each cycle,
accumulating stacking faults that alter its magnetic ordering temperature,
suppress its thermal conductivity, and make its experimental behavior nearly
impossible to reproduce from laboratory to laboratory.
The study, led by Zachary Morgan of ORNL's Neutron Scattering Division
together with Iris Ye, Jiasen Guo, and materials scientists Michael A. McGuire and
Jiaqiang Yan, used neutron single-crystal diffraction at the Spallation Neutron
Source to track both materials simultaneously through their structural transitions
— the first direct, side-by-side comparison of this kind on single crystals of both
compounds.
In a transition-metal trihalide, metal ions — chromium in CrCl ₃ , ruthenium in α -
RuCl ₃  — sit at the vertices of a honeycomb lattice inside each layer. Those layers
are stacked on top of one another and held together not by strong chemical
bonds but by weak van der Waals forces: the same gentle intermolecular
attraction that allows graphite to flake off onto paper.
That weak interlayer coupling is what makes these materials so attractive for
device engineering. It allows layers to be mechanically peeled away to atomically
thin sheets, which can then be reassembled into heterostructures — layered
sandwiches of different two-dimensional materials assembled like atomic-scale
building blocks. Chromium trihalides including CrCl ₃  have already been
demonstrated as spin-filter magnetic tunnel junctions with tunneling
magnetoresistance values ranging from 17,000% to 57,000%, opening a route
toward voltage-controlled magnetic memory.

But weak coupling also means the stacking arrangement is energetically fragile.
At high temperature, both CrCl ₃  and α -RuCl ₃  adopt a monoclinic structure in
which adjacent layers are offset in a near-face-centered-cubic pattern. On
cooling, both transition to a rhombohedral structure in which the layers adopt a
different lateral offset — closer to hexagonal close-packing. This first-order
structural phase transition, driven by the collective sliding of layers relative to one
another, reshuffles the exchange pathways through which magnetic interactions
are transmitted between layers.
The central question the ORNL team set out to answer: does that reshuffling
happen the same way in both materials, and does it couple to magnetism the
same way?
To answer that question, the team used neutron single-crystal diffraction — the
technique of choice for probing both crystal structure and magnetic order
simultaneously, because neutrons carry their own magnetic moment and scatter
from both atomic and magnetic arrangements. By tracking Bragg peaks (sharp
reflections from long-range crystalline order) and diffuse scattering (broader,
smeared intensity from short-range correlations or disorder) as the temperature
swept through the transition range, they could watch each material rearrange
and document what happened on either side of the transition.
The contrast was stark.
CrCl ₃  handled the transition smoothly. Its in-plane lattice parameter — the
spacing between chromium ions within a single honeycomb plane — evolved
continuously and without any abrupt jump as the temperature passed through
the transition. The structural changes were reversible: heat the crystal through the
transition, cool it back down, and it returned to essentially the same state it
started from, with no progressive accumulation of damage across repeated
cycles.
Magnetically, CrCl ₃  ordered into an A-type antiferromagnetic structure at a Néel
temperature of 14 K — a configuration in which adjacent honeycomb planes
align their spins antiparallel, while spins within each plane align ferromagnetically.
But what stood out was what happened above that ordering temperature: diffuse
magnetic scattering — the neutron signature of short-range spin correlations —
persisted all the way up to roughly 40 K, some 26 degrees above the point where
true long-range magnetic order snaps into place. CrCl ₃ 's spins remain correlated
and locally organized long before they commit to global magnetic order.
α -RuCl ₃  told the opposite story. Its in-plane lattice parameter did not evolve
smoothly — it jumped discontinuously across the structural transition, showing
pronounced hysteresis between the heating and cooling legs of the same thermal
cycle. More critically, repeating the cycle caused the crystal to progressively
degrade. Each thermal trip accumulated stacking faults that did not heal on
cooling — a ratchet-like buildup of crystalline disorder.

Magnetically, α -RuCl ₃  showed a complete absence of diffuse scattering above its
ordering temperature of 7.6 K. Where CrCl ₃ 's spins remained correlated far above
T_N, those in α -RuCl ₃  appeared to disengage abruptly at the ordering
temperature, leaving no short-range magnetic precursor.
The key to understanding the contrast lies in the fundamentally different
electronic characters of chromium and ruthenium in these compounds.
Chromium in CrCl ₃  is a 3d transition metal — a lighter element — with three
electrons in its d-shell, all with parallel spins. Its magnetic interactions are
dominated by conventional Heisenberg-type exchange: isotropic, relatively short-
ranged, and insensitive to the precise geometry of the crystal lattice beyond
nearest-neighbor distances. When CrCl ₃  layers slide into a new stacking
configuration during the structural transition, the magnetic exchange pathways
shift, but the Heisenberg exchange adapts smoothly. The in-plane bonds do not
need to strain abruptly to accommodate the new configuration.
Ruthenium in α -RuCl ₃  is a 4d metal — heavier — and its greater atomic number
brings strong spin-orbit coupling into play. Spin-orbit coupling is the relativistic
interaction between an electron's spin and its orbital motion; in heavier elements
like ruthenium, it is strong enough to fundamentally entangle spin and orbital
degrees of freedom into a new effective quantum number (J_eff = 1/2).
This is precisely what makes α -RuCl ₃  famous: it is one of the leading candidate
materials for realizing a Kitaev quantum spin liquid — a theoretically predicted
state, solved exactly by mathematician Alexei Kitaev in 2006, in which spins on a
honeycomb lattice are so highly entangled that they never magnetically order,
instead hosting exotic fractionalized excitations called Majorana fermions. The
2019 review of Kitaev materials by Takagi, Takayama, Jackeli, Khaliullin, and
Nagler provides a comprehensive account of the concept and its material
realizations.
The magnetic interactions that produce this state — the Kitaev couplings — are
bond-dependent and highly anisotropic: each of the three types of bonds in the
honeycomb lattice carries a different spin component interaction. This means the
interactions are exquisitely sensitive to the precise geometry of the ruthenium-
chloride-ruthenium exchange pathways — and hence to the exact layer stacking.
When α -RuCl ₃  layers slide during the structural transition, the resulting change in
exchange pathway geometry is not something the in-plane lattice can
accommodate elastically. The abrupt jump in the in-plane lattice parameter —
and the progressive damage — is the mechanical consequence of that geometric
sensitivity.
In CrCl ₃ , by contrast, the Heisenberg-dominated exchange imposes no equivalent
geometric constraint. Layers slide, the stacking rearranges, and the in-plane
bonds remain unperturbed.
The structural fragility of α -RuCl ₃  is not merely an inconvenience for
experimentalists. It is the root cause of one of the most consequential
reproducibility problems in contemporary condensed matter physics.

For the past decade, α -RuCl ₃  has been the community's best bet for observing
Majorana fermions in a material setting — not as individual exotic particles in
expensive cryogenic setups, but as collective excitations in a relatively ordinary-
looking black crystal. Topological quantum computers would use Majorana
fermions to store information in a form that is inherently resistant to
decoherence: rather than isolating a qubit from its environment (the conventional
approach), topological quantum computing would encode information in the
global entanglement pattern of the spin liquid itself, making it robust to any local
perturbation.
The smoking-gun signature of that state is a half-quantized thermal Hall
conductance — a precise fractional value that emerges from Majorana edge
modes when a magnetic field is applied to α -RuCl ₃ . Several research groups have
reported seeing this half-quantized value. Several others have not been able to
reproduce it, or have measured values that fall far short of quantization — a
controversy documented in preprint form and debated actively across the field.
Prior ORNL work published in Physical Review Materials in 2024 — by McGuire,
Yan, and co-workers, several of whom are also authors on the current study —
showed that the thermal Hall conductance measurement in α -RuCl ₃  is strongly
sample-dependent, and that samples with more stacking disorder systematically
show lower values that push the measured result further from the half-quantized
target. Crystals with minimal stacking disorder show higher thermal conductivity
and a thermal Hall conductance that approaches the predicted quantized value.
The current study explains why stacking disorder accumulates in α -RuCl ₃ : the
abrupt in-plane lattice jump during the first-order structural transition generates
elastic strains that damage the crystal's layer stacking — and that damage adds
up with every thermal cycle a sample undergoes during growth, post-growth
annealing, or repeated experimental cycling. CrCl ₃  does not have this problem
because its Heisenberg-dominated exchange does not lock the in-plane bonds to
the stacking geometry the way Kitaev coupling does in α -RuCl ₃ .
In other words: the property that makes α -RuCl ₃  scientifically extraordinary — the
bond-directional, spin-orbit-driven Kitaev exchange — is the same property that
makes its crystals mechanically fragile. The exotic physics and the structural
weakness share a common root.
Both CrCl ₃  and α -RuCl ₃  switch between two well-defined crystal structures as they
pass through the structural transition. At high temperature, both adopt a
monoclinic arrangement (space group C2/m), in which adjacent honeycomb
layers are offset from one another by a lateral displacement that corresponds
roughly to face-centered-cubic layer ordering. On cooling below the transition
temperature — which occurs near 240 K (−33°F) for CrCl ₃  and near 150 K
(−189°F) for α -RuCl ₃  — both switch to a rhombohedral arrangement (space
group R 3̄ ), in which the lateral displacement corresponds to a different close-
packed ordering.
The difference the ORNL team documented is in how that switching happens in
the two materials. In CrCl ₃ , the lattice breathes smoothly through the change. In
α -RuCl ₃ , the abruptness of the transition — visible as a discontinuous jump in the
in-plane lattice parameter, and as a pronounced difference between the heating
and cooling curves — is the mechanical signature of a system whose in-plane
bonding chemistry is being pulled into conflict with its stacking geometry as the
layers try to slide.

The c-lattice parameter (the spacing between layers, measured perpendicular to
the honeycomb planes) shows a step-like change in both materials — consistent
with the expected stacking rearrangement. It is the in-plane response that
diverges, and that divergence is the key finding.
For researchers building spintronic devices from van der Waals magnetic layers,
the study's message is straightforward: structural reversibility under thermal
cycling cannot be assumed from crystal-structure analogy alone.
CrCl ₃  and its chromium trihalide relatives — CrBr ₃  and CrI ₃  — have attracted
intense interest as magnetic layers in van der Waals heterostructure devices. In
those architectures, a magnetic CrX ₃  layer sandwiched between graphene
contacts creates an atomically thin spin filter: switching the magnetization state
of the CrX ₃  layer changes the tunneling resistance dramatically, enabling voltage-
controlled magnetic memory and logic without traditional magnetic materials.
The ORNL result validates CrCl ₃ 's suitability for those applications by confirming
that it cycles through its structural transition cleanly and without accumulating
disorder that would unpredictably shift its magnetic switching behavior. Research
on CrCl ₃  flakes to monolayer thickness further supports the path toward scalable
device manufacturing.
For α -RuCl ₃  and topological quantum computing, the implications are more
sobering in the short term. The progressive stacking disorder accumulation
means that samples degrade with use, making sample selection, preparation, and
characterization not incidental to the experiment but central to it. The ORNL
team's prior work established criteria for selecting minimal-disorder crystals: a
structural transition near 140 K on cooling, and a single magnetic anomaly near
7.6 K in specific heat. The new study explains the mechanism behind those criteria
— a crystal that shows the transition cleanly near 140 K has not yet accumulated
the in-plane strain damage that pushes the transition temperature down and
smears it.
More broadly, the study establishes that in van der Waals magnets, structural
analogy does not imply functional equivalence: materials with the same space
group and the same nominal crystal architecture can differ fundamentally in how
their layers respond to thermal stress, and how that stress couples back to
magnetic behavior. For quantum device engineering, where reproducibility is not
a statistical luxury but an operational requirement, the coupling between
structural mechanics and electronic configuration is a design variable that must
be tested explicitly.
The methodological choice at the heart of the study — neutron single-crystal
diffraction using the CORELLI elastic diffuse scattering spectrometer at ORNL's
Spallation Neutron Source — was critical to the findings. Neutrons interact with
both the nuclear and magnetic structure of a material in a single experiment,
allowing the team to track structural and magnetic order simultaneously as a
function of temperature, rather than running separate measurements that might
reflect slightly different sample conditions.

A Kitaev quantum spin liquid is a state of matter predicted by mathematician
Alexei Kitaev in 2006, in which spins on a honeycomb lattice are quantum
mechanically entangled in a specific topological pattern without ever settling into
ordinary magnetic order. The excitations of this state — called Majorana fermions
— obey a type of quantum statistics (non-Abelian anyons under a magnetic field)
that could be used to store and process quantum information in a form that is
inherently resistant to the environmental disturbances that cause ordinary qubits
to fail. Alpha-ruthenium trichloride ( α -RuCl ₃ ) is currently the most studied
material candidate for hosting this state.
Multiple research groups have reported conflicting measurements of the same
key property — the thermal Hall conductance, which should show a precise half-
integer quantized value if Majorana fermions are present. Prior ORNL work
established that the measurement is strongly sample-dependent: crystals with
more stacking disorder (layers offset from their ideal positions) produce lower
thermal conductivity and measurements that fall further from the predicted
quantized value. The new Morgan et al. study explains why stacking disorder
accumulates in α -RuCl ₃ : the abrupt, hysteretic jump in the in-plane lattice
parameter during the structural transition generates elastic damage that adds up
with each thermal cycle. This structural explanation for the reproducibility
problem is a significant advance.
CrCl ₃ 's magnetism is governed by isotropic Heisenberg exchange, which is not
geometrically sensitive to the precise stacking arrangement of the honeycomb
layers. As a result, CrCl ₃  cycles through its structural phase transition smoothly —
the in-plane lattice parameter evolves continuously, with no abrupt jump, and the
crystal does not accumulate damage. This structural reversibility means CrCl ₃  can
withstand the thermal cycling inherent in device manufacturing and repeated
operation without progressively shifting its magnetic properties — a requirement
that α -RuCl ₃ , which degrades with each cycle, cannot currently meet. Research on
growing CrCl ₃  to monolayer thickness confirms its promise for scalable spintronic
applications.

Diffuse magnetic scattering is the broad, spread-out neutron signal that arises
when spins in a material are correlated over short distances — organizing
themselves into local patches of order — without yet committing to the long-
range, fully-ordered magnetic state. In CrCl ₃ , this diffuse scattering persists up to
about 40 K, roughly 26 K above the temperature at which true long-range
magnetic order locks in at 14 K. That persistence reflects the gradual, isotropic
nature of Heisenberg exchange: spins build up local coherence progressively over
a wide temperature range before snapping into global order. In α -RuCl ₃ , no
equivalent diffuse scattering was detected above the 7.6 K ordering temperature
— consistent with the anisotropic, bond-directional Kitaev exchange, which
suppresses the conventional precursor fluctuations that CrCl ₃ 's Heisenberg
exchange readily supports.
