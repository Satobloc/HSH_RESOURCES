The Boundary Between Matter and Radiation 
An Unresolved Classificatory Inconsistency in Modern Physics 
Juliet Zhong 
Independent Researcher, London, United Kingdom 
ORCID: 0009-0006-5099-3671 
 
 
Abstract 
Modern physics employs two classificatory frameworks that have never been formally 
reconciled: the Standard Model’s fermion/boson taxonomy and the inherited 
matter/radiation terminology of nineteenth-century physics. This paper argues that their 
coexistence conceals a genuine classi ficatory inconsistency. The term “radiation” 
includes electrons, classified as fermion matter particles, and photons, which are 
massless gauge bosons. Beta radiation is an electron flux; gamma radiation is a photon 
flux. Three arguments are developed. Firs t, the historical formation of this 
inconsistency is traced: radiation terminology was extended to material and non -
material entities without conceptual revision. Second, electron–photon interconversion 
through pair production and annihilation provides dyn amical evidence against a 
categorical opposition between the two. Third, radiation is reinterpreted as a category 
of transferable field excitations rather than as an ontological opposite of matter. Within 
this framework, the electron is defined as a localised quantum excitation of the electron 
field, not itself a material structure but a constituent excitation from which material 
structures are formed. The ontological status of invariant mass remains an open 
foundational question. Matter is reassigned to th e structural level, where the concept 
remains consistent. On this basis, the hypothesis is proposed that the electron's non -
zero invariant mass may represent the effective behaviour of a more fundamental 
massless field structure in a bound configuration; t hree falsification conditions are 
stated.

1. Introduction: Two Coexisting Classification Systems 
Every working physicist uses the words "matter" and "radiation." These terms appear 
in textbooks at every level, in the names of experimental programmes, in the framing 
of theoretical questions, and in the language with which physics communicates with 
adjacent sciences and with the public. Yet if one asks where, in the modern literature, 
the boundary between matter and radiation was precisely defined —defined in a way 
that is compatible with the Standard Model, consistent across subfields, and not merely 
inherited from nineteenth-century convention—the answer is surprisingly elusive. 
The Standard Model of particle physics provides a rigorous and experimentally 
validated taxonomy of fundamental constituents. All known elementary particles are 
either fermions or bosons. Fermions—quarks and leptons—have half-integer spin and 
obey Fermi–Dirac statistics, which, through the spin–statistics connection, leads to the 
Pauli exclusion principle for identical fermions.  Bosons—the gauge bosons of the 
strong, weak, and electromagnetic forces, and the Higgs scalar—have integer spin and 
obey Bose –Einstein statistics. The photon is a gauge boson of the electromagnetic 
interaction. The electron is a lepton, hence a fermion. This classification is rooted in 
the representation theory of the Lorentz group and has direct, measurable experimental 
content [Weinberg, 1995; Griffiths, 2008]. 
Alongside this taxonomy, a second and older classification continues in use: matter 
versus radiation. This distinction predates quantum mechanics and derives its intuitive 
force from classical mechanics and classical electrodynamics. Matter was the substance 
of atoms and molecules —localised, massive, and subject to Newtonian inertia. 
Radiation was the propagation of energy through the electromagnetic field —
continuous, massless, and immaterial. The distinction felt absolute because, within 
classical physics, it was: no material object was also a wave in the electromagnetic field, 
and no electromagnetic wave had mass or occupied a definite position.

Quantum mechanics and the subsequent development of quantum field theory did not 
simply complicate this picture; they rendered the classical dichotomy untenable in 
several respects. Wave –particle duality showed that entities previously classified as 
material exhibited wave -like interference phenomena, while entities previously 
classified as radiation exhibited particle -like localisation and momentum. The field-
theoretic framework of the Standard Model placed both categories within a common 
field-theoretic description: both the electron and the photon are described as excitations 
of quantum fields, differing in the type of field, the nature of the excitation, and the 
associated quantum numbers, but sharing the same fundamental mode of description. 
What has not happened, in the century since these developments, is a formal revision 
of the matter/radiation terminology to reflect the new physics. The Standard Model 
provides a precise taxonomy, but physics education and scientific discourse continue 
to use the classical vocabulary alongside it, without reconciliation.  The present paper 
argues that this unreconciled coexistence has produced a genuine terminological and 
classificatory inconsistency within the existing body of physics literature, and that this 
inconsistency has consequences for how fundamental questions about the nature of the 
electron and the photon are posed and answered. 
The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 traces the historical formation of the concept 
of radiation and documents how the term was extended to cover ontologically 
heterogeneous entities. Section 3 states the inconsistency precisely, using only 
definitions accepted in the standard literature. Section 4 examines electron –photon 
interconversion as a dynamical argument against the categorical opposition of the two. 
Section 5 proposes a reinterpreted classificatory basis. Section 6 provides a positive 
definition of the electron within this framework and addresses the question of matter at 
the constituent level. Section 7 addresses the status of matter at the structural level. 
Section 8 concludes. 
Two terminological clarifications at the outset. "Radiation" in what follows refers to 
the term as used across physics broadly —electromagnetic radiation, radioactive 
emission, and related usages —not exclusively to ionising radiation or to cosmic rays. 
"Matter" refers to the classical category of massive, substantial entities, not to any 
cosmological technical usage. The inconsistency addressed is a problem of physical

2. How "Radiation" Acquired Its Meaning: A Historical Survey 
To understand how the term "radiation" came to carry incompatible ontological 
commitments, it is necessary to trace its conceptual history from classical 
electrodynamics through the early twentieth century. The story is not one of confusion 
introduced by c areless terminology; it is a story of terminology extended under 
experimental pressure, faster than conceptual revision could follow, and never 
subsequently brought into order. 
In the framework of classical electrodynamics, as systematised by Maxwell, radiation 
meant electromagnetic waves [Maxwell, 1865]. These were disturbances of the 
electromagnetic field propagating through space at the speed of light, carrying energy 
and momentum but no rest mass. The characterisation was clear and its ontological 
commitment equally clear: radiation was immaterial. Matter, in the same framework, 
was the substance of atoms and molecules —subject to inertia, gravity, and contact 
forces. Radiation and matter interacted, but they were ontologically distinct. 
This classical picture was under internal pressure before the discoveries of radioactivity. 
Planck's analysis of blackbody radiation (1900) required that electromagnetic energy 
be exchanged in discrete units [Planck, 1900]. Einstein's 1905 analysis of the 
photoelectric effect argued that the behaviour of light in that context was most 
economically explained by treating radiation as composed of discrete energy packets 
[Einstein, 1905]. The photon concept introduced a particle character to radiation. On 
the other side, Hertz's experimental confirmation of electromagnetic waves in 1888 had 
consolidated the continuous field picture [Hertz, 1888]. The ontological status of 
radiation was already undergoing revision before radioactivity forced the terminology 
further outward. 
The discovery of radioactivity opened a second and decisive front of terminological 
pressure. Becquerel observed in 1896 that uranium salts spontaneously emitted 
something capable of producing detectable effects even when shielded from visible 
light [Becquerel, 1896]. The Curies systematically investigated this phenomenon and 
identified radium and polonium as intensely radioactive elements [Curie and Curie,

1898]. The emissions from radioactive materials were called "radiation" by analogy 
with electromagnetic radiation: they were invisible, penetrating, and detectable by their 
ionising effects. The naming was operationally motivated and experimentally 
convenient. It was not conceptually examined. 
The differentiation of radioactive emissions into distinct types came rapidly. Rutherford 
showed in 1899 that uranium radiation contained at least two components with very 
different penetrating powers, labelled alpha and beta [Rutherford, 1899]. Villard 
identified a third component in 1900, eventually named gamma radiation [Villard, 
1900]. The physical identification of these components took further years. Alpha 
particles were shown to be helium -4 nuclei —two protons and two neutrons, a 
composite massive obj ect [Rutherford and Geiger, 1908]. Beta radiation was 
established as an electron flux: the same particles identified by Thomson in cathode ray 
experiments [Thomson, 1897; Chadwick, 1914]. Gamma radiation was established as 
high-energy electromagnetic radia tion—photons—of the same fundamental nature as 
visible light and X-rays, differing only in frequency. 
The significance of this identification for the matter/radiation distinction was not drawn 
at the time, and it has not been drawn since. Under the Standard Model, the three 
"radiations" of radioactive decay represent three entirely different ontological 
categories. Alpha radiation is a composite nuclear bound state —matter in the fullest 
classical sense. Beta radiation is an electron: a fundamental fermion, conventionally 
classified as a matter particle in Standard Model discussions.  Gamma radiation is a 
photon: a gauge boson, massless, not classified as a matter particle in the Standard 
Model. The word "radiation" had been extended, by experimental necessity and 
conceptual inertia, to cover all three —a helium nucleus, an electron, and a photon —
without any acknowledgement that this grouping placed ontologically heterogeneous 
entities under a single term. 
The terminology was actively reinforced by the institutional and pedagogical structure 
of early twentieth-century physics. Nuclear physics and radiation physics developed as 
applied fields with strong connections to medicine, engineering, and military 
applications. In these contexts, the operational definition of "radiation" —something 
that penetrates matter, ionises gases, and can be detected by its effects —was entirely 
adequate. The operational aims of these fields did not require a resolution of their

deeper ontological classification.  The Standard Model, when it arrived, provided the 
right conceptual tools, but it was developed in a different scientific context, and no 
formal revision of the broader radiation vocabulary followed. 
The 1927 Solvay Conference illustrates how acute the terminological tension had 
become by the mid-1920s. The central debates concerned the interpretation of quantum 
mechanics—the status of the wave function, the relationship between wave and particle 
descriptions [Bacciagaluppi and Valentini, 2009]. The wave –particle duality of both 
matter and radiation was acknowledged, but it was treated as a puzzle about the 
relationship between descriptions, not as a signal that the matter/radiation distinction 
itself required revision. The language of "radiant energy" for electromagnetic waves 
and "corpuscular rays" for particle beams persisted as separate traditions, both in 
tension with a unified quantum field picture that was only beginning to be articulated. 
The conclusion of this historical survey is as follows. The term "radiation" in modern 
physics carries the trace of two distinct conceptual traditions: the electromagnetic 
tradition, in which radiation is immaterial wave propagation, and the 
nuclear/radiological tradition, in which radiation designates any penetrating emission 
from a radioactive source, regardless of material character. These two traditions were 
never reconciled. The result is a term that simultaneously includes and excludes 
material particles depending on which tradition is operative. This is the historical root 
of the inconsistency; the next section states it with logical precision. 
3. The Inconsistency Made Explicit: Matter Inside Radiation 
The historical survey of the preceding section documents how the inconsistency arose. 
The present section states it precisely, using only propositions individually accepted in 
the standard literature. No new theoretical claim is introduced; the classificatory 
inconsistency is entirely internal to the existing terminology of physics. 
Consider the following four propositions, each endorsed by mainstream physics texts. 
Proposition (a): In conventional Standard Model terminology, electrons belong to the 
fermionic matter sector. Electrons are members of the first generation of leptons, with 
non-zero rest mass (0.511 MeV/c²), spin -1/2, and charge −e. Leptons, together with 
quarks, constitute the fermionic sector conventionally associated with matter particles,

===== PAGE 7 =====
7 
 
as opposed to the gauge bosons, which are force carriers [Griffiths, 2008; Halzen and 
Martin, 1984; Particle Data Group, 2022].  This classification is grounded in the 
mathematical structure of the theory and in the physical differences between fermions 
and bosons. 
Proposition (b): In nuclear physics and radiation physics, beta radiation is defined as 
the emission of electrons (beta -minus) or positrons (beta -plus) from unstable atomic 
nuclei. Beta radiation is universally listed as one of the three principal types of ionising 
radiation, alongside alpha and gamma [Krane, 1988; Knoll, 2010; Turner, 2007]. Health 
physics, radiation protection standards, and nuclear engineering texts define beta 
radiation as an electron flux. 
Proposition (c): From (a) and (b) together, the electron is simultaneously a matter 
particle (Standard Model) and a component of radiation (nuclear physics). The same 
physical entity is included within a category of radiation in one disciplinary context 
while classified as matter in another, without a corresponding terminological 
reconciliation in either body of literature. 
Proposition (d): The photon is classified as radiation—electromagnetic radiation in the 
general case, and gamma radiation in the high-energy case. The photon is a gauge boson 
of the electromagnetic interaction, with zero rest mass, spin -1, and no electric charge. 
It is not classified as a matter particle in the Standard Model [Weinberg, 1995; Particle 
Data Group, 2022]. 
The logical upshot is direct. If matter and radiation are mutually exclusive categories, 
then proposition (c) is a contradiction: the electron cannot be both a matter particle and 
a component of radiation. If they are not mutually exclusive—if something can be both 
matter and radiation —then the matter/radiation boundary is not the dichotomy it 
purports to be, and it requires formal redefinition. Either conclusion demonstrates that 
the existing classificatory framework is inadequate. 
Two objections present themselves. The first is that "radiation" in the nuclear physics 
sense and "radiation" in the electromagnetic sense are simply two different technical 
usages of the same word —homonymy rather than inconsistency. This objection, if 
accepted, concedes the central point: the same scientific vocabulary carries two 
incompatible technical meanings in different subfields without formal disambiguation.

Standard texts in nuclear physics define radiation as inclusive of both electrons and 
photons without distinguishing two separate senses, treating the category as unified 
while assigning it members that belong to ontologically different Standard Model 
classes. This objection shifts the issue from contradiction to unresolved terminology; 
the classificatory ambiguity remains, and with it the consequence: the matter/radiation 
distinction does not provide a coherent basis for fundamental classification. 
The second objection is that the matter/radiation distinction was never intended as a 
fundamental ontological boundary—only as a practical operational classification. This 
objection is historically questionable; the distinction has been treated as ontologically 
significant throughout its history and in foundational discussions. But even if it were 
accepted, it would deepen rather than resolve the problem: a distinction that is merely 
operational cannot legitimately bear the ontological weight that has been placed on it. 
The task of the remainder of this paper is to provide a replacement classification that 
does not carry this internal inconsistency. 
There is a further, less visible consequence worth noting. The partial coincidence 
between the Standard Model's fermion/boson distinction and the classical 
matter/radiation distinction has allowed the inconsistency to persist unnoticed. In 
standard cases, fermions are material (quarks, leptons) and bosons are radiation -like 
(photons, gauge bosons), and the electron fits this casual mapping. But the mapping is 
not identity. The fermion/boson distinction is a classification of statistical behaviour 
and spin; the matter/radiation distinction is an ontological claim about the nature of 
physical entities. Treating them as interchangeable has obscured the fact that 
"radiation" in nuclear physics is doing something ontologically different from 
"radiation" in electrodynamics. The solution is not to continue conflating them but to 
adopt a classificatory basis that generates no inconsistency —which is the task of 
Section 5. 
4. Conditional Reversibility: Electron–Photon Interconversion 
The terminological argument of the preceding section establishes that the 
matter/radiation boundary is internally inconsistent within existing physics, using only 
standard definitions. The present section introduces a second, dynamical argument: the 
known physics of electron –photon interconversion provides additional grounds for

questioning whether the electron and the photon should be placed in categorically 
opposed ontological classes. 
4.1 Electron–Positron Annihilation 
When an electron and a positron meet with sufficiently low relative velocity, they may 
annihilate: 
e⁻ + e⁺ → 2γ 
The products are two photons. The two-photon requirement is not arbitrary; it follows 
from the simultaneous conservation of energy, linear momentum, and angular 
momentum. In the centre -of-momentum frame, a single photon would need to carry 
zero total momentum while carrying the full energy of the annihilating pair —a 
condition that is kinematically impossible for a massless particle. Two photons emitted 
in opposite directions with equal energies satisfy all conservation laws [Dirac, 1930; 
Berestetskii, Lifshitz, and Pitaevskii, 1982]. Each photon carries approximatel y 0.511 
MeV in the case where the e lectron and positron are initially at rest, corresponding 
exactly to the electron rest-mass energy. The amplitude contains two QED vertices and 
is therefore of order e².  It has been verified to great precision and forms the physical 
basis of positron emission tomography [Knoll, 2010]. 
What this process demonstrates is that the entire energy and all the quantum numbers 
of an electron–positron pair can be transferred completely into photons. The electron –
positron state conversion is total under the allowed conditions: nothing of the pair' s 
quantum state, beyond what is encoded in the two outgoing photons, persists in the final 
state. 
4.2 Pair Production 
The reverse process exists: 
γ → e⁻ + e⁺ 
A high-energy photon can produce an electron –positron pair. This process requires a 
condition that the simple notation does not display. A single photon in vacuum cannot 
produce a pair while conserving both energy and momentum simultaneously: the 
photon has zero invariant mass while the pair has invariant mass of at least 2m_e c² =

1.022 MeV. The momentum mismatch must be absorbed by an external agent, typically 
the Coulomb field of a nucleus, or alternatively by a second photon:

where the combined four-momentum of the two photons can carry sufficient invariant

The conditionality of pair production is important and must not be overstated. Electron–
photon interconversion is not a free or spontaneous process; it is a conditional, 
conservation-law-governed quantum transition. Overstating the reversibility would 
misrepresent the physics. The present paper does not claim that electrons and photons 
are identical entities, or that any photon can spontaneously become an electron. 
4.3 The Classificatory Implication 
What the conditionality does not alter is the classificatory significance of the 
interconversion. The fact that an electron –positron pair can be completely and 
reversibly converted into photons —and that photons under specified conditions 
produce electron–positron pairs—means that the two kinds of entity are dynamically 
coupled at the most fundamental level. They are connected by actual physical processes 
in which one form of field excitation becomes another. 
Physics has repeatedly found that when two apparently distinct entities can be mutually 
converted under specifiable conditions, a deeper theoretical unification follows. In 
some historical cases, conditional interconvertibility between apparently distinct 
entities has motivated the search for a deeper classificatory framework. The pattern is 
not limited to gauge unification. The proton and neutron, once treated as distinct 
fundamental entities, were recognised as two isospin states of a common nucleon 
structure, a revision that preceded and motivated the quark model [Heisenberg, 1932]. 
The recognition that the photon and the electroweak gauge bosons are different 
manifestations of a common gauge structure is one such case [Glashow, 1961; 
Weinberg, 1967; Salam, 1968]. The analogy with the electron –photon case is not 
exact—annihilation and pair production involve particle creation, not rotation within a 
multiplet—but the broader pattern provides motivation: interconvertibility has in 
several instances preceded classificatory revision.

The argument of this section is bounded precisely. The existence of conditional 
reversible interconversion between electrons and photons provides a physical reason to 
question their placement in categorically opposed ontological classes. A classification 
system in which entities are placed in mutually exclusive fundamental categories, but 
in which one can become the other under specifiable conditions, is a classification 
system under tension. That tension is what the proposal in Section 5 is designed to 
resolve. 
5. A Reinterpreted Category: Field Excitation as the Basis of Classification 
The preceding sections have established two lines of argument against the classical 
matter/radiation dichotomy. The terminological argument shows that the same physical 
object—the electron—is simultaneously classified as matter and as radiation within the 
existing literature. The dynamical argument shows that entities placed in opposed 
ontological categories undergo conditional reversible interconversion, a pattern that has 
historically moti vated classificatory unification. The present section proposes a 
replacement classificatory basis. 
5.1 The Proposed Reinterpretation 
The proposal is as follows. Radiation is reinterpreted here as a category of quantum 
excitations capable of carrying energy, momentum, and conserved quantum numbers 
across macroscopic physical boundaries, rather than as an ontological opposite of 
matter. This reinterpretation does not claim that every quantum field excitation is 
radiation in any prior sense of the word; it claims that the word "radiation" should no 
longer be defined as the ontological negation of matter, and should instead be 
understood as covering the class of field excitations that can propagate, transfer, or be 
exchanged across space. The reinterpretation targets the matter/radiation boundary, not 
the Standard Model taxonomy of fields. 
Under this reinterpretation, radiation admits multiple modes, of which the following are 
empirically known and are directly relevant to the argument of this paper. The free -
propagating mode is exemplified by the photon: a quantum excitation of the 
electromagnetic field that propagates through space without restriction, encompassing 
the full spectrum from radio waves through microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, 
X-ray, and gamma-ray frequencies. All of these are photons of different energies, not

different ontological types. The localised mode is exemplified by the electron: a 
quantum excitation of the electron field that can exist in localised wave -packet states 
and is characterised by associated quantum numbers.  The reinterpretation does not 
exclude other forms of quantum field excitation within this broad category; the neutrino, 
for instance, is a propagating fermionic excitation with near -zero invariant mass, and 
its classificatory status under the present framework is a question left for further 
analysis. Observable differences between these modes—in invariant mass, spin, electric 
charge—are properties of the modes; they are not ontological boundaries that place the 
modes in opposed fundamental categories. 
The reinterpretation resolves the terminological inconsistency documented in Section 
3. Under the new reading, beta radiation and gamma radiation are both radiation in a 
coherent and unified sense: one is a flux of localised field excitations (electrons), the 
other a flux of free -propagating field excitations (photons). They are different modes 
of field excitation, not different kinds of radiation that happen to share a name by 
historical accident. Alpha radiation—a helium-4 nucleus, a composite bound state—is 
radiation in the operational sense of a penetrating emission, but is not a fundamental 
field excitation in the same sense; its status is that of a composite structure, which is 
addressed in Section 7. The inconsistency arose because "radiation" was exte nded to 
composite structures and fundamental excitations alike, without distinguishing the two 
levels. 
5.2 Scope and Limits of the Reinterpretation 
The reinterpretation requires careful demarcation. It does not assert that all quantum 
field excitations are radiation. Quarks, for instance, do not propagate freely; they are 
permanently confined within hadrons by the strong interaction, and it would be 
misleading to describe a confined quark as "radiation" in any standard sense. The Higgs 
field is responsible for electroweak symmetry breaking and fermion mass generation; 
the Higgs boson is its quantum excitation and does not propagate in the same sense as  
the photon or the free electron.  The massive gauge bosons W and Z are short -range 
force carriers. The reinterpretation focuses specifically on excitations that are 
transferable or propagating in the relevant physical sense —those that can travel 
macroscopic distances and carry energy, momentum, and quantum numbers across 
space. This scope is broad enough to include electrons and photons, which are the

entities at issue, without committing to a universal equation of "field excitation" with 
"radiation." 
The reinterpretation also does not claim that the Standard Model's fermion/boson 
distinction is incorrect or should be replaced. The distinction between fermionic and 
bosonic excitations is real, important, and experimentally grounded: it governs 
statistical behaviour, the Pauli exclusion principle, and the population of quantum states. 
What the reinterpretation denies is that the fermion/boson distinction coincides with the 
matter/radiation distinction in a way that makes the latter a fundamental ontologic al 
category. The electron is a fermion; that is a correct and useful classification. The claim 
that being a fermion places the electron on the matter side of a matter/radiation 
ontological boundary is what is challenged here. 
5.3 Compatibility with the Higgs Mechanism 
The Higgs mechanism provides the Standard Model's account of why the electron has 
a non-zero invariant mass while the photon does not. The electromagnetic U(1) gauge 
symmetry requires the photon to be massless; a mass term would break local gauge 
invariance [Weinberg, 1995; Higgs, 1964; Englert and Brout, 1964]. The electron 
acquires its mass through the Yukawa coupling to the Higgs field: the coupling term

vacuum expectation v alue through spontaneous symmetry breaking. This mechanism

The Higgs mechanism is compatible with the proposed reinterpretation. It specifies how 
the electron excitation mode acquires its characteristic mass parameter; it does not 
specify, in an ontological sense, whether the resulting mass is an irreducible intrinsic 
property of the electron field or an effective parameter of a particular excitation 
configuration. The Higgs mechanism provides the Standard Model explanation of the 
electron mass parameter; whether additional ontological interpretation is required is  a 
separate foundational question. The reinterpretation proposed here does not conflict 
with the Higgs mechanism; it situates that mechanism within a broader classificatory 
account and leaves that foundational question open. That question is addressed in t he 
following section. 
6. The Electron: Constituent Excitation and Its Classification

===== PAGE 14 =====

The reinterpretation of Section 5 places the electron and the photon within the same 
broad classificatory territory—both are transferable or propagating field excitations —
while distinguishing them by mode and associated quantum numbers. This section 
fulfils the obligation that placement creates: it gives the electron a positive definition 
within the new framework. The central argument of this section concerns a principle 
that is prior to any discussion of the electron's quantum numbers or mass: the 
classification of a constituent cannot be inferred from the classification of the composite 
system it participates in forming. This principle, when applied to the electron, dissolves 
the most persistent source of confusion about its ontological status. 
6.1 Constituent versus Composite: The Primary Distinction 
The electron participates in forming atoms. In the hydrogen atom, the electron occupies 
the bound states of the Coulomb potential defined by the proton; in heavier atoms, 
electrons fill the shells of the nuclear potential, and their configuration determine s the 
atom's chemical identity. From atoms, electrons participate in forming molecules 
through covalent, ionic, and metallic bonding. From molecules, all the composite 
structures of condensed matter physics, chemistry, biology, and engineering are built. 
The electron is, in this sense, a constituent of what we ordinarily call material reality. 
The question is whether this constitutive role determines the electron's own 
classification. The answer is that it does not, and cannot. The classification of a 
component is not determined by the classification of the composite structure that the 
component helps to form. This is a general principle of classificatory logic, not a claim 
specific to physics: the category to which a part belongs is established at the level of 
parts, not at the level of wholes. A word is not itself a sentence because sentences a re 
composed of words; a cell is not itself an organism because organisms are composed 
of cells. Applying the classification of the composite to its constituents is a category 
error—the confusion of two levels of description that must be kept distinct. 
Applied to the electron: the fact that electrons participate in forming atoms, and that 
atoms constitute what we call matter, does not make the electron itself a material 
structure in the relevant sense. The electron is a constituent excitation from which 
material structures are formed; it is not itself a material structure. The two levels are 
distinct. The ontological classification of a constituent is not determined by the 
composite structure it participates in forming. This is the distinction between the

fundamental excitation level and the composite structure level. At the level of 
fundamental excitation, the electron is a localised quantum excitation of the electron 
field. At the level of composite structure, atoms and molecules formed from such 
excitations constitute matter. Conflating these two levels is precisely what the classical 
matter/radiation framework has done, by classifying the electron as a matter particle on 
the basis of its role in composite matter, and then treating that classification as if it 
established the electron's fundamental ontological kind. 
This distinction resolves the apparent paradox that motivates much of the difficulty. It 
seems obvious that electrons are material: they are what atoms are made of, and atoms 
are material. But the appearance of paradox dissolves when the levels are separat ed. 
Atoms are material because they are stable composite structures with mass, volume, 
and chemical identity. The electron is a constituent of those structures. Its being a 
constituent does not transfer the classification of the composite to the constituen t; it 
means that the composite is built from something that is, at a more fundamental level, 
a field excitation. The electron is not less real or less physical for being classified as a 
field excitation rather than as matter; it is classified at the level at which the 
classification is appropriate. 
6.2 Positive Definition of the Electron 
Within the framework proposed in this paper, the electron is a localised quantum 
excitation of the electron field. This is its positive definition, stated at the level of 
fundamental excitation rather than at the level of composite structure. 
"Localised" specifies the excitation mode: the excitation energy is confined to a region 
of space, in contrast to the free-propagating mode of the photon, in which the excitation 
propagates indefinitely through space. "Quantum excitation of the electron fi eld" 
situates the electron in the framework of quantum field theory: it is not a classical point 
particle, nor a classical wave, but a discrete excitation of a fundamental field. 
The observable characteristics of the electron excitation mode are: invariant mass m_e 
= 0.511 MeV/c²; spin quantum number s = 1/2, making it a fermion subject to the Pauli 
exclusion principle; and electric charge q = −e, where e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. The 
positron—the electron's antiparticle —is the antiparticle excitation of the same field: 
identical mass and spin, charge +e. For comparison: the photon is a free -propagating

quantum excitation of the electromagnetic field, with zero invariant mass, spin -1, and 
zero electric charge. 
These definitions are definitions of excitation modes within the same broad category. 
The electron and photon differ in the field to which they belong, in the mode of 
excitation (localised versus free-propagating), and in their associated quantum numbers. 
None of these differences constitutes an ontological boundary of the kind the classical 
matter/radiation distinction claimed to mark. They are differences among modes within 
a shared fundamental description. 
6.3 The Invariant Mass: An Open Foundational Question 
The most salient observable difference between the electron and the photon is the 
electron's non -zero invariant mass. The photon's zero mass is required by the U(1) 
gauge symmetry of electrodynamics: a mass term for the photon would violate local 
gauge invariance [Weinberg, 1995]. The electron's non -zero mass is generated by the 
Higgs mechanism through the Yukawa coupling, as described in Section 5.3. The 
Standard Model specifies the acquisition mechanism and fixes the numerical value of 
m_e. 
What the Higgs mechanism does not settle is the ontological status of the mass it 
generates: whether the electron's invariant mass should be understood as an irreducible 
intrinsic property of the electron field, or as an effective parameter that characteri ses 
the localised excitation mode and could in principle be understood as arising from a 
more fundamental configuration. This is an open foundational question, distinct from 
the question of the mass's numerical value or its acquisition mechanism. The Higgs  
mechanism answers "how does the electron acquire this mass value" but not "what does 
it mean for a localised excitation mode to have mass rather than a propagating one." 
If the electron and the photon are both field excitations in different modes, then the 
question of why one mode has non -zero invariant mass and the other does not is a 
question about the relationship between excitation mode and mass —a foundational 
question that the Standard Model describes quantitatively rather than resolves at the 
interpretive level. Whether the answer lies in a deeper field -theoretic structure, in the 
topology of excitation configurations, or in some other mechanism is not addressed 
here. The contribution of the present classification is to identify this as a well -formed

open question and, further, to motivate a specific hypothesis about the relationship 
between excitation mode and invariant mass. 
The classification proposed in this paper makes this question more precisely formulable 
and motivates a specific hypothesis. The electron's localised excitation mode —
characterised by non-zero invariant mass—may represent the effective behaviour of a 
more fundamental massless field structure in a bound configuration, rather than an 
irreducible intrinsic property of the electron field. This is proposed as a hypothesis, not 
as a conclusion of the classificatory argument above. The classificatory argument 
stands independently of whether this hypothesis is confirmed or refuted. 
Three falsification conditions are stated： 
First, if experiment confirms that the electron possesses an irreducible internal 
structural scale and a mass component not accounted for by Higgs coupling —a 
component that cannot be attributed to any bound configuration of a more fundamental 
structure—the hypothesis requires revision.  
Second, if future theoretical work demonstrates that no bound configuration of any 
massless field can produce half -integer spin and quantised electric charge 
simultaneously, the hypothesis is excluded.  
Third, the hypothesis requires that the electron's invariant mass be strictly constant 
across all bound and free environments; any experimentally confirmed environment -
dependence of the electron's invariant mass would falsify the specific form of the 
hypothesis as stated here. 
This hypothesis is not a precondition of the classificatory proposal of this paper. It is a 
direction that the classification makes clearly formulable: if the electron and the photon 
are both field excitations in distinct modes, the question of whether the localised mode 
with invariant mass and the free -propagating massless mode share a deeper field -
theoretic origin becomes a well-posed research question rather than a category error. 
7. What Then Is "Matter"? 
If the electron is classified as a constituent field excitation rather than as a matter 
particle in the classical sense, the concept of matter requires relocation. The relocation

===== PAGE 18 =====
18 
 
proposed here is clarifying rather than eliminative: matter is assigned to the structural 
level, where it has always done its genuine explanatory work, and is removed from the 
level of fundamental excitations, where it has generated the inconsistency this paper 
has documented. 
Consider what the concept of matter actually explains or delineates in scientific practice. 
When physicists, chemists, engineers, and biologists speak of matter, they are 
concerned with substances that have mass, occupy volume, possess chemical identity, 
and interact with other substances through forces that can be described by the laws of 
chemistry and condensed matter physics. Solid matter has a crystal structure; liquid 
matter has surface tension; gaseous matter obeys an equation of state; plasma is a ga s 
of ionised constituents. These descriptions apply to systems composed of large numbers 
of atoms and molecules, interacting through electromagnetic forces at the atomic scale. 
The four states of matter —solid, liquid, gas, plasma —are descriptions of collective 
behaviour, statistical properties of many-particle systems, not intrinsic properties of the 
fundamental constituents. 
The atom is the basic unit of this level of description. An atom is a bound state of a 
nucleus (itself a composite of quarks and gluons) and electrons bound to the nucleus by 
the electromagnetic interaction. From atoms, molecules are formed by chemical 
bonding. From molecules, all macroscopic structure is built. At every level of this 
hierarchy, the description is of composite structure—of how constituents are organised 
and interact—not of what the constituents fundamentally are. 
Within the classificatory proposal of this paper, matter is treated primarily as a 
structural concept: it refers to composite, stable configurations of field excitations, 
particularly the configurations of electrons and quark -composite nuclei that together  
form atoms and the structures built from atoms.  This definition preserves everything 
that the concept of matter needs to do in chemistry, condensed matter physics, biology, 
and engineering. It also eliminates the inconsistency: the electron, as a fundamen tal 
excitation, is not itself a material structure in the structural sense defined here; it is a 
constituent excitation from which material structures are formed. 
This reassignment has a clarifying consequence for the four -state taxonomy. It has 
always been slightly anomalous to ask what "state of matter" a single electron occupies: 
the question applies the wrong tool at the wrong level. Solid, liquid, gas, and plas ma

===== PAGE 19 =====
19 
 
are descriptions of many-body collective behaviour; they apply to atoms and to systems 
of atoms, not to fundamental excitations. On the present account, this is transparent 
rather than puzzling: the four states apply at the structural level, where matter is defined, 
and do not reach to the excitation level, where the electron resides. A single electron 
occupies no state of matter for the same reason that a single constituent of a structure 
cannot itself instantiate the structure's macroscopic properties—the description does not 
apply at that level. 
The concept of matter is preserved rather than abolished. What has changed is that 
matter is no longer treated as a fundamental ontological category co -extensive with 
fermions, or with "that which has mass," or with "that which is opposed to radiation." 
It is a macroscopic, structural, emergent category —the appropriate description of 
organised composite structures built from fundamental excitations. The classical 
intuition that matter is the substantial stuff of the world is preserved at the level where 
that intuition is correct: the level of atoms, molecules, and the structures composed of 
them. What is revised is the extension of that category downward to fundamental 
excitations, where it was never correctly applicable and where it produced the 
inconsistency documented in this paper. 
8. Conclusion 
This paper has pursued three arguments whose convergence supports a unified proposal. 
The first argument is historical and terminological. The term "radiation" entered 
physics in two distinct traditions —the electromagnetic tradition, in which radiation is 
immaterial wave propagation, and the nuclear/radiological tradition, in which radiation 
designates any penetrating emission regardless of material character. When 
radioactivity was characterised, the word "radiation" was extended without conceptual 
revision to cover helium nuclei, electrons, and photons alike. The Standard Model 
subsequently provided a precise taxonomy distinguishing these three —composite 
nuclear state, fundamental fermion, massless gauge boson—but did not formally retire 
or correct the radiation vocabulary. The result has been more than a century  during 
which the same scientific community classifies the electron as a matter particle in one 
context and as a component of radiation in another, without acknowledging the 
inconsistency.

===== PAGE 20 =====
20 
 
The second argument is dynamical. Electron–positron annihilation and pair production 
demonstrate that the electron and the photon are not categorically incommensurable: 
under specifiable conditions, electron –positron states and photon states can undergo 
conditional reversible conversion. The conditionality of pair production—requiring an 
external field or a second photon —has been carefully noted. What the conditionality 
does not alter is the classificatory significance of the interconversion: the pattern of  
conditional reversible conversion between apparently distinct entities has, in the history 
of physics, repeatedly motivated and preceded classificatory unification. 
The third argument is constructive. Radiation is reinterpreted as a category of quantum 
excitations capable of carrying energy, momentum, and conserved quantum numbers 
across macroscopic physical boundaries, rather than as an ontological opposite of 
matter. Within this reinterpretation, the electron is given a positive definition as a 
localised quantum excitation of the electron field—not itself a material structure, but a 
constituent excitation from which material structures are formed. The distinction 
between constituent and composite is the philosophical core of the proposal: t he 
classification of a fundamental constituent need not be inherited from the classification 
of the composite structures that it forms.  The concept of matter is accordingly 
reassigned to the structural level —composite stable configurations of field 
excitations—where the four-state taxonomy of solid, liquid, gas, and plasma finds its 
proper domain of application. The electron's non-zero invariant mass, and the question 
of why the loca lised excitation mode has mass while the free -propagating mode does 
not, is identified as an open foundational question that the present classification makes 
more precisely formulable. 
The contribution is classificatory: it fills a gap that has persisted in physical ontology 
for more than a century , clarifies the relationship between two frameworks that have 
been coexisting without formal reconciliation, and states more exactly the question that 
follows. If the electron and the photon are both field excitations in distinct modes, the 
question of whether those modes arise from a single underlying field structure becomes 
clearly formulable. The classical matter/radiation framework, with its  internal 
inconsistency and its ontological commitments inherited from the nineteenth century, 
has not permitted this question to be stated with the precision it deserves. The 
framework proposed here does.
