1. Introduction
Fractal characteristics are ubiquitous in nature, ranging from the branching patterns 
of vegetation to the large-scale structure of the universe[1,2]. In the geological domain, 
subterranean rock formations and fracture networks exhibit pronounced self-similarity 
over a wide range of scales[3,4]. Both naturally occurring fractures and those induced 
by hydraulic fracturing possess these fractal features, which are critical in governing 
the flow and transport properties within porous media[5]. Understanding the transport 
mechanisms in such multiscale systems is fundamental to applications in enhanced oil 
recovery, groundwater remediation, and carbon sequestration.
Among the key parameters characterizing transport, tortuosity ( τ) plays a pivotal 
role. It serves as a bridge connecting the microscopic pore structure to macroscopic 
transport properties, such as permeability and electrical conductivity[6,7]. In classical 
theories, such as the Kozeny-Carman equation, tortuosity is defined as the ratio of the 
actual fluid path length to the straight-line distance across the medium[8,9]. Accurately 
quantifying tortuosity is essential for predicting flow resistance and diffusion rates[10]. 
Traditionally, tortuosity has been analyzed using simplified geometric models (e.g., 
capillary bundles) or numerical simulations based on Euclidean geometries[11–13]. 
While these approaches have established robust baselines for homogeneous media, they 
often struggle to capture the extreme heterogeneity and connectivity constraints 
inherent in disordered geological materials[14,15]
In recent years, fractal theory has provided a powerful tool to describe these 
complex, non-Euclidean structures. Numerous studies have derived analytical models 
linking fractal dimensions ( 𝐷𝑓) to transport parameters[16,17]. For example, models 
based on deterministic fractals, such as the Sierpiński carpet, have highlighted the 
effects of fractal dimensions and porosity on tortuosity, providing deeper insights into 
flow paths[18]. Furthermore, researchers have utilized fractal geometry to refine the 
analysis of seepage and imbibition, demonstrating that flow resistance is significantly 
influenced by the scaling laws of pore size distributions[19].
Despite these advancements, a fundamental gap remains: most existing studies 
operate within a "Euclidean framework with fractal corrections." Traditional models 
typically treat fractal dimensions merely as fixed geometric parameters entered into 
classical conservation equations (e.g., standard Fickian diffusion or Darcy’s law). They 
implicitly assume that the metric of the space remains Euclidean, where distance scales 
linearly[20]. However, transport in intrinsic fractal spaces is fundamentally different. 
As established in the physics of disordered media, diffusion on fractals is often 
anomalous (sub-diffusive), governed by the spectral dimension rather than the 
Euclidean dimension[21]. In such spaces, the concept of a constant tortuosity becomes 
questionable. The fluid path is not merely longer; it is structurally constrained by 
topological features—such as dead ends, bottlenecks, and back-turns—that exist at all 
scales[22]. Consequently, classical models fail to capture the scale-dependent nature of 
transport pathways in fully fractal environments.

2. The random porous media in fractal space
The random porous medium discussed in this paper is represented by an 𝑛 × 𝑛 
matrix, where each element consists of either 0 or 1, with 1 indicating solid material 
and 0 representing the pore spaces through which fluid can flow. In constructing a 
random porous medium with a given porosity using the Monte Carlo method, a random 
number is first generated. For each matrix element, a uniform random number is 
generated and compared against the specified porosity. If the random number exceeds 
the specified porosity, the corresponding element is assigned a value of 1; otherwise, it 
is assigned a value of 0. Iterating this procedure over a sufficiently large 𝑛 × 𝑛 domain 
yields a baseline random porous medium with the desired macroscopic porosity.
The Sierpiński carpet is an ideal fractal structure in two dimensions. The standard 
construction process of the Sierpiński carpet involves dividing a square into 9 equal 
smaller squares, removing the central square, and retaining the remaining 8 squares. 
This process is then recursively applied to the 8 remaining smaller squares, continuing 
infinitely to form the Sierpiński carpet. To generate a random porous medium in a 
fractal space, this paper utilizes a finite number of iterations to create the Sierpiński 
carpet as the fractal space. In this fractal space, the random porous medium with a given 
porosity can be obtained using the previously described method.
To better study the tortuosity of random porous media in fractal spaces, we replace 
the standard Sierpiński carpet with a random Sierpiński carpet. There are three main 
modifications: First, the square is not uniformly divided into 9 equal smaller squares, 
but also into squares of sizes 4 × 4, 5 × 5, and 6 × 6, among others. Second, the 
number of squares removed in each iteration is fixed but not necessarily equal to 1. 
Third, the positions of the removed squares are randomized during each iteration. 
Through this approach, we can obtain fractal spaces with different fractal dimensions, 
and the calculation formula for the fractal dimension Df is as follows[24]:
Df = log(m2 - k)
log m                             （1）
where m represents the number of subdivisions along each side of the square 
during each iteration, and k denotes the number of squares removed at each step.

resulting random Sierpiński carpet generated through iteration is 1.892. The Fig.1 (b) 
denotes the random porous media in fractal space with porosity 𝜑 = 0.5 and fractal

3. Numerical algorithms for tortuosity
According to the definition of tortuosity, we can calculate the flow field using fluid 
dynamics methods and obtain the streamlines by analyzing the pathlines in steady-state 
flow, from which the streamline length can be determined and tortuosity calculated. 
However, this approach is computationally intensive and presents modeling challenges, 
especially for random porous media. The presence of a large number of small, randomly 
distributed solid particles in the medium adds complexity to the calculations. To address 
the need for efficient tortuosity calculations in large-scale fractal-space random porous 
media, this paper employs an efficient finite random walk method[23].
In this method, particles are released from the pore at the edge of the random porous 
medium flow domain (typically chosen as the left edge), where each pore is represented 
as "0." The particles attempt to move toward the opposite edge. If the direction of 
movement is not "0," the particle randomly chooses a direction perpendicular to its 
current path and continues to attempt to move toward the opposite edge. If the particle 
encounters a dead-end or flow boundary, it backtracks to the most recent random choice 
and selects another direction. This process continues until the particle either reaches the 
opposite edge or returns to the starting point. The path of particles successfully reaching 
the opposite edge is recorded as a streamline. By analyzing the lengths of all streamlines, 
tortuosity can be calculated.
The tortuosity values for random porous media with different porosities in 
Euclidean space, calculated using this method, show that in the two-dimensional case, 
due to the percolation threshold, the theoretical and computed values align closely only 
when the porosity exceeds 0.7. In the three-dimensional case, the better connectivity 
leads to an even closer match between the theoretical and computed values. This 
validates the effectiveness of the finite random walk method. Therefore, this method is 
employed in this paper to calculate the tortuosity of random porous media in fractal 
spaces.

5. Conclusion
Fractal phenomena are ubiquitous in nature, and uncovering the transport laws that 
emerge in fractal geometries remains an important and challenging topic. In this work, 
we investigated the tortuosity of random porous media embedded in fractal spaces 
constructed from an improved random Sierpiński carpet. Tortuosity was evaluated 
using a finite random-walk framework, and its dependence on porosity 𝜑 and fractal 
dimension Df was quantified and benchmarked against the Euclidean two-
dimensional case.
The results confirm that tortuosity generally decreases with increasing porosity in 
both Euclidean and fractal spaces. More importantly, the influence of fractal dimension 
is not purely additive: the effect of Df depends on 𝜑, indicating a nontrivial coupling 
between pore availability and fractal geometry. Compared with Euclidean media at the 
same porosity, random porous media in fractal spaces exhibit systematically larger 
tortuosity, demonstrating an intrinsic enhancement of pathway complexity induced by 
the fractal embedding. This discrepancy can be attributed to two complementary 
mechanisms. First, the random Sierpiński carpet reduces the effective space-filling 
capacity and thus lowers the equivalent porosity available for transport. Second, even 
after accounting for porosity effects, the fractal geometry introduces additional 
connectivity constraints (e.g., bottlenecks, dead ends, and heterogeneous channeling) 
that lead to transport scaling distinct from that of Euclidean space.
