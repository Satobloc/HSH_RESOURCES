1 Abstract 21 
Scientific discovery is driven by the iterative process of observation, hypothesis generation, 22 
experimentation, and data analysis. Despite recent advancements in applying artificial intelligence 23 
to biology, no system has yet automated all these stages [1, 2, 3]. Here, we introduce Robin, the first 24 
multi-agent system capable of fully automating both hypothesis generation and data analysis for 25 
experimental biology. By integrating literature search agents with data analysis agents, Robin can 26 
generate hypothese s, propose experiments, interpret experimental results, and generate updated 27 
hypotheses, achieving a semi-autonomous approach to scientific discovery. By applying this system, 28 
we were able to identify promising therapeutic candidates for dry age-related macular degeneration 29 
(dAMD), the major cause of blindness in the developed world [4, 5]. Robin proposed enhancing 30 
retinal pigment epithelium phagocytosis as a therapeutic strategy, and identified and confirmed in 31 
vitro efficacy for ripasudil and KL001. Ripasudil is a clinically-used Rho kinase (ROCK) inhibitor 32 
that has never previously been proposed for treating dAMD. To elucidate the mechanism of 33 
ripasudil-induced upregulation of phagocytosis, Robin then proposed and analyzed a follow -up 34 
RNA-seq experiment, which revealed upregulation of ABCA1, a lipid efflux pump and possible novel 35 
target. All hypotheses, experimental directions, data analyses, and data figures in the main text of 36 
this report were produced by Robin. As the first AI system to autonomously dis cover and validate 37 
novel therapeutic candidates within an iterative lab-in-the-loop framework, Robin establishes a new 38 
paradigm for AI-driven scientific discovery. 39 
2 Main 40 
Advances in our ability to measure, perturb, and model biological systems have resulted in rapid growth of our 41 
collective scientific knowledge [6]. Yet, complementary technologies to interpret, synthesize, and generate hypotheses 42 
from this knowledge have l agged behind [7]. Artificial intelligence (AI) systems based on large language models 43 
(LLMs) show promise for automating this knowledge synthesis process and accelerating scientific discovery. As a 44 
primary goal of biomedical research is the development of new treatments for disease, our ability to produce new 45 
therapeutics may be the ultimate beneficiary of these approaches. Drug development heavily relies on a confluence of 46 
biological, clinical, and pharmaceutical expertise, and is limited by the rate at wh ich these experts can synthesize the 47 
scientific literature [8]. 48 
 49 
The repurposing of existing drugs for new indications presents a promising application space for LLM systems. The 50 
history of drug repurposing often shows a pattern: while insights often exist ed in scientific literature, only after a 51 
significant lag did that knowledge crystallize into a new treatment. For example, dabrafenib, an inhibitor of BRAF 52 
kinase that is used in various cancers with mitogenic mutations in BRAF, is being repurposed to pre vent hearing loss 53 
[9, 10]. While its molecular action was well characterized by 2010 [11, 12, 13], dabrafenib’s otoprotective effects were 54 
only discovered 10 years later via unbiased high -throughput screening. This delayed discovery occurred despite 55 
dabrafenib’s otoprotective effects being a direct result of its known inhibition of BRAF [9, 14, 10], suggesting that 56 
more repurposing opportunities could be identified through logical connection of existing biological insights in the 57 
literature. Further cases a cross medicine from ketamine (22 -year lag [15, 16]) to leucovorin ( 5-year lag [17, 18]) to 58 
KarXT (13-year lag [19, 20]) – underscore how repurposing efforts are frequently realized years after core insights are 59 
documented. Such delays in connecting existing insights to new therapeutic applications highlight the challenge of 60 
synthesizing disparate scientific knowledge. Trained on data across many fields, large language models (LLMs) can 61 
store and recall information on a wide variety of scientific topics and thus transcend the limitations of individual human 62 
knowledge. Previous work has shown that fine -tuned LLMs and specialized RAG systems can exceed human 63 
performance on retrieving and summarizing information from the scientific literature. These advances raise the 64 
possibility that LLM systems could be used for novel hypothesis generation [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. 65 
 66 
Several LLM systems have recently been developed to automate hypothesis generation [26, 1, 27, 28, 2, 3, 29]. 67 
Specialized systems have also been developed to automate specific tasks in drug discovery, such as prediction of 68 
pharmacological properties and safety profiles [3, 27]. These systems have demonstrated they can generate reasonable 69 
hypotheses by utilizing multi -agent architectures that decompose scientific reasoning into discrete manageable sub -70 
tasks [3], domain-specific fine-tuning [2, 30], integration of external tools [2, 3], and incorporation of human feedback 71 
[3]. However, none of these systems have automated all of the key intellectual steps of the scientific process, including 72 
generating hypotheses and experimental strategies, analyzing resu lts from laboratory experiments, and refining 73 
hypotheses in light of new data. 74

Here, we introduce Robin, the first multi -agent system for discovery in biology that integrates novel hypothesis 75 
generation with experimental data analysis in one continuous wor kflow (Figure 1A ,B). Robin utilizes specialized 76 
language agents for literature search (Crow and Falcon) and data analysis (Finch) to enable semi-autonomous scientific 77 
discovery (Figure 1C) [25, 31]. Though this system could be applicable to scientific disc overy across disciplines, in 78 
this report, we focus on its potential in therapeutics. After giving Robin a disease of interest, Robin automatically 79 
identified relevant in vitro assays that model key disease mechanisms and proposed specific drug candidates to evaluate 80 
in these experimental models. We then conducted the experiments and provided the resulting data to Robin for 81 
autonomous analysis. Robin then interpreted the results of this analysis to generate a new round of therapeutic 82 
candidates. Through this  process, Robin drove an iterative therapeutics development cycle where hypotheses were 83 
generated, tested, analyzed, and refined based on experimental results. The key intellectual steps of the scientific 84 
method were thus automated while coordinating with scientists throughout the experimental loop. Robin represents 85 
the first system to connect literature -based hypothesis generation with autonomous analysis of biological laboratory 86 
data in a continuous feedback system. 87 
To demonstrate Robin’s ability to generate and refine novel therapeutic hypotheses, we attempted to identify potential 88 
new treatments for dry age-related macular degeneration (dAMD). dAMD is the leading cause of irreversible sight loss 89 
in developed countries, yet limited treatment options are a vailable. In the U.S. alone, 1.5 million people have vision -90 
threatening dAMD, and 600,000 are legally blind due to AMD, a figure projected to almost triple by 2050 due to an 91 
aging population [4, 5]. By applying Robin to discover novel therapeutic candidates for dAMD, this work represents a 92 
first step towards AI-generated discovery in scientific research. 93 
2.1 Robin: A multi-agent system for scientific discovery 94 
Robin integrates multiple language agents in a structured workflow to generate therapeutic candidates for a given 95 
disease (Figure 1A,B). Crow and Falcon are literature search agents based on PaperQA2 that conduct concise and deep 96 
literature summaries, respectively [25]. PaperQA2 achieves expert -level performance in information retrieval and 97 
summarization, with access to scientific literature, clinical trial reports, and the Open Targets Platform [32]. Finch is a 98 
scientific data analysis agent that performs analyses of experimental data from assays, such as RNA -seq and flow 99 
cytometry (Figure 1C) [31]. By coo rdinating these agents to identify novel and readily lab -testable therapeutic 100 
strategies, we used Robin to enable an experimentally-guided system that drives the process of scientific discovery. An 101 
example of the Robin hypothesis generation workflow is shown in Supplementary Figure 1. In the workflow described 102 
in this paper, Robin analyses 551 papers in 30 minutes compared to an estimated time of 540 hours for a human. Using 103 
estimates derived from published surveys, we estimate a 200 -fold reduction in time spent completing the entire 104 
scientific workflow manually (Table 1). 105 
2.1.1 Therapeutic hypothesis generation 106 
When provided with the disease of interest ( “dry age-related macular degeneration ”), Robin formulated a series of 107 
general questions about the disease pathology and queries Crow to answer each question. Using the reports from Crow 108 
as context, Robin then identified 10 potential causal disease mechanisms. For each mechanism, Robin deployed Crow 109 
to prepare a detailed report describing an in vitro model of the d isease mechanism which could be used to test drug 110 
efficacy. Robin then used an LLM judge to make pairwise comparisons between reports, which are used to calculate 111 
their relative rankings (see Methods). The top -ranked in vitro model is used by Robin to defi ne the experimental 112 
strategy for therapeutic candidate hypothesis generation. 113 
Once an in vitro model was selected, Robin conducted a similar sequence of general literature review and hypothesis 114 
generation steps to propose 30 therapeutic candidates for experimental testing. Robin then queried Falcon to generate 115 
a detailed report to evaluate each candidate. These reports contained both justification for why each drug is suitable for 116 
mitigating the disease mechanism represented in the in vitro model and potential limitations the drug may pose. The 117 
drug candidates are ranked by an LLM -judged tournament a ccording to the strength of the scientific rationale, 118 
pharmacological profile, and methodology of the supporting literature. This ranked list was then reviewed by human 119 
scientists, and top drug candidates were tested in the lab by executing a human-generated experimental protocol based 120 
on the assay suggested by Robin. 121 
2.1.2 Experimental data analysis 122 
Once the experiments were complete, we uploaded raw or semi -processed data (in this case, .FCS files from flow 123 
cytometry or gene counts for RNA-seq) and prompted Robin with a desired analysis approach, e.g., “flow cytometry” 124 
or “differential expression analysis”. Robin then deployed Finch to carry out the desired analysis. This analysis step 125 
presents unique challenges due to the inherently ambiguous nature of biologic al data interpretation. For example, the 126

gating choices in flow cytometry analysis or the filters used in RNA-seq analyses will vary between human scientists 127 
and may impact the final conclusions. Similarly, Finch ’s analysis results can vary between runs, e ven when given 128 
identical prompts and data, due to the stochasticity of the language agent. To leverage this diversity, we configured 129 
Robin to launch 8 Finch analysis trajectories, each of which independently analyzed the experimental data. In each 130 
trajectory, Finch executed analysis code in a Jupyter notebook and provided an interpretable and reproducible summary 131 
of its findings. After all trajectories were completed, a meta -analysis was conducted to synthesize all outputs into a 132 
consensus-driven conclusion . In this way, Finch explored diverse analytical trajectories while delivering highly 133 
consistent end results based on consensus (Methods) [33]. 134 
Finally, Robin distilled actionable scientific insights from these processed experimental results. Robin also ha s the 135 
ability to propose targeted follow -up assays to explore or confirm significant or unexpected findings. These 136 
experimental insights are used to inform the next cycle of therapeutic hypothesis generation. The cycle continues until 137 
a human has a satisfactory drug candidate. 138 
2.2 Robin expedites hypothesis generation 139 
We applied Robin to generate therapeutic candidates for dAMD as an initial proof -of-concept (Figure 2A , 140 
Supplementary Figures 1 -11). Robin began the therapeutic hypothesis generation workflow by identifying and 141 
reviewing 151 papers to propose ten biologically relevant dAMD mechanisms to assay. After ranking the disease 142 
mechanisms and corresponding experimental strategies, Robin propo sed treating dAMD by increasing RPE cell 143 
phagocytosis, and suggested testing how well drugs increase the phagocytic capacity of either patient-derived or stem 144 
cell-derived RPE cells in a flow cytometry assay. 145 
Robin then called Crow (Supplementary Figure 4) to conduct a literature review of about 400 papers relating to RPE 146 
phagocytosis and the therapeutic landscape of dry AMD and synthesized the results to propose 30 existing drug 147 
candidates for experimental testing in the phagocytosis assay. Robin called Fa lcon to produce comprehensive 148 
evaluation reports on each of these molecules (Supplementary Figure 9), which were ranked in an LLM -judged 149 
tournament. 150 
We calculated the cost of a Robin workflow under the same configuration that was run in the paper: num_quer ies=5, 151 
num_assays=10, and num_candidates=30. This configuration would lead to 45 Crow calls and 30 Falcon calls, which 152 
are on average $4.33 and $6.43, respectively, or $10.76 for a total run. 153 
Our time-on-task analysis (Table 1) demonstrates that the Robin framework reduces the total cognitive labor of a 154 
discovery cycle from an estimated 872–937 human hours to less than two hours. Notably, the system synthesized  155 
approximately 825 references in roughly 30 minutes, a task that would require over 800 hours of m anual processing 156 
based on established literature benchmarks for scholarly reading. 157 
2.2.1 ROCK inhibitor enhances phagocytosis 158 
We then selected the top five candidates from this ranking for experimental testing: Exendin-4, Fingolimod, MFGE8, 159 
Y-27632, and the combination of AICAR and TUDCA. MFGE8 is known to increase phagocytosis in cultured RPE 160 
cells [38], so we included it to ser ve as a positive control. Although Robin suggested using fluorescently labeled 161 
photoreceptor outer segments as a substrate for RPE phagocytosis, we initially decided to use pHrodo beads due to 162 
availability. Additionally, Robin suggested using primary or stem cell derived RPE, but we used ARPE-19 cells for the 163 
initial drug screen to expedite our evaluation of Robin ’s hypotheses. pHrodo beads are fluorescently activated in the 164 
low pH environment of the lysosome, allowing detection of phagocytosis in single cells using flow cytometry (Figure 165 
2B, See Supplementary Figure 12 for microscopy validation). After testing these therapeutic candidates in the RPE 166 
phagocytosis assay, the raw flow cytometry data, associated metadata, and an analysis prompt were uploaded to Robin. 167 
Robin called the data analysis agent Finch, which developed a Jupyter notebook to quantify the effect of each 168 
compound on RPE phagocytosis by gating the flow cytometry data and performing statistical tests. Plots from a 169 
representative individual Finch trajectory are shown in Figure 2C-E. These results were confirmed by a human analysis 170 
of the same data (Supplementary Figure 13 and Supplementary Table 3). Preclinical models have demonstrated that Y-171 
27632 can restore phagocytic efficiency in RPE cells [39], confirming Robin’s literature-based rationale for suggesting 172 
this candidate. 173

to investigate the transcriptional effects of ROCK inhibition (Figure 3A). We conducted a second RPE phagocytosis 176 
experiment with Y-27632 and profiled the samples using bulk RNA sequencing. Finch conducted differential gene 177

The DGE analysis identified a 3-fold upregulation (adjusted p=2.13x10-83) of ABCA1, a critical lipid efflux pump, in  190

These mechanistic insights, derived from experiments proposed by Robin and analyzed by Finch, demonstrate how AI-197 
driven scientific discovery can not o nly expedite the generation of in vitro data for candidate therapeutic compounds 198 
but also reveal novel molecular targets within disease pathways that might have otherwise remained unexplored. 199 
2.2.3 A repurposed drug for dAMD 200 
In addition to suggesting RNA-seq analysis on Y-27632, Robin also conducted a subsequent iteration of candidate drug 201 
hypotheses (Figure 4A). We tested 10 of these drugs experimentally and provided the data to Finch for analysis. Finch’s 202 
analysis showed that ripasudil, a ROCK inhibitor approved for treatment of glaucoma in Japan, outperformed Y-27632 203 
and increased RPE cell phagocytosis 1.89 -fold compared to DMSO controls (Figure 4B; human analysis showed  a 204 
1.75-fold increase - see Supplementary Figure 15 and Supplementary Table 4). A dose response experiment in ARPE-205 
19 cells confirmed the greater potency of ripasudil as compared to Y-27632 (Figure 4C). Additionally, as an approved 206 
drug, ripasudil has a known safety profile, providing an advantage over Y -27632 (a research compound) in clinical 207 
translation.  208

2.2.4 Validation in primary human RPE 210 
Retinal Pigment Epithelium Stem Cells (RPE-SC) are a subpopulation of the human RPE that retains limited mitotic 211 
potential allowing for cultivation in vitro. When expanded into a confluent monolayer and allowed to mature in vitro, 212 
RPE-SC are known to express canonical RPE genes, retain polarized barrier function and morphologically resemble 213 
native human RPE [45]. Additionally, ROCK inhibitors are not required in the culture of RPE-SC, unlike iPSC-derived 214 
RPE [46]. We obtained RPE-SC isolated from a >60-year old patient from the Eye Bank For Sight Restoration (New 215 
York) and expanded them into a confluent monolayer. We validated their RPE phenotype through 216 
immunocytochemistry of canonical RPE markers and phagocytosis machinery [47] (Extended Data Figures 1 and 2). 217 
We re-screened all drugs suggested by Robin, this time using RPE -SC in place of ARPE -19 cells and fluorescently 218 
labeled bovine rod outer segments (ROS) in place of beads. Once again, ripasudil and Y-27632 were identified as hits, 219 
with ripasudil again showi ng higher potency (Figure 4D; see also human analysis in Supplementary Figure 16 and 220 
Supplementary Table 5). There is a dose dependent increase in phagocytosis with both ROCK inhibitors, with ripasudil 221 
being the more potent (Figure  4E). Supernatants from t his dose response experiment were assayed for cytotoxicity. 222 
There was no relationship between Y-27632 dose and LDH release from RPE-SC cells. Surprisingly, ripasudil showed 223 
a negative relationship with LDH release, with higher drug concentrations reducing LDH release (Extended Data Figure 224 
3).  225 
Interestingly, KL001, a circadian clock modulator which works by preventing the ubiquitin-dependent degradation of 226 
CRY proteins, was also identified as a hit in RPE-SC. Robin suggested this drug based on the circadian control of RPE 227 
phagocytosis [47] and to our knowledge no one has previously proposed KL001 as an enhancer of phagocytosis. 228 
To further validate the ABCA1 finding, we performed RNA -seq on RPE -SC cells treated with ripasudil or vehicle 229 
control (with or without ROS). ABCA1 was also upregulated in RPE-SC cells in response to ripasudil exposure, with 230 
or without ROS (Supplementary Figure 17).  231

2.3 Validation of the Robin architecture 235 
We started by ablating the literature s earch agents Crow and Falcon, replacing them with calls to OpenAI ’s o4-mini. 236 
We then assessed the performance of Robin (wild type) versus its ablated version by asking both to generate 50 drug 237 
candidate proposals for dAMD and then assessing the quality of a sampled subset of these proposals. 238 
We manually assessed their quality by performing a human reference check. As expected, this showed that ablation of 239 
Falcon, or both Crow and Falcon, but not Crow alone led to a dramatic increase in hallucinated referenc es (Extended 240 
Data Figure 4A and Supplementary Tables 6-8, respectively). (Crow is responsible for producing the literature searches 241 
that guide Falcon in producing the final drug candidate report, and so any references hallucinated during the Crow 242 
literature search phase could be corrected by Falcon in producing its final report.) To confirm that Falcon was indeed 243 
masking the hallucinated LLM-generated references in the Crow-only ablation we generated 15 assay proposals from 244 
wild type and Crow-ablated Robin (Crow, not Falcon, produces final assay reports, see Supplementary Figure 1). We 245 
then performed a human reference check, and as expected Crow produced no hallucinated references while 44.5 ± 246 
6.37% (mean ± SEM, n=15 assay proposals)) of o4-mini’s references were hallucinated (Extended Data Figure 4B and 247 
Supplementary Table 9). 248 
 249 
To probe whether Crow and Falcon literature searches produced higher quality proposals over those produced by o4 -250 
mini, we compared the 50 wild type and 50 ablated proposals in a head -to-head tournament adjudicated by an LLM 251 
judge (Claude Sonnet 3.7; See Judge prompts in Supplementary Figure 11). Ablation of Crow, Falcon or Crow and 252 
Falcon always led to a reduction in the quality of the final drug proposals (Extended Data Figure 4C). 253 
Next, we assessed the perform ance of Finch on the flow cytometry and RNA -seq tasks performed in round 2 of the 254 
dAMD drug screen. Finch was given the task-specific prompts and data from round 2 and performance was measured 255 
by assessing Finch’s adherence to expert -generated rubrics (Supplementary Figures 18 and 19). Finch demonstrated 256 
consistent performance on RNA-seq (86±0%; (mean ± SEM, n=3 runs)) and flow cytometry (100±0%; (mean ± SEM, 257 
n=3 runs)) (Extended Data Figure 5A). 258 
To probe Finch ’s failure mode on a range of more difficult da ta analysis tasks without the task -specific multistep 259 
prompting given in the Robin workflow, we curated an expert -generated panel of 170 question -answer pairs from 260 
BixBench which span bioinformatics and statistics tasks relevant to drug discovery  (Extended Data Figure 5B  and 261 
Supplementary Table 10) [31]. Finch’s overall performance was 22.8±1.7% compared to 1.6±1.2% for Sonnet 3.7 262 
alone without the agent harness (mean ± SEM, n=3; Extended Data Figure 5C and 5D). Finch performed better on the 263 
statistics subset (47.9±1.5%) than the bioinformatics subset (15.3± 2.0%). Typically, the bioinformatics subset required 264 
executing multi-step pipelines which are sensitive to parameterization, while the biostatistics questions required only 265 
single-step computations on clean, tabular data. These results demonstrate a cle ar utility for agent harnesses such as 266 
Finch, which augment LLMs with the tools necessary to engage with data sources, but also point out room for 267 
substantial performance improvement on multi-step problems. 268 
To confirm that Robin’s identification of ROCK inhibitors in the phagocytosis assay could not be trivially recapitulated 269 
by a general purpose LLM-based agent, we gave the same candidate generation prompt (Supplementary Figure 8) to 270 
OpenAI’s Deep Rese arch, an agent designed to conduct multi -step research on complex tasks. Like Robin, Deep 271 
Research was asked to generate 19 novel drug candidates (equal to the total number of Robin drug candidates in round 272 
1 and round 2) which we screened in RPE -SC. Deep Research generated 17 unique candidates (duplicating two drug 273 
candidates). None of Deep Research ’s suggested drugs were hits in this assay and notably Deep Research did not 274 
suggest ROCK inhibition as a means of enhancing RPE phagocytosis (Extended Data Figure 6). 275 
3 Discussion 276 
In this report, we present Robin, a multi -agent system integrating automated hypothesis generation and experimental 277 
data analysis for scientific discovery. When tasked with identifying novel therapeutics for dAMD, Robin proposed 278 
phagocytosis enhancement as a therapeutic strategy for dAMD and identified Ripasudil and KL001 as enhancers of 279 
RPE phagocytosis among tested compounds through an iterative lab -in-the-loop discovery cycle. Ripasudil ’s 280 
established safety profile and clinical approval for ocular use present a promising drug repurposing opportunity while 281 
KL001 represents, to our knowledge, a totally novel approach to phagocytosis enhancement in RPE cells. 282 
Notably, while ROCK inhibitors have been previously suggested for treatment of wet AMD and other retinal diseases 283 
of neovascularization, Robin is the first to propose their therapeutic application in dry AMD via a mechanism of 284

phagocytosis enhancement [48]. This approach is supported by several lines of evidence. RPE phagocytic dysfunction 285 
is observed not only with normal aging but is pronounced in AMD patients [49, 50, 51]. Additionally, the ability of 286 
ROCK inhibition to enhance phagocytosis in RPE cells is already known [39, 40, 41]. This hypothesis would of course 287 
require validation in a suitable disease model and ultimately in a randomized, placebo -controlled trial to confirm 288 
clinical validity. 289 
Taken together, these results demonstrate Robin ’s ability to effectively generate novel, testable hypotheses by 290 
synthesizing insights already present in the scientific literature. By focusing on “combinatorial synthesis” (identifying 291 
non-obvious connections between disparate fields), Robin effectively targets “low-hanging fruit” that human experts 292 
may overlook due to the compartmentalization of scientific knowledge. This paradigm of reliable, literature-grounded 293 
synthesis is applicable beyond drug repurposing and could be readily extended to fields such as materials science [52]. 294 
Beyond the specific application of dAMD, Robin addresses a broader challenge in therapeutic development. With FDA 295 
approvals stagnating at approximately 50 novel drugs annually over the past decade [53], new approaches to scale 296 
therapeutic discovery are urgently needed. As the first system to automate both literature -grounded hypothesis 297 
generation and experimental biology data analysis, Robin is poised to accelerate the pace of drug discovery compared 298 
to traditional approaches. 299 
In light of the potential for automated discovery systems to identify potent biological agents, we have implemented 300 
guardrails within the Robin framework. Firstly, the system prioritizes candidates with establis hed safety profiles and 301 
searches for known toxicities or off -target interactions. Secondly, as a “lab-in-the-loop” system, Robin’s outputs are 302 
treated as therapeutic hypotheses that must undergo standard pre -clinical validation, ensuring that any unintende d 303 
toxicity is identified through traditional in vitro and in vivo safety filters prior to clinical translation. Regarding dual -304 
use concerns, Robin utilizes off-the-shelf LLMs that have undergone extensive safety alignment (via red-teaming and 305 
RLHF) to prevent the generation of malicious biological protocols. Additionally, all queries to our platform are run 306 
through an LLM classifier that checks against unsafe topics. 307 
In its current implementation, Robin has several opportunities for continued development. F or example, while Robin 308 
generates experimental outlines, it does not yet produce precise, executable protocols—future iterations aim to provide 309 
detailed methodologies that require minimal human translation for laboratory execution. The Finch data analysis agent 310 
is also reliant on prompt engineering by domain experts to produce reliable analytical results. Adapting Finch to 311 
independently generate or adapt prompts to specific data modalities would enable a more autonomous discovery 312 
pipeline. Finally, while the results in this manuscript rely on frontier LLMs available in early 2025, Robin ’s model-313 
agnostic architecture ensures its capabilities will scale alongside advances in underlying foundation models. While the 314 
rapidly advancing baseline capabilities of general-purpose coding agents may achieve parity with specialized harnesses 315 
like Finch on standard computational tasks, domain -specific architectures remain crucial for strictly enforcing 316 
experimental constraints and reliably orchestrating specialized bioinformatics toolchains. 317 
By automating hypothesis generation, experimental planning, and data analysis in an integrated system, Robin 318 
represents a powerful new paradigm for AI-driven scientific discovery. This approach can be used to not only reshape 319 
therapeutic development but fundamentally accelerate the scientific process to drive a greater understanding of the 320 
natural world. 321

6 Methods 473 
6.1 Robin Implementation 474 
Robin is implemented as a Jupyter  notebook using the Aviary framework to instantiate and call agents [33]. Robin 475 
utilizes the OpenAI o4 -mini model to synthesize literature and generate hypotheses, and the Anthropic Claude 3.7 476 
Sonnet model as the judge for pairwise comparisons to rank hypotheses. Prompts used for agents and LLMs called in 477 
Robin are shown in Supplementary Figures 2-11. 478 
Notably, while the experiments in this paper were conducted using an agentic implementation of Robin, we observed 479 
that Robin almost always called tools in the same order, leading to a deterministic workflow. Therefore, we translated 480 
Robin into a streamlined Jupyter notebook to improve stability and ease of use. 481 
The ranking of hypotheses was calculated via a series of pairwise comparisons adjudicated by the LLM judge. When 482 
ranking 25 or fewer hypotheses, all possible pairs were compared in a round robin tournament. When considering more 483 
than 25 hypotheses, 300 pairwise comparisons were randomly selected to achieve a comprehensive assessment within 484 
reasonable computational and time constraints. The outcomes from these comparisons were used to estimate strength 485 
parameters via the Bradley -Terry-Luce (BTL) model, which  subsequently informed the relative ranking of each 486 
hypothesis [54]. While there is no clear consensus on the optimal strategy for LLM -judge based rankings, we opted 487 
for pairwise comparisons rather than pointwise. Some literature [55, 56] shows pairwise comparisons to be more robust 488 
than pointwise grading, as pointwise grading may be unable to distinguish nuanced differences between similar pairs. 489 
Position bias in the pairwise comparisons is addressed by utilizing a highly distributed tournament paired with the BTL 490 
model, rather than relying on simple win/loss tallies, which are highly susceptible to positional bias. To generate the 491 
prompt for this LLM judge, domain experts were asked to conduct pairwise comparisons of Robin ’s therapeutic 492 
candidate hypotheses. The results of these expert evaluations were given to Google ’s Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview model 493 
to generate a prompt for Robin ’s LLM judge. This approach was designed to elicit decision -making from the LLM 494 
judge consistent with expert preferences and criteria. 495 
When comparing the LLM judge ’s preferences with experts ’, the LLM judge demonstrated high concordance with 496 
expert preferences, with an average of 7.25 of its top 10 hypotheses matching those in the experts ’ top 10 497 
(Supplementary Figure 20A). This concordance is more than double that expected from random selection. Furthermore, 498 
the LLM judge exhibited higher intra -rater consistency than human experts (Supplementary Figure 20B). When 499 
presented with identical pairwise comparisons, the LLM judge selected th e same hypothesis in 88% of comparisons, 500 
as compared with human experts who selected the same hypothesis 61% of the time. 501 
6.2 Finch Implementation 502 
Finch is an autonomous, Jupyter -native data analysis agent designed using the Aviary framework [33]. Finch 503 
systematically processes bioinformatics workflows, such as RNA sequencing differential expression analysis or flow 504 
cytometry, based on a provided dataset and research question. 505 
Rather than retrieving static templates from a codebase, the agent operates as a generative reasoning engine that writes 506 
original, executable Python/R code line-by-line in real time, ensuring the analytical logic is dynamically adapted to the 507 
data distribution rather than constrained by hard -coded subroutines. The agent operates within a s tructured execution 508 
environment provided by the Aviary framework; an extensible gymnasium tailored for language agent evaluation and 509 
iterative problem-solving [33]. Aviary was selected specifically for its controlled environment, supporting reproducible 510 
evaluations through standardized software environments, consistent tool access, and structured multi-step reasoning. 511 
Finch leverages an agentic prompting strategy based on the ReAct approach, balancing logical reasoning and practical 512 
execution effectively [5 7]. Each Finch trajectory unfolds within a pre -built Docker container (BixBench -env:v1.0), 513 
containing extensive bioinformatics -oriented Python, R, and Bash libraries. This standardized container ensures 514 
reproducibility and isolates evaluation strictly to F inch’s analytical capability rather than software installation or 515 
dependency management. That being said, Finch is capable of installing new dependencies when needed and does so 516 
competently. 517

6.2.2 Finch evaluation 524 
We evaluated Finch and its base model (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) without agent capabilities on 170 q uestions relevant to 525 
drug discovery from the BixBench bioinformatics benchmark [31]. After excluding low -quality categories and 526 
categories not relevant to human drug discovery, 170 questions across 38 data capsules were retained and classified 527 
into two cat egories: Bioinformatics (n=131  questions), comprising RNA -seq, genomics, epigenomics, functional 528 
enrichment, and sequence analysis tasks; and Biostatistics (n=39  questions), comprising image analysis tasks and 529 
general statistical analysis tasks. Each quest ion was assigned to its first listed category in the BixBench category 530 
mapping to avoid double-counting across multi-label assignments. Both Finch and the base LLM were run in triplicate. 531 
Mean accuracy was calculated per category per replicate, and error bars represent the standard error of the mean across 532 
replicates. For failure mode analysis, each non-correct Finch response was classified as Wrong Answer (agent produced 533 
a specific incorrect value), Distractor Match (response matched a provided distractor) , Formatting (correct value in 534 
wrong format), Unsure (agent declined to answer), or Empty/Failed (no response produced), aggregated across all three 535 
replicas. 536 
Each BixBench question consists of a natural language prompt which references data files packaged in a capsule (ZIP 537 
archive), an expected answer, three distractor options, and a designated evaluation method. Finch receives both the 538 
question and access to the capsule data and may write and execute code (Python or R) over up to 10 steps to produce 539 
an answer. The base LLM receives only the question text with no data access or code execution capability. Responses 540 
were evaluated using one of three methods specified per question: exact match (string or numeric equality), range match 541 
(numeric answer within a specified interval), or LLM judge (semantic equivalence assessed by a separate model for 542 
free-text answers). A response was scored as correct only if it passed its designated evaluation; all other outcomes 543 
including valid analyses that produced wrong values, formatting mismatches, and refusals were scored as incorrect. 544 
6.3 Robin Validation 545 
6.3.1 Crow and Falcon Ablation experiments 546 
Calls to the FutureHouse platform agents Crow and Falcon were replaced with calls to o4 -mini via the OpenAI API 547 
using the same system and user prompt. For the human reference check, a scientist blinded to the source was provided 548 
with 10 randomly chosen proposals from the wild type and ablated versions of Robin. Each time a citation or reference 549 
was encountered in a proposal, the scientist attempted to find the original article online. If the original article could not 550 
confidently be identified from the citation or reference (including the available context from the proposal) the reference 551 
was labeled as hallucinated. Otherwise, the reference was labeled legitimate. 552 
For the LLM judge comparison, 50 wild type and 50 ablated proposals were submitted to a head -to-head tournament 553 
adjudicated by Claude Sonnet 3.7 and the outcomes from these comparisons were used to generate a proposal ranking 554 
via the BTL model (as implemented in the Robin internal ranking system). Statistical significance of differences in 555 
mean ranks between groups was assessed using a permutation test. The observed test statistic was the difference in 556 
mean ranks between the ablated and wild-type groups (mean rank of ablated minus mean rank of wild-type). Under the 557 
null hypothesis of no difference between groups, the null distribution was generated by randomly permuting group 558 
labels 10,000 times and recalculating the test statistic for each permutation. The p-value was calculated as the proportion 559 
of permuted differences greater than or equal to the observed difference (one-tailed test). All analyses were performed 560 
using Python 3.12.10 with NumPy and pandas. 561

6.6 Data Analysis 678 
6.6.1 Flow Cytometry 679 
Finch, the data analysis agent, performed end -to-end analysis of our flow cytometry data. Debris and cell aggregates 680 
were excluded from the analysis based on forward and side scatter parameters. In general , Finch clusters the forward 681 
scatter versus side scatter plot by using the flowMeans package for the R programming language to apply a k -means 682 
clustering before choosing the cluster most likely to contain the main cell population. For subsequent gates, Fin ch 683 
generated scatter plots and then used the plots to define gates. Single cells were gated based on pulse width and area 684 
parameters. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the pHrodo signal was quantified for each well to assess the 685 
extent of phagocytosis. 686 
Flow cytometry data was also analyzed by a human scientist (Supplementary Figures 13, 15 and 16). The human flow 687 
cytometry analysis was like Finch’s except that the human analysis had the additional step of using the no-bead control 688 
to remove background signal in the Alexa 647 channel, which proved minimal. The human analysis started by 689 
identifying the main cell population in the forward versu s side scatter plot. Next, singlets were identified. Dead cells 690 
were then removed using the DAPI channel before finally removing Alexa 647 background signal using the no bead 691 
control. 692

6.6.3 Read Alignment and Processing 696 
Raw paired-end RNA-Seq reads (2 x 150 bp) for twelve samples (including Y27632-treated, vehicle control, and bead-697 
treated conditions) were processed using a custom Bash pipeline. Sequencing files (*_R1_001.fastq.gz and 698 
*_R2_001.fastq.gz) were retrieved from the FASTQ directory and aligned individually to the GRCh38 human 699 
reference genome (HISAT2 index built from GENCODE v44) using HISAT2 with the –dta flag to retain splice junction 700 
information for downstream transcriptome assembly. Alignments were performed with eight CPU threads ( -p 8) and 701 
output in SAM format. Each SAM file was converted to BAM with samtools view -bS, then sorted (samtools sort) and 702 
indexed (samtools index) to produce compressed, coordinate -sorted BAMs. Intermediate SAM and unsorted BAM 703 
files were removed to conserve disk space. This procedure ensured that all alignments were unifor mly processed and 704 
ready for quantitative analysis. 705

6.6.4 Gene-Level Quantification 707 
Gene counts were obtained with the featureCounts tool from the Subread package, using the GENCODE v44 GTF 708 
annotation file. Paired -end mode ( -p) was specified to correctly handle fragment counting, and eight threads ( -T 8) 709 
were used to accelerate processing. All the indexed, sorted BAM files were supplied simultaneously, and read 710 
assignment to genes was performed according to the provided exon features. The resulting raw gene-level count matrix 711 
was written to gene_counts.txt, serving as the input for subsequent normalization and differential expression analyses. 712 
This workflow provides a reproducible framework for high -throughput RNA-Seq data processing from raw reads 713 
through gene-level quantification. 714

6.6.5 Differential Gene Expression Analysis 715 
Raw gene counts were imported into R (v4.2.0) and filtered to remove non -count columns, yielding a matrix of 6 716

For this experiment, which was designed to validate the ABCA1 finding from the ARPE-19 RNA-seq experiment, total 724

Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina platform to a depth of approximatel y 10 million deduplicated reads (20 732 
million raw reads) per sample. Raw reads were processed by clustering UMI sequences to remove PCR duplicates, and 733 
gene counts were generated from the resulting deduplicated, aligned reads. 734

Methodology
Sample preparation For flow cytometry, samples were prepared as follows: 
 
1. Drug Treatment: ARPE-19 cells or RPE-SC were incubated with test compounds or a vehicle control for 60 minutes. 
2. Bead Addition: pHrodo beads or ROS were added, and the plates were incubated for 3 hours. 
3. Dissociation: Cells were treated with TrypLE Express for 10 minutes to detach them. 
4. Suspension: Cells were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer (1% BSA and 500 ng/ml DAPI or  1ug/ml Hoechst 33342 in 
PBS) before being transferred to the flow cytometer.
Instrument Attune NxT flow cytometer with autosampler
Software Either Finch (agent analysis) or FlowJo (human analysis). 
Cell population abundance After passing all gates, there was between 2000 and 6000 cells in the final population (apart from the Cytochalasin D 
negative control and 4-phenylbutyric acid test condition which showed some cytotoxicity and had <1000 cells in the final 
population).
