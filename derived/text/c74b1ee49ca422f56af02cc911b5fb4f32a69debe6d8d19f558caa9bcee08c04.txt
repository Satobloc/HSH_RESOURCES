Abstract 
Quantum illumination has been proposed as a theoretical route to detecting low -reflectivity targets, such as 
stealth aircraft, in high -background-noise environments where classical radar performance degrades. The 
protocol, as originally formulated  (Lloyd, 2008; Tan et al., 2008), uses continuous -variable entangled light 
(two-mode squeezed vacuum) and a specific phase -sensitive joint receiver to achieve a theoretical detection 
advantage over classical illumination of matched energy, even though the entanglement itself does not 
survive the round trip through a lossy, noisy channel. This study investigates whether that advantage appears 
in a simpler, discrete -variable (qubit) toy model, of the kind commonly used for pedagogical intuition, 
implemented o n PKTron and cross -checked on IBM Quantum hardware. A rigorously fair comparison was 
constructed in which an entangled Bell pair and a classically correlated (separable) mixture of matched initial 
correlation strength were each subjected to identical loss and background -noise channels, with detection 
performance assessed via the same joint correlation measurement (Z⊗Z expectation) in both cases. In PKTron 
simulation, the entangled probe showed a correlation separation between target -present and target-absent 
hypotheses of 0.0100 at zero background noise, decreasing to 0.0025 at high background noise, while the 
classically correlated probe showed a substantially larger separation, decreasing from 0.1000 to 0.0500 over 
the same range — indicating no entangleme nt advantage, and if anything, a disadvantage, in this discrete 
model. This qualitative finding was independently corroborated on IBM Quantum's ibm_kingston hardware, 
using natural qubit T1 relaxation (rather than an artificially injected noise parameter) as the loss channel: 
entangled correlation degraded to 54.5% of its initial value after 60 microseconds, compared to 77.7% 
retained by the classically correlated mixture over the same interval. These results indicate that the 
theoretical quantum illumination advantage does not straightforwardly emerge from a simple discrete -qubit 
correlation model, consistent with the broader understanding that the effect is specific to continuous -
variable Gaussian states and phase -sensitive joint detection, and consistent with quantum illumination's 
status as a theoretical concept with no demonstrated range or signal -to-noise advantage over classical radar 
to date. 
Keywords 
Quantum illumination; quantum radar; stealth detection; entanglement; discrete -variable toy model; 
amplitude damping; T1 relaxation; PKTron; IBM Quantum; Qiskit.

1. Introduction 
Detecting low -observable (stealth) aircraft is a longstanding challenge for air defense radar, since stealth 
design specifically minimizes radar cross-section and therefore returned signal strength, particularly against a 
background of thermal and clutter noise. Quantum illumination has been proposed as a theoretical means of 
improving detection performance in exactly this high -noise, low-reflectivity regime, by exploiting quantu m 
correlations between a transmitted 'signal' beam and a retained 'idler' beam [1, 2]. Critically, and as explicitly 
acknowledged in the framing of this study, quantum illumination remains a theoretical concept: it has not 
been demonstrated to provide a ra nge or signal-to-noise advantage over classical radar in practice, and the 
engineering challenges of realizing a working quantum radar remain substantial and unresolved [3]. 
The original quantum illumination protocol uses continuous -variable entangled ligh t — specifically, two -
mode squeezed vacuum states — combined with a phase-sensitive joint (optical parametric amplifier-based) 
receiver, and its proven advantage is a property of that specific combination of quantum state and 
measurement strategy, not of e ntanglement in general [2, 4]. Simplified discrete -variable (qubit) toy models 
are commonly used in the broader quantum information literature for pedagogical purposes, to build 
intuition about entanglement's behavior under loss and noise, but it is not gu aranteed, and is in fact a 
nontrivial and separate question, whether such simplified models reproduce the specific advantage of the full 
continuous-variable protocol. 
This study constructs the fairest possible discrete -qubit comparison — matching initial c orrelation strength 
and measurement strategy between an entangled and a classically correlated probe, varying only the 
presence of entanglement — and tests, in both PKTron simulation and on IBM Quantum hardware, whether a 
detection advantage for the entangled probe emerges under loss and background noise conditions modeling 
a low-reflectivity, high-clutter target scenario. 
2. Theoretical Background 
2.1 The Quantum Illumination Protocol 
In the original proposal, an entangled photon pair is generated; the sig nal photon is sent toward a suspected 
target through a lossy, noisy channel, while the idler photon is retained locally. Even though the round -trip 
loss and background noise destroy the entanglement itself (a phenomenon known as entanglement 
breaking), sufficient quantum correlation survives that a joint measurement between the returned signal and 
the retained idler can outperform an equivalent classical illumination scheme of matched transmitted energy, 
under specific conditions of low reflectivity and hig h thermal background — the exact regime relevant to 
detecting a low-radar-cross-section target against a noisy background [1, 2]. 
2.2 The Discrete-Qubit Toy Model and Its Limitations 
This study represents the signal -idler pair as a two -qubit system, using amplitude damping to model round -
trip loss (with damping rate 1 − reflectivity) and depolarizing noise to model background clutter, evaluated on 
PKTron's density -matrix simulator. Det ection performance is quantified via the change in Z ⊗Z correlation 
between target-present and target -absent hypotheses — the discrete-variable analogue of the correlation 
statistic used in the continuous -variable protocol's joint receiver. It is important to note explicitly that this

2.3 Constructing a Fair Comparison 
An earlier, less rigorous version of this comparison (documented in this study's development process for 
transparency) initially compared the entangled probe against a classical baseline with zero initial correlation, 
which trivially favored the entangled case and did not isolate entanglement as the relevant variable. This 
study instead compares the entangled Bell pair against a classically correlated (separable) mixture, (|00⟩⟨ 00| 
+ |11⟩⟨ 11|)/2, which has an identical initial Z⊗Z correlation value (+1) to the Bell pair, and is subjected to an 
identical noise channel and measured with an identical statistic. The only difference between the two probes 
is the presence or absence of entanglement, isolating the scientific question of interest. 
3. Methods 
3.1 Simulation Platform: PKTron 
Simulation was performed using PKTron' s DensityMatrixSimulator (version 9.0.6), which implements Kraus -
operator noise channels. For the entangled case, a Bell pair was prepared (Hadamard on qubit 0, CNOT from 
qubit 0 to qubit 1); for the classical baseline, density matrices for |00 ⟩  and |11 ⟩  were each propagated 
through the noise channel independently and classically averaged (0.5×ρ₀₀ + 0.5×ρ₁₁), producing the 
classically correlated mixture. In both cases, the 'signal' qubit (qubit 1) received amplitude damping at rate (1 
− reflectivity) when a target was modeled as present, or full damping (rate 1) when absent, plus depolarizing 
noise at a swept background level (0.0 to 0.5) representing clutter/thermal background. Target reflectivity 
was fixed at 0.10, representing a low-reflectivity, stealth-like target. 
3.2 Hardware Validation: IBM Quantum 
Hardware validation was performed via IBM Quantum Platform's Open Plan using Qiskit and Qiskit Runtime, 
on ibm_kingston. Rather than artificially injecting a noise parameter (which real superconducting hard ware 
has no direct mechanism to accept), this study used the qubits' own natural T1 relaxation as the physical loss 
channel: a variable delay (0, 20, and 60 microseconds) was inserted on the signal qubit before measurement, 
allowing natural amplitude dampi ng to degrade the correlation in direct physical analogy to the reflectivity 
parameter in the PKTron simulation. Three circuits were run per delay value — the entangled Bell pair, and 
the two classical basis -state preparations (|00 ⟩  and |11 ⟩ ) whose results  were classically averaged post -
measurement to reconstruct the classically correlated mixture's statistics — batched into a single Sampler job 
with 2,048 shots per circuit.

5. Discussion 
5.1 Interpretation of Results 
Both simulation and independent hardware validation converge on the same finding: a rigorously fair, 
discrete-qubit toy model of quantum illumination does not reproduce the theoretical detection advantage 
associated with the full continuous -variable protocol. If anything, the entangled probe showed faster 
correlation degradation than a classically correlated probe of matched initial strength, in both the idealized 
PKTron simulation and on real IBM Quantum hardwa re using physically genuine T1 relaxation as the loss 
mechanism. 
This finding should not be interpreted as evidence against the theoretical validity of quantum illumination as 
originally formulated. Rather, it is consistent with the specific and well -established understanding in the 
quantum metrology literature that the protocol's advantage arises from the particular combination of 
continuous-variable Gaussian entangled states (two -mode squeezed vacuum) and a phase -sensitive joint 
receiver (optical parametric amplification or equivalent), and is not a generic property of entanglement that 
automatically transfers to arbitrary discrete-variable analogues or arbitrary correlation measurements [2, 4]. 
This study's result illustrates precisely why quantum illumin ation remains a theoretical concept without a 
demonstrated practical radar advantage: reproducing its benefit requires a specific, technically demanding 
physical implementation, not merely 'using entanglement' in a generic sense. 
5.2 Scope and Limitations 
● This study does not implement or simulate the actual quantum illumination protocol (continuous-
variable two-mode squeezed vacuum states with a phase-sensitive joint receiver). It tests a simplified 
discrete-qubit analogue commonly used for pedagogical purposes, and its negative result applies to 
that analogue, not necessarily to the full continuous-variable protocol, which has established 
theoretical support in the literature under its own specific assumptions. 
● This study does not simulate real microwave or RF radar physics, real atmospheric or 
electromagnetic propagation, or any actual stealth aircraft radar cross-section model. All noise and 
loss parameters are illustrative and chosen for qualitative demonstration, not calibrated to any 
specific real radar system or target.

● The hardware validation uses natural T1 relaxation as a stand-in for target-reflectivity-dependent 
loss; this is a physically genuine amplitude-damping process but is not independently tunable per-
shot in the way the simulation's reflectivity parameter is, limiting the hardware test to a small 
number of discrete delay values rather than a continuous reflectivity sweep. 
● This study's initial development included a less rigorous comparison (entangled probe vs. a zero-
correlation classical baseline) that trivially favored the entangled case; this is documented 
transparently in Section 2.3 and the supplementary notebook, and was corrected before the results 
reported in Section 4 were obtained. 
In summary: this study provides an honest,  methodologically corrected, hardware -corroborated test of 
whether a simple discrete -qubit entanglement advantage exists for stealth -target-like detection scenarios, 
and finds no such advantage in this model — a result consistent with, and explanatory of, quantum 
illumination's continued status as an undemonstrated theoretical concept for practical radar applications. 
6. Potential Defense and Dual-Use Application Context 
Quantum radar and quantum illumination are discussed in unclassified defense and resear ch literature as a 
theoretical, long -term research direction for detecting low -observable targets, explicitly including stealth 
aircraft. Given this study's findings, this section describes the conditions under which the concept could 
become relevant, rather than near-term applications. 
6.1 Air Force — Stealth Aircraft Detection 
If a full continuous -variable quantum illumination system (entangled microwave or optical sources, phase -
sensitive joint receivers) could be engineered at practical scale and range,  it is theoretically proposed to 
improve detection of low-radar-cross-section targets against high-clutter backgrounds. This study's finding — 
that a naive discrete -qubit implementation does not reproduce this advantage — underscores that realizing 
this benefit requires the full, technically demanding continuous -variable protocol, not a simplified quantum 
circuit. 
6.2 Distinguishing Genuine Quantum Advantage from Superficial Quantum Framing 
This study's methodology — constructing a rigorously fair compariso n and reporting a negative result 
transparently — is itself relevant to defense technology evaluation: proposals invoking 'quantum radar' or 
'quantum illumination' should be assessed against the same standard of fair, matched comparison used here, 
since th is study demonstrates that superficially plausible discrete -qubit implementations can fail to 
reproduce claimed advantages, and can even underperform classical alternatives of matched resource. 
6.3 Caveat on Application Readiness 
Quantum illumination, as this study's own framing states at the outset, remains a theoretical concept with no 
demonstrated range or signal-to-noise advantage over classical radar. This study's results are consistent with, 
and help explain, that status: even in a fair and carefully constructed comparison, this study's discrete -qubit 
toy model showed no advantage. Realizing any practical quantum radar capability would require full 
continuous-variable quantum optics engineering at microwave or optical frequencies, entirely beyond the 
scope of this study, and remains an open, unresolved research question.

This study constructed a rigorously fair, discrete -qubit toy -model comparison between entangled and 
classically correlated probes for stealth -target-like detection under l oss and background noise, using PKTron 
simulation and IBM Quantum hardware validation. In simulation, the classically correlated probe showed a 
target-present/target-absent detection separation 5 to 20 times larger than the entangled probe across all 
tested background noise levels. On real IBM Quantum hardware, using natural T1 relaxation as a physically 
genuine loss channel, entangled correlation degraded to 54.5% of its initial value after 60 microseconds, 
compared to 77.7% retained by the classically cor related probe. Both results converge on the same finding: 
no entanglement advantage emerges in this simplified discrete model, consistent with the established 
understanding that quantum illumination's theoretical advantage is specific to continuous -variable Gaussian 
states and phase -sensitive joint detection, and consistent with the topic's own correct framing as a 
theoretical concept with no demonstrated practical radar advantage to date. 
8. Future Work 
● Extend the hardware validation to a continuous sweep of delay values (rather than three discrete 
points) to more precisely characterize the relative decay rates of entangled versus classically 
correlated probes on real hardware. 
● Investigate whether a continuous-variable simulation framework (Gaussian states, homodyne or 
heterodyne detection) more faithfully reproduces the theoretical quantum illumination advantage, 
as a more direct test of the original protocol than the discrete-qubit analogue used here. 
● Test alternative discrete-variable entangled resource states and joint measurement strategies 
beyond the simple Bell pair and Z⊗Z correlation used in this study, to determine whether any 
discrete-variable analogue can reproduce the continuous-variable advantage. 
● Survey the broader quantum sensing literature for other proposed 'quantum radar' schemes and 
subject each to the same rigorous, fair-comparison methodology established in this study, to build a 
clearer picture of which proposed advantages are robust and which are artifacts of unfair baseline 
comparisons.
