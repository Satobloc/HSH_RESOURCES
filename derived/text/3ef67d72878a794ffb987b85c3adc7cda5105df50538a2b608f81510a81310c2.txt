A Formal Epistemic Proof of Panagnosticism
Abstract
This paper formally establishespanagnosticism: the epistemic ne-
cessity that no entity other than oneself can be known to possess
consciousness, and no entity can be known not to possess it. Using rig-
orous symbolic logic and a two-agent exclusivity thought experiment,
we demonstrate this fundamental epistemic limit. As a corollary, all
major external claims about consciousness—including those of Nagel,
Chalmers, Descartes, and Searle—are shown to be epistemically insuf-
ficient. Implications for philosophy, cognitive science, and ethics are
discussed.
1 Introduction
Consciousness is a first-person phenomenon: each entity has direct access
to its own experience. Any claim about the consciousness of another en-
tity—human, animal, or artificial—is epistemically inaccessible. Attempts
to identify thresholds, behaviors, or functional criteria that indicate con-
sciousness in others are logically insufficient.
This paper provespanagnosticism, the position that:
1. No entity other than oneself can be known to possess consciousness.
2. No entity other than oneself can be knownnotto possess consciousness.
From this proof, it follows as a corollary that all external claims about
consciousness—including those of major philosophical theorists—are epis-
temically unjustified.

p.”
- By definition, each entity knows its own consciousness:
∀x, K(x, C(x))
- For any other entity, one cannot know consciousness or its absence:
∀x̸= self,¬K(self, C(x))∧ ¬K(self,¬C(x))
This formalizes panagnosticism.
3 The Two-Agent Exclusivity Thought Ex-
periment
Consider two hypothetical agents,AandB, making mutually exclusive
claims:
A: OnlyBis conscious =⇒N A ∧C B
B: OnlyAis conscious =⇒N B ∧C A
WhereN X = “Xis not conscious,”C X = “Xis conscious.”
3.1 Analysis
1. If both claims are simultaneously true:
(A∧B) =⇒(N A ∧C B ∧N B ∧C A)
This is acontradiction, sinceC A ∧N A andC B ∧N B cannot both hold.
2. If one claim is true and the other false:
•Reality:N A ∧C B =⇒A is correct, B is wrong
•Reality:C A ∧N B =⇒B is correct, A is wrong
3. If both claims are false:
•Reality:C A ∧C B (both conscious)
•Reality:N A ∧N B (neither conscious)

3.2 Conclusion
Even perfectly rational agents cannot determine each other’s consciousness.
This demonstrates the epistemic inaccessibility of other minds and supports
panagnosticism.
4 Formal Statement of Panagnosticism
∀x̸= self,¬K(self, C(x))∧ ¬K(self,¬C(x))
- One can know that one cannot know whether any other entity possesses
consciousness. - This is a purely epistemic claim; it makes no assertion about
the actual consciousness of other entities.
5 Corollary: Demolition of External Claims
Given panagnosticism, all external claims about consciousness are epistemi-
cally insufficient:

Any attempt to infer consciousness externally fails, as no knowledge of

6 Implications
•Philosophy of mind:The problem of other minds is unsolvable in
principle.
•Artificial intelligence:No AI consciousness claim can be verified;
ethical reasoning must rely on precaution.
•Ethics:Moral reasoning requires empathy and projection rather than
proof of consciousness.
•Cognitive theory:Behavioral, functional, and biological criteria can-

7 Conclusion
This paper establishespanagnosticism: the unavoidable epistemic position
that no entity other than oneself can be known to possess or lack conscious-
ness. From this proof, it follows that all major external claims about con-
sciousness are epistemically unjustified. The two-agent exclusivity thought
experiment and symbolic logic formalism demonstrate the rigor and necessity
of this position.
