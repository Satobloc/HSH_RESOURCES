Introduction
More than three decades ago, Robert May emphasized the great relevance of nonlinear first-order difference equations to understand fascinating features of the nonlinear dynamics observed in diverse contexts, including biological and social sciences [1]. Since then the importance of the study of difference equations, or maps, has not stopped growing not only as simple models of natural phenomena, but also as tools to analyze differential equations as well as to explore universal characteristics of chaotic dynamics [2]. But the capture of significant nonlinearity into the mathematical equations of model systems is far from an obvious task, which allows us to understand the historical persistence of some linear ballasts in many equations of current models [3], [4]. The overwhelming use of harmonic functions to model time-dependent periodic functions is one of the most extended of such ballasts [5], [6]. Clearly, this experimentally and mathematically convenient choice imposes a drastic and unnecessary restriction in the model equations of nonlinear systems, preventing thus the exploration (and exploitation) of unanticipated aspects of the underlying dynamics [7], [8]. Indeed, it has been demonstrated that the consideration of time-dependent periodic forces which are the output of generic (nonlinear) systems deals to the discovery of the impulse transmitted by the force (temporal integral over a period between two of its consecutive zeros) as a relevant quantity that integrates the conjoint effects of the force’s amplitude, period, and waveform (spectral content) [9]. The relevance of the time-dependent force’s impulse has been shown previously in quite different contexts, such as ratchet transport [10], chaotic dynamics of a pump-modulation Nd:YVO4 laser [11], topological amplification effects in scale-free networks of signaling devices [12], driven two-level systems and periodically curved waveguide arrays [13], bouncing of droplets on a vibrated soap film [14], and phase-disorder effects in coupled bistable systems [15]. And the force’s impulse could be the key in the discovery and characterization of new phenomena in systems described by nonlinear partial differential equations [16], [17], [18], [19]. It is worth mentioning that most of these works have considered Jacobian elliptic functions (JEFs) instead of harmonic functions to model periodic functions. Indeed, JEFs lie at the heart of nonlinear science and have been known for about 200 years [20]. They have been applied to a vast diversity of physics and mathematics problems, including the simple pendulum [21], quartic oscillators [22], spinning tops (i.e., rotating rigid bodies with one point fixed) [23], sound propagation and the design of organ pipes [24], water waves [25], DNA superhelicity [26], planetary orbits in general relativity [27], statistical correlations [28], as well as diverse phenomena in electrostatics and hydrodynamics [25], [29], to quote just a few examples.
